## 本文
```
庭の土がまだ昼の熱を抱えている夕暮れどき。そこへ大きなたらいをどんと据えて、ホースで水を張っていく。底に落ちた水が跳ねて光を散らす。ただそれだけの景色なのに、見ているうちに体の力がふっとゆるんだ。行水というのは、要するにこれきりのものだ。たらいに張った水をひしゃくですくい、火照った体に浴びる。今ではめっきり見かけなくなった、素朴な涼み方である。

昔はエアコンのある家ばかりではなかった。シャワーだって、どこでも当たり前というわけではない。だから暑さのこもった日は、庭先で水をかぶって汗を流した。ブリキのたらいはふちが日に焼けて、手をかけるとほんのりあたたかい。のぞきこむと傾いた光がゆらゆら揺れている。冷たい水が背中を伝って落ちる、あの一瞬がたまらなかった。単純といえば単純だ。それでもうちではあれがなかなか気持ちよくて、夕方が来るのをどこかで待っていた覚えがある。

[写真①]

■ 水の、ひんやり

たらいの水は井戸から汲んだり水道から張ったり、家によっていろいろだったと思う。日なたにしばらく置くと、上のほうだけがほんのりぬるむ。それでも汗をかききった体には、そのぬるい水でもじゅうぶん冷たく感じられたのだから不思議なものだ。手を入れてかき混ぜると、底のほうにひやりとした層が残っている。その冷たさをすくい上げる瞬間が、なんだか得をしたようでうれしかった。張った水を指でつつくと、輪がひろがってまた消える。急ぐでもなく、その水面をしばらく眺めているだけで、昼のあいだ肩にたまっていた暑さが、少しずつほどけていく気がした。

ひしゃくで頭からかぶると、最初のひとかけはうっと身がすくむ。それが二度、三度とくり返すうちに体が水になじんでいく。じりじりしていた暑さが、水といっしょにすうっと引いていく。上から降ってくるシャワーとは、また違う心地だ。自分の手ですくって、かけて、また、すくう。その手間のぶんだけ涼しさが濃くなる気がした。乱暴なようでいて、どこか丁寧な涼み方だったなと思う。かけ終えたあと、たらいに残った水面が夕日を映してしずかに揺れている。あれもよかった。

そのころのたらいは、行水だけの道具ではない。洗濯にも使ったし、野菜を冷やすのにも使った。ひとつの道具をあれこれ使いまわす。それが夏になると、体を洗うためのものに変わる。同じたらいなのに季節で役目が入れ替わるのが、子ども心にどこかおもしろかった。

■ 夕方の、庭先

行水の時間になると、庭先はにわかににぎやかになる。水をかけあう声、ふちを叩く音、笑い声。あちこちの家からも似たような気配が伝わってきて、夏の夕方はそういう音でできていたのかもしれない。ぬれた土のにおい。まだ残る昼の熱。遠くの夕焼け。ぜんぶがまざりあって、一日が静かに畳まれていく。日が落ちるまでのわずかなあいだだけ、その庭先が家じゅうでいちばん涼しい場所になった。

浴び終えると手ぬぐいで体をさっと拭き、ひと息つく。まだ濡れた肌を夕方の風が通ると、これがまた気持ちいい。すっかり涼しくなった体で、台所から流れてくる夕ごはんの支度のにおいを待つ。使い終えたたらいの水は庭木の根元にまいて、最後まで無駄にしなかった。行水のあとのあのさっぱりした感じは、暑い一日のちょっとしたごほうびだった気がする。まだ濡れた足の裏に、縁側の板がいやにひんやり伝わってきたのも覚えている。その冷たさに、夏の一日がようやく終わるのだと、子どもながらにどこかほっとしていた。

[写真②]

■ 今は、見なくなった

エアコンがどの部屋にも行き渡り、シャワーもすっかり当たり前になって、行水はいつのまにか見かけなくなった。うちでももう何年、たらいを出していないだろう。物置のどこかにまだしまってある気もするが、いざとなると引っぱり出す気にはなれない。水を張って、汲んで、浴びて、片づけて。思えばずいぶん手のかかる涼み方だ。スイッチひとつで涼しくなる今からすると、よくもまああんな面倒を、と自分でも笑ってしまう。便利になったのはありがたい。ただ、あの庭先で水を浴びた涼み方を、ときどき妙になつかしく思い出す。

たらいと、ひしゃくと、汲んだ水。ほんのそれだけの道具で、あの暑さと渡り合っていた。冷房の効いた部屋から振り返ると、汗をかいて、水を浴びて、また汗をかいたあの夏も、なんだか人間くさくて悪くなかったなと思う。涼しさを買うのではなく、ひしゃく一杯ぶんずつ手ですくっていたころの話だ。またいつか、あのたらいを庭に据える夏があってもいいのかもしれない。

【English】Gyozui — bathing in water from a tub.

Dusk, when the garden soil still holds the heat of noon. You set a big tub down with a thud and fill it from the hose; the water hits the bottom, leaps up, and scatters the light. Nothing more than that as a sight — and yet, watching it, the tension in my body loosened all at once. Gyozui really comes down to just this: you scoop the water in the tub with a dipper and pour it over a heated body. A plain way of cooling off you hardly see anymore.

In the old days not every house had air conditioning, and a shower was no given either. So on days the heat had built up, you rinsed the sweat away out in the yard. The tin tub, its rim sun-worn, was faintly warm to the touch; peer inside and the slanting light wavers on the water. That single moment of cold running down your back was hard to beat. Simple enough — but at our house it felt so good that I half remember waiting for the evening to come.

The water was drawn from a well or run from the tap, different from house to house. Left in the sun a while, only the top goes mildly tepid; even so, to a body wrung out with sweat, even that mild water felt plenty cold — a strange thing. Stir it with a hand and a cold layer lingers underneath, and scooping that coldness up felt like a small stroke of luck. Poke the surface with a finger and a ring spreads, then fades; just watching that water, in no hurry at all, the day's heat pooled in my shoulders seemed to come undone bit by bit.

Pour it over your head with the dipper and the first scoop makes you flinch, ugh; by the second and third the body takes to the water, and the stewing heat draws away with it. Unlike a shower falling from above: you scoop with your own hand, pour, and scoop again — and for all that trouble the coolness somehow deepens. Rough, and yet somehow careful. I liked, too, the look of the water left in the tub afterward, its surface holding the sunset and swaying quietly.

Back then the tub was not only for gyozui. It washed laundry and chilled vegetables — one tool put to many uses. Come summer it turned into something to wash the body with. The same tub, its role changing with the season; to a child, that was quietly interesting.

When gyozui time came the yard grew suddenly lively — voices, the tub's rim being tapped, laughter. From the other houses a similar air drifted over, as if a summer evening were made of such sounds. The smell of wet earth. The heat of the day still lingering. A far-off sunset. All of it mixing as the day was quietly folded up. For that brief while before dark, the yard alone became the coolest place in the whole house.

Done, you towel off and take a breath; the evening wind over still-damp skin feels good all over again. In a well-cooled body you wait for the smell of supper drifting from the kitchen. The leftover water we poured at the roots of the garden trees, wasting none of it. That freshness after gyozui was a small reward for a hot day. I remember, too, how the veranda boards felt oddly cold against still-wet feet — and how, at that cold, I somehow felt, even as a child, that the long summer day was finally over.

Once AC reached every room and a shower became ordinary, gyozui quietly slipped out of sight. We ourselves have not brought the tub out in years. It may still be tucked somewhere in the shed, but when it comes to it I never feel like dragging it out. Fill it, draw the water, pour, put it away — a rather laborious way to cool off, now that I think of it. From a today where a flick of a switch brings the cool, you have to laugh at all that fuss. Convenient, and I am grateful. Still, that way of pouring water in the yard I find myself, now and then, oddly missing.

A tub, a dipper, drawn water — with only those we held our own against the heat. Looking back from a cooled room, that summer of sweating, pouring, and sweating again seems, somehow, humanly good. Not buying the cool, but scooping it up by hand, one dipperful at a time. Maybe someday there will be a summer where I set that tub out in the yard again. (Whether a household does gyozui varies greatly by region and generation, and it is rare now; this is our one example and memory. When handling water, take great care — never take your eyes off small children in particular.)

## 英語要約
```
You set a big tub in the yard and fill it brimming; a hot summer evening, you put the child in and pour water with a dipper, zabaa — "so cold!" a shriek half delight — that's gyozui, an old way of cooling off you don't see now. In the old days there was no AC, not every house a shower, so you filled a tub in the yard and poured it over yourself, rinsing sweat, cooling a heated body, a simple primitive way that a Takaoka resident remembers feeling quite good. The water (well or tap), left in the sun, goes tepid, but to a sweated body even that felt cool; the first scoop makes you flinch, but by the third the body adjusts and the stewing heat flows away with the water, a plain pleasure unlike a shower. A child's gyozui is usually done in the yard, minding no neighbors' eyes, only that the water is cold and good, shrieking and splashing — a peaceful, idyllic scene; done, you towel off fresh and wait for supper, that freshness a reward of the evening. Once AC and showers were ordinary, gyozui disappeared, not done in years — grown convenient, but that plain way is missed; a tub, a dipper, well-water held their own against the heat, and from a cool room now, that summer of sweating and pouring seems humanly good. (Varies by region and generation, now rare; mind water accidents — never take your eyes off small children.)
