## 本文
```
夏休みの朝、まだ涼しいうちに、近所の子どもたちが広場や公園に集まってくる。眠い目をこすりながら、ぞろぞろと。そして、ラジオから流れるあの音楽に合わせて、みんなで体操をする。ラジオ体操だ。

朝の六時台、決まった時間に、決まった場所へ。うちの子どものころは、それが夏休みの朝の、当たり前の風景だった。あの、誰もが一度は聞いたことのある音楽が流れ始めると、まだ寝ぼけていた体が、ゆっくり動き出す。腕を大きく回して、体をそらして、飛び跳ねて。特に張り切るわけでもなく、でも、なんとなく最後までやる。

[写真①]

■ カードに、ハンコをもらう楽しみ

ラジオ体操には、たいてい「出席カード」がついてくる。首から下げた小さなカードに、体操が終わると、係の人からハンコやスタンプを押してもらう。マス目が少しずつ埋まっていくのが、子ども心にうれしかった。毎日通って、全部のマスが埋まると、なんだか誇らしい。皆勤で、最後にちょっとしたごほうびがもらえることもあった。

正直、体操そのものより、あのハンコが目当てだった子も多いと思う。私もそのひとりだ。でも、そのハンコほしさに、夏じゅう早起きを続けた——今思えば、あれはあれで、りっぱな習慣だったのかもしれない。

■ 世代を超えて、朝の広場に

面白いのは、ラジオ体操に集まるのが、子どもだけではないことだ。近所の大人や、お年寄りも、朝の運動がわりに混じっている。ふだんは接点のない世代が、朝の広場で、同じ音楽に合わせて、同じ動きをする。終わったら「おはよう」と声をかけ合って、それぞれの一日が始まる。

体操そのものは、ほんの十分ほど。でも、あの十分のあいだに、町の朝が、ゆるやかに立ち上がっていく感じがする。ラジオ体操は、運動というより、夏の朝の、小さな集まりなのだと思う。

[写真②]

■ 減っても、覚えている夏の音

近ごろは、ラジオ体操をやらない地域も増えたと聞く。生活のかたちが変わって、朝みんなで集まること自体が、難しくなってきたのかもしれない。うちのあたりでも、昔ほどではなくなった。

それでも、あの音楽を聞くと、今でも体が勝手に動きそうになる。眠い目をこすって広場へ向かった、あの夏の朝の空気を、ふっと思い出す。日本の夏の、ささやかだけれど、たしかにあった風景。海外から来た人にも、朝の広場でみんなが同じ体操をする、この不思議でのどかな習慣を、どこかで知ってもらえたらうれしい。

【English】On a summer-holiday morning, while it's still cool, the neighborhood children gather at a square or park, shuffling along and rubbing sleepy eyes. Then, to that music coming from the radio, everyone does exercises together. This is rajio taiso — "radio calisthenics."

Around six in the morning, at a set time, to a set place. When I was a child that was simply the scene of a summer-holiday morning. As that music — the one almost everyone has heard at least once — begins, a still-half-asleep body slowly starts to move: swing the arms wide, bend back, hop in place. Nobody tries especially hard, but somehow you see it through to the end. Rajio taiso usually comes with an "attendance card": a small card hung around your neck, stamped by a helper each time the exercises finish. Watching the little squares fill in, one by one, was a childhood joy; go every day and fill them all, and you felt somehow proud, sometimes with a small reward at the end for perfect attendance. Honestly, plenty of kids were there for the stamp more than the exercise — I was one of them. But wanting that stamp kept me getting up early all summer, and looking back, that was a fine habit in its own right. What's interesting is that it isn't only children who gather: neighborhood adults and older folk join in as a bit of morning exercise. Generations that don't usually cross paths do the same movements to the same music in the morning square, then trade "good mornings" as each day begins. The exercise itself is only about ten minutes, but in those ten minutes the town's morning seems to rise gently to its feet — rajio taiso is less exercise than a small summer-morning gathering. These days, I hear, more areas no longer do it; as ways of living change, just gathering in the morning has grown harder, and around us too it's less than it was. Even so, hearing that music, my body still nearly moves on its own, and the air of those summer mornings — rubbing sleepy eyes on the way to the square — comes suddenly back. A small but real scene of the Japanese summer. I'd be glad if visitors could somewhere know this odd, peaceful custom of everyone doing the same exercises in a morning square. (This is my own, older memory; whether it's done, and the cards and rewards, vary greatly by area, era and generation, and fewer places do it now; this doesn't generalize any one area's custom.)

## 英語要約
```
On a summer-holiday morning, while it's still cool, neighborhood children shuffle to a square or park, rubbing sleepy eyes, and do exercises together to music from the radio — rajio taiso, "radio calisthenics." Around six a.m., set time, set place; when I was a child that was simply the morning scene. As that music almost everyone knows begins, a half-asleep body starts to move — arms wide, bend back, hop — nobody trying hard, but seeing it through. It usually comes with an attendance card hung round your neck, stamped each time; watching the squares fill was a childhood joy, and perfect attendance sometimes earned a small reward. Many kids came for the stamp more than the exercise — I was one — but wanting it kept me rising early all summer, a fine habit in its own right. And it isn't only children: adults and older folk join as morning exercise, generations who don't usually meet doing the same moves to the same music, then trading "good mornings." Only ten minutes, but in them the town's morning rises gently — less exercise than a small gathering. Fewer areas do it now as life changes, and around us too it's faded; yet that music still nearly moves my body, bringing back those mornings. A small but real scene of the Japanese summer. (My own older memory; customs vary by area, era and generation, and fewer do it now.)
