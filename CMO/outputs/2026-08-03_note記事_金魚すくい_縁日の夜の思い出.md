## 本文
```
夏祭りの夜、屋台の並ぶ通りを歩いていると、必ず足が止まる場所がある。金魚すくいだ。

浅い水槽に、赤や白の金魚が、すいすいと泳いでいる。その前にしゃがんで、「ポイ」という、紙が張られた小さな枠を持つ。この紙で、金魚をすくうのだ。けれど、この紙が、とにかく破れやすい。ちょっと力を入れると、すぐに、ぷつっと破れてしまう。

[写真①]

■ 破れるとわかっていても、真剣になる

金魚すくいの何が楽しいかというと、あの「もどかしさ」だと思う。金魚は、すばしっこい。狙いを定めて、そっと紙を近づけても、ひらりと逃げられる。あわてると、紙が破れる。だから、息をつめて、そうっと、そうっと、すくいあげる。子どもも大人も、あの小さな水槽の前では、みんな真剣な顔になる。

たいていは、うまくいかない。何匹もすくえる人は、なかなかいない。でも、それでいいのだ。すくえたら、うれしい。すくえなくても、「くやしい、もう一回!」と言いたくなる。あの、うまくいきそうでいかない感じが、縁日の夜を、少し特別にしてくれる。

■ 持ち帰った金魚と、そのあと

うまくすくえても、すくえなくても、たいていは、店の人が何匹か袋に入れてくれる。水の入ったビニール袋の中で、赤い金魚がゆらゆら泳いでいる。それを大事に持って、祭りの帰り道を歩く。家に着いたら、鉢や水槽に移して、飼うことになる。

ただ、正直に書いておくと、縁日で持ち帰った金魚が、長く元気でいてくれるとは限らない。環境が変わってすぐに弱ってしまうことも、少なくない。だからこそ、持ち帰ると決めたら、最後まできちんと世話をする——それが、金魚をすくった者の、小さな責任だと思う。遊びで終わらせず、命を預かる。そこまで含めて、金魚すくいなのかもしれない。

[写真②]

■ 夜店の灯りと、あの夏の空気

金魚すくいそのものより、あの場面の空気を、私はよく覚えている。夜店の裸電球の灯り。水のはねる音。まわりの子どもたちの歓声。祭りのにおい。しゃがんだ足がしびれてくるのも構わず、金魚を追いかけた、あの時間。

大人になった今、金魚すくいをする機会は、めっきり減った。でも、夏祭りで金魚すくいの屋台を見かけると、つい足が止まって、しゃがみたくなる。海外から来た人にも、この縁日の小さな遊びを、一度体験してみてほしい。紙はすぐ破れるし、たぶんうまくすくえない。でも、そのもどかしさごと、日本の夏の夜を、楽しんでもらえたらうれしい。

【English】On the night of a summer festival, walking the street lined with stalls, there's always a spot where my feet stop: the goldfish scooping — kingyo-sukui.

In a shallow tank, red and white goldfish swim briskly about. You crouch before it holding a "poi," a small frame with paper stretched across it, and with this paper you scoop the goldfish. But the paper tears terribly easily — put in a little force and it goes, with a soft pop, right through. What makes kingyo-sukui fun is, I think, that very frustration. The goldfish are quick; take aim, bring the paper gently near, and they slip away with a flick; hurry, and the paper tears. So you hold your breath and lift, ever so gently — child and adult alike wear a serious face before that little tank. Usually it doesn't go well; few people scoop many. But that's fine: scoop one and you're glad; scoop none and you want to say "so close — one more time!" That almost-but-not-quite feeling makes the festival night a little special. Whether you scoop well or not, the stall keeper usually bags a few for you; in a water-filled plastic bag, red goldfish sway as you carry them carefully home along the festival road, to move into a bowl or tank and keep. But let me be honest: a goldfish brought home from a festival won't always stay healthy long — often the change of environment weakens it quickly. That's exactly why, if you decide to take one home, you care for it properly to the end — that, I think, is the small responsibility of one who scooped a goldfish. Not ending it as mere play, but taking a life into your keeping; maybe that, too, is part of kingyo-sukui. More than the scooping itself, I remember the air of that scene: the bare-bulb light of the night stall, the splash of water, the cheers of the children around, the smell of the festival, and the time spent chasing goldfish, never minding my crouched legs going numb. As an adult I rarely do it now, but seeing a goldfish-scooping stall at a summer festival, my feet stop and I want to crouch down. I'd love visitors from abroad to try this small festival game once: the paper tears at once, and you probably won't scoop well — but I hope you enjoy a Japanese summer night, frustration and all. (This is my own, older memory; tools, rules and whether you take fish home vary by area, stall and era; a goldfish brought home must be cared for responsibly to the end, never treating a life carelessly; no specific festival, venue or shop is named.)

## 英語要約
```
On a summer festival night, walking the stall-lined street, my feet always stop at the goldfish scooping — kingyo-sukui. In a shallow tank red and white goldfish dart about; you crouch with a "poi," a small paper-covered frame, and scoop with the paper — which tears terribly easily, going through with a soft pop at the least force. The fun is that frustration: the fish are quick, slipping away with a flick, and hurrying tears the paper, so you hold your breath and lift ever so gently — child and adult both serious before the little tank. Usually it fails; few scoop many, but that's fine — scoop one and you're glad, scoop none and you want "one more time!" That almost-but-not-quite makes the night special. The keeper usually bags a few anyway; red goldfish sway in a water-filled bag as you carry them home to a bowl. Honestly, a festival goldfish won't always stay healthy long — the change often weakens it — which is why, if you take one home, you care for it to the end: the small responsibility of one who scooped a life, part of kingyo-sukui too. More than the scooping I remember the scene: bare-bulb light, splashing water, children's cheers, the festival smell, numb crouched legs. Rarely do it now, but a stall stops my feet. Try it once: the paper tears, you won't scoop well, but enjoy a Japanese summer night, frustration and all. (My own older memory; customs vary; care for the fish responsibly.)
