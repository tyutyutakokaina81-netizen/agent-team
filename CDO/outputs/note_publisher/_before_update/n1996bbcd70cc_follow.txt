【English】Onigiri — shaped with the salt worked in.

You wet your palm a little, a sprinkle of salt, and set the just-cooked hot rice on it; going "hot, hot," you roll it in your hands and shape it into a triangle — pressed not too hard, but not so it falls apart, just the right force — and finally wrap it with a crisp sheet of nori. There: an onigiri. It's perhaps the most familiar Japanese food, just rice shaped by hand, and yet somehow it has a goodness utterly unlike rice served in a bowl — that slight salt and the fluffy rice pressed by hand; onigiri has a taste only onigiri can give.

An onigiri eaten in summer is especially good with a little salt worked in. In a summer that makes you sweat a lot, the body wants salt, so a shio-musubi (salt rice ball) that's almost a touch salty tastes wildly good in summer. It needs no filling: salt, rice and nori are feast enough. Of course a filling is good too — umeboshi (pickled plum) is a summer standard, its sourness clean and appetite-whetting; salmon, kombu, okaka — none go wrong. But on a hot day, going for a filling-less shio-musubi and biting straight in — that clean, decisive goodness is fine too. The good of onigiri is that you can carry it: to the sea, the mountains, the river, a summer outing usually comes with onigiri, and opening the wrap to eat one outdoors — strangely, eaten outside it tastes twenty percent better than at home.

Sweated and hungry, an onigiri with the salt worked in, eaten then, is something special; biting into a rice ball under a blue sky makes a summer outing a little special all by itself — onigiri is the most dependable travel food of summer. One thing to watch, though: in the heat food spoils easily, and onigiri is no exception, so shape it with clean hands, avoiding sticky bare-handed contact where you can, using plastic wrap; and once made, eat it soon, and avoid leaving it long in a hot place — a cool pack alongside, a cool spot to keep it. It'd all be for nothing to upset your stomach with a lovely onigiri. Tasty and safe: that's the iron rule of a summer onigiri. (Fillings, shaping and salt vary by taste and view; this is our one example. Food spoils easily in summer — mind hand hygiene, cooling and eating it soon, and take care with food safety.)
