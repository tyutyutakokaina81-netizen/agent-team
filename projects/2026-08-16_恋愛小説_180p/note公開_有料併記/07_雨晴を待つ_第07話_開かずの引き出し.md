　「遥、戻ってこない?」葉子は言った。「今度は、誰かに使われるんじゃなくて、自分たちのやりたいことを、やろうよ。『思ってたのと違う』なんて言われない、いい仕事を。……遥の才能、あんな会社で、すり減らすのは、もったいなかった。私は、ずっと、そう思ってた」
　遥は、すぐには、答えられなかった。

　遥は、自分の心が、揺れているのを感じた。

【English (opening)】

CHAPTER SEVEN — The Locked Drawer

The call came on a humid night at the end of August.
It was from Yoko. A colleague from Haruka's Tokyo days. No — more than a colleague; in seven years, the one person Haruka had been able to open her heart to. They had been in the same department, worked the same late nights, worn down the same way. Even after Haruka left the company, she and Yoko had kept in touch now and then.
"Haruka, how are you?" Yoko's voice was familiar. "How's life out there?"
"Good," Haruka said. "Not bad. The sea's beautiful."
"The sea, huh," Yoko said, a little envious. "Nice. Here it's the same as ever. Buildings, trains, deadlines."
They talked of this and that. Then Yoko changed her tone, a little, and said, "Actually — there's something I wanted to talk to you about."
Yoko was starting a small design office of her own, she said. She was gathering people. Designers she could trust, who were good. And the first to come to mind had been Haruka.
"Won't you come back?" Yoko said. "This time, instead of being used by someone, let's do what we want to do. Good work — nobody telling us 'not quite what we had in mind.' Your talent, Haruka — wearing it down at a place like that was a waste. I always thought so."
Haruka could not answer right away.
"Sorry to spring it on you," Yoko said. "You don't have to answer now. But think about it. I want to launch by the end of the year, so — if you could let me know by autumn."
After she hung up, Haruka could not move for a while.
Outside, the sound of the waves.
She felt her own heart wavering.
Yoko's words had struck at the softest place in her. "Wearing your talent down was a waste." No one had said such a thing to Haruka. Not her manager, not the client. Haruka herself had thought her talent long since worn out. And Yoko still believed in it. More — this time, instead of being used, they would do what they wanted. That was what Haruka had dreamed of for seven years.
But—
Haruka looked at the dark sea outside the window.
Beyond that sea, there was Minato. There was Migiwa, and the smell of coffee, and Meiwa Rock, and the promise to wait for the most beautiful morning of the year.
Haruka was confused.
What did she want? She did not know.

That weekend, Haruka pushed on with the clearing.
She was trying to divert her tangled feelings by moving her body. She turned to the old chest of drawers in her grandmother's bedroom, which she had not yet touched.
She opened the drawers from the top. Clothing. Hand towels. Her grandmother's kimono, meticulously folded. All of it, like her grandmother, neatly arranged.
Only the bottom drawer would not open.
It was locked.
Haruka was surprised. That her grandmother had owned something worth locking. Her grandmother's life had seemed to hold no secrets. And yet this one drawer was locked.
Haruka searched for the key.
The key was in an unexpected place — in the back of the dried-goods drawer in the kitchen, beneath the bag of dried radish strips, a single small key was hidden. Her grandmother had hidden the key to her most precious thing in the place most her own.
With that key, Haruka opened the locked drawer.
Inside was an old wooden box.
When she opened the box, a bundle of letters came out.
Yellowed, old letters. There were dozens. All in the same hand, addressed to her grandmother — Sawako — under her maiden name. Which meant they were letters from before her grandmother married. And the sender's name was a man Haruka did not know.
Haruka opened a letter, gently.
It was a love letter.
A bundle of love letters, addressed to her grandmother when she was young. The man, it seemed, had been a fisherman. The letters were full of the sea. What fish were caught today. What color the sea was today. And, between these, a plain, direct feeling for Sawako.
The oldest letter was from when her grandmother was eighteen.
And the newest—
Haruka read its date and caught her breath.
A winter, in a certain year of the Shōwa era. The letter began like this:
"Sawako-san. The sea has turned rough, and I may not be able to see you for a while. But when spring comes, I will come for you. I promise. When the coldest winter clears, I will come to you first of all."
That letter was the last.
Haruka noticed that there was one more sheet of paper in the box.
It was an old newspaper clipping.
An article reporting a disaster at sea. In a winter sea, a fishing boat had capsized, and the fisherman aboard had died. The name of that fisherman — was the same as the name of the sender of the letters.
Haruka sank down where she stood.
Her grandmother had, before she married, loved someone. A fisherman. A man who promised, "When the coldest winter clears, I will come for you." But that man had died in the winter sea. Without keeping his promise.
Her grandmother had kept that letter, and that clipping, in a locked drawer, for more than sixty years.
Without telling a soul.
Haruka held the bundle of letters to her chest.
The tears would not stop.
She had known nothing of her grandmother. Kind, a good cook, devoted to her grandchild — that was the whole of the grandmother Haruka knew. But her grandmother had been a woman. A woman who had loved, and been loved, and had that love taken from her by a winter sea.
Haruka noticed a single photograph at the very bottom of the box.
Her grandmother, young, stood beside a young man.
The place was the Amaharashi coast.
Meiwa Rock was in the background. And beyond it, the snow-covered Tateyama Range, sharp and clear.
It was a photograph taken on a clear winter morning.
Her grandmother and the man, with the Tateyama beyond Meiwa Rock at their backs, were laughing, happily.
Haruka gazed at the photograph.
The winter Tateyama Range beyond Meiwa Rock.
The view Minato wanted to photograph. The coldest, most beautiful morning of the year.
Her grandmother had seen that view, more than sixty years ago, with the person she loved.
And this single photograph of that view she had kept in a locked drawer, all along. Even after she lost the one she loved.
Haruka gazed at the photograph.
And she thought of Minato.
Minato too was waiting for the Tateyama beyond Meiwa Rock. For a brother he had lost. For a reconciliation he never got.
Inside Haruka, something connected.
Her grandmother, and Minato. Different eras, different circumstances. And yet both of them waited, on and on, for the most beautiful morning of the year beyond Meiwa Rock, for someone they had lost.
And Haruka.
Haruka now held both of those longings in her hands.
Go back to Tokyo, or stay here.
The answer to that question was beginning, little by little, to take shape inside her.
Haruka set her grandmother's photograph, gently, beside the memorial portrait.
"Grandma," she said to it. "I've decided. I'll wait — with him — for the morning you waited for. So that this time, someone will come for us, properly."
Outside the window, the sound of the waves went on, quiet.
