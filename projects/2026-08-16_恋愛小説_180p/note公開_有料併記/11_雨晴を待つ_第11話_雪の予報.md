【English (opening)】

CHAPTER ELEVEN — The Forecast of Snow

When December came, the snow began to fall.
Toyama's snow was heavy. Different from the dry, fluttering snow that fell now and then in Tokyo. Damp snow, full of water, piled up soundlessly. In a single night, the town was buried white.
The sea, seen from Migiwa's window, changed too.
The sky filled with grey cloud. The sea, reflecting the grey, heaved dark. Meiwa Rock, capped with snow, turned white. And the Tateyama Range beyond it — vanished.
In winter, the Tateyama Range was almost never visible.
"What?" Haruka was surprised. "You can't see it in winter? In the coldest season of the year?"
"Winter's mostly cloudy," Minato said. "On the Japan Sea side there are few clear days. Cloud hides the mountains. So the morning when the whole Tateyama Range shows sharp beyond Meiwa Rock — even in winter, it almost never comes."
For the first time, Haruka understood how rare the view Minato wanted to photograph was.
The coldest, windless, clear morning of the year. Air so pure it froze, the sea gone perfectly still, the whole Tateyama Range visible beyond Meiwa Rock. It was a miracle of a morning, one that came on perhaps a handful of days in a whole winter.
That was why, in two years of shooting every morning, Minato had not yet taken it.
"Will it come this year?" Haruka asked, uneasy.
"I don't know," Minato said. "Some years it doesn't come. But there's nothing to do but wait. That's the winter of this land."
Every morning, Haruka and Minato waited.
Every morning, before dawn, they stood on the beach and looked beyond Meiwa Rock.
And every morning, the sky was clouded. Snow fell. Grey cloud hid the Tateyama Range.
A week passed.
Two weeks passed.
Not once did the Tateyama Range show.

Then one day, a letter came for Minato.
From Tokyo.
It was from the photographer who had been Minato's teacher in his student days. A famous name in the world of news photography. The man Minato most respected.
The letter said this:
He had been asked to judge a major overseas photography prize. He wished to recommend a young, promising photographer. He had remembered Minato's sea photographs — the ones Minato had shown him before he left Tokyo. Those he could not forget. If Minato had the will, he should send the photographs he was taking now. It was a chance to face the world.
Minato showed the letter to Haruka.
Reading it, her chest stirred.
This was a great chance for Minato. A road to being a photographer who faced the world. The road Minato had once dreamed of. The one he could not give up, even after his brother said, "You're running away."
"Minato," Haruka said. "This is an incredible thing, isn't it?"
"I don't know," Minato said. "I've left the world of news photography. Now I only shoot this sea."
"But," Haruka said. "The photograph you want — the most beautiful morning of the year. If you get it, you could send that too, couldn't you."
Minato looked at her.
"Haruka," he said. "Are you telling me to go back to Tokyo?"
Haruka started.
She realized she was, at that moment, urging Minato to leave.
That autumn misunderstanding. Minato, afraid of losing her, had tried to push her away. Now she understood his feeling. The fear of sending away someone you love. The fear of letting go.
But, Haruka thought.
Her grandmother had not been afraid.
Had not been afraid to lose the one she loved. Had loved, and lost, and loved again.
And Haruka remembered. Her own talent, worn down in Tokyo. The work that vanished with one line — "not quite what we had in mind." No one had believed in her talent. Only Yoko had said "a waste."
Haruka did not want Minato to feel the same.
She did not want to shut Minato's talent up in this small seaside shop. If Minato had the talent to become a photographer who faced the world, then to make him give it up for her sake was wrong.
"Minato," Haruka said. "I don't want you to go. Truly. I want you to stay in this town, always. To brew coffee in this shop, and live with me. But—"
Minato gazed at her.
"But I don't want to shut your talent away," Haruka said. "What wore me down in Tokyo was that no one believed in my talent. I believe in yours. So if you want to face the world — if you want to take that road — I'll send you off. The courage my grandmother couldn't find, I want to have."
Tears stood in her eyes.
As she spoke, her chest felt as if it would tear. She didn't want Minato to go — that was her true heart. But even crushing that true heart, Haruka wanted to believe in Minato's talent. That, she thought, was love. Not to bind the other to your side, but to send them off to the place where they can shine brightest. That.
Minato was quiet a while.
Then, quietly, he said, "Haruka. Thank you. That you believe in my talent. That you'd even send me off. How happy that makes me."
He took her hand.
"But," Minato said. "I'm not going to Tokyo."
Haruka looked at him.
"I'm grateful to my teacher," Minato said. "For remembering me. For trying to give me a chance. But what I want to photograph isn't a picture the world will praise. What I want to photograph is this sea. The Tateyama beyond Meiwa Rock. The sea my brother last saw. I don't shoot it for a prize. I shoot it for my brother. And now — I shoot it with you."
He held her hand tight.
"My place is here," Minato said. "Just as you refused Tokyo and chose this place, I choose too. This sea, this shop, and you. I don't need the world. As long as I have the morning beyond Meiwa Rock, and you — that's enough."
Tears spilled from Haruka's eyes.
This time, they were not tears of sorrow.
Minato drew her close.
Haruka buried her face in his chest.
His body was warm. It smelled of coffee. It smelled of the sea, too. In that smell, Haruka closed her eyes.
For a while, they stayed like that.
Outside, the snow fell, quiet.
That night, Minato wrote his reply to his teacher.
Words of thanks, and words of refusal.
"I will stay by this sea. The photograph I want to take is not for a prize, but to offer to a single person. If someday I take it, I will show it to you. Not for a prize, but only to be seen."
After he had posted the letter, Minato said to Haruka, "Now my hesitation is gone too. All that's left is to wait for that morning."
The next morning.
Matsuzo came bursting into Migiwa.
"Minato!" Matsuzo was excited. "Day after tomorrow. Day after tomorrow morning, it'll clear."
"What?" Minato looked up.
"The color of the sky this morning, and the smell of the wind," Matsuzo said. "Tomorrow night, the coldest air of the winter comes in. Radiative cooling — the morning after next will freeze. And the wind will drop dead still. That morning will be the coldest, and the clearest, of this whole winter. The morning you've been waiting for."
Minato and Haruka looked at each other.
The morning after next.
The coldest, most beautiful morning of the year.
At last — it was coming.
