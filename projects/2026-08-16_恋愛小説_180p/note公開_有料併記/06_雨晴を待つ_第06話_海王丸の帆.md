第六章　海王丸の帆

　夏が来た。
　富山の夏は、遥が思っていたよりも、暑かった。海があるから涼しいのだろうと勝手に思っていたが、湿った熱気が、盆地のほうから流れてきて、町を包んだ。それでも、夕方になると、海から風が入り、いくらか涼しくなった。夕涼み、という言葉を、遥は、この町で初めて、体で覚えた。
　汀の店には、風鈴が下がった。
　湊が、どこからか、古いガラスの風鈴を出してきて、軒先に吊るしたのだ。海風が吹くたびに、ちりん、と、澄んだ音が鳴った。その音は、店の静けさを、壊さなかった。むしろ、静けさを、より深くした。
　「夏になると、いつも吊るすんです」湊は言った。「祖父が、そうしてたんで」
　遥は、その風鈴の音が、好きだった。
　夏のあいだ、遥の暮らしは、少しずつ、形を持ちはじめていた。
　祖母の家の片づけは、ゆっくりと進んだ。急ぐ必要はなかった。遥は、祖母のものを、ひとつひとつ、手に取り、その来歴を想像し、それから、残すものと、手放すものを、分けていった。手放すものは、少なかった。祖母のものは、どれも、まだ使えるものばかりだった。祖母は、ものを大切にする人だった。
　そして、遥は、仕事を、始めていた。
　といっても、大した仕事ではなかった。町の小さな印刷所が、チラシやポスターのデザインをする人を探していると、松蔵が教えてくれたのだ。遥は、東京でグラフィックデザイナーをしていたことを話すと、印刷所の主人は、飛び上がって喜んだ。「そんな人が、この町におってくれるがか」と。
　遥がデザインするのは、町の祭りのポスターや、商店街のセールのチラシや、漁協のイベントの案内だった。東京でやっていた仕事に比べれば、規模も、報酬も、比べものにならなかった。けれど、遥は、その仕事が、思いのほか、好きだった。自分のつくったチラシが、町の掲示板に貼られ、それを見た人が、祭りに来る。自分のデザインが、確かに、誰かの目に触れ、誰かを動かしている。それが、遥には、うれしかった。
　東京では、遥のつくったものは、いつも、誰かの承認を待っていた。上司の、クライアントの、承認を。そして、その承認は、しばしば得られなかった。「思ってたのと違う」の一言で、三か月分の仕事が、消えた。
　ここでは、遥のつくったものは、まっすぐに、町の人の目に届いた。
　「あんたが作ったポスター、よかったよ」と、魚屋のおかみが言った。「あれ見て、うちの孫が、祭り行きたいって、うるさいがや」
　その一言が、東京での、どんな高い報酬よりも、遥の胸を温めた。

　八月のある日、湊が、遥を、海王丸パークへ誘った。
　「海王丸が、総帆展帆するんです」湊は言った。「全部の帆を、広げるんですよ。年に何回かしか、見られない。……写真を撮りに行くんですけど、よかったら、一緒に」
　海王丸。遥は、その名前は知っていた。かつて、船乗りを育てるために使われた、大きな帆船。いまは、海に浮かぶ博物館のようになって、港に係留されている。遥も、子どもの頃、一度、祖母に連れられて見たことがあった。
　その日、遥と湊は、海王丸パークへ向かった。
　港に着くと、そこに、白い帆船が、浮かんでいた。四本のマストが、青い夏空へ、まっすぐに伸びていた。まだ、帆は、たたまれていた。けれど、その骨組みだけでも、圧倒的な迫力があった。
　やがて、展帆が、始まった。
　船員たちが、マストに登り、次々と、帆を広げていった。白い帆が、一枚、また一枚と、夏の空に広がった。すべての帆が広がると、それは、二十九枚の、白い翼のようだった。海の上に浮かぶ、巨大な、白い鳥。青い海と、青い空を背景に、その白さは、まぶしいほどだった。

【English (opening)】

CHAPTER SIX — The Sails of the Kaiwo Maru

Summer came.
Toyama's summer was hotter than Haruka had imagined. She had assumed, without thinking, that with the sea it would be cool, but a damp heat flowed in from the direction of the inland basin and wrapped the town. Still, toward evening a wind came off the sea, and it grew somewhat cooler. Yūsuzumi — the evening cool — was a word Haruka learned, for the first time, with her body, in this town.
A wind bell was hung at Migiwa.
Minato had brought out an old glass one from somewhere and hung it under the eaves. Each time the sea wind blew, it rang, chirin, a clear note. That sound did not break the shop's quiet. If anything, it deepened it.
"I hang it every summer," Minato said. "My grandfather used to."
Haruka loved the sound of that wind bell.
Through the summer, Haruka's life, little by little, began to take a shape.
The clearing of her grandmother's house went slowly. There was no need to hurry. One by one, Haruka took up her grandmother's things, imagined their histories, and then divided them into what to keep and what to let go. There was little to let go. Her grandmother's things were all still usable. Her grandmother had been a person who cared for things.
And Haruka had begun to work.
Not much of a job, admittedly. A small print shop in town had been looking for someone to design flyers and posters, Matsuzo had told her. When she said she had been a graphic designer in Tokyo, the shop's owner all but leapt for joy. "To think a person like that is here in this town."
What Haruka designed were posters for the town's festivals, flyers for shopping-street sales, notices for fishery-cooperative events. Compared to the work she had done in Tokyo, the scale and the pay were beyond comparison. And yet Haruka found she liked the work more than she had expected. A flyer she had made would be posted on the town notice board, and someone who saw it would come to the festival. Her design was, certainly, reaching someone's eyes, moving someone. That made Haruka happy.
In Tokyo, what Haruka made had always been waiting on someone's approval. The manager's, the client's. And that approval was often withheld. "Not quite what we had in mind" — and three months of work vanished.
Here, what Haruka made reached the townspeople's eyes, straight.
"That poster you made was good," the fishmonger's wife said. "My grandkid saw it and won't stop pestering me to take him to the festival."
That one sentence warmed Haruka's chest more than any high pay in Tokyo ever had.

One day in August, Minato invited Haruka to Kaiwo Maru Park.
"The Kaiwo Maru is going to unfurl all her sails," Minato said. "Every one of them. You only get to see it a few times a year. I'm going to photograph it — come with me, if you like."
The Kaiwo Maru. Haruka knew the name. A great sailing ship once used to train sailors, now moored in the harbor like a museum on the water. Haruka too had seen it once as a child, taken by her grandmother.
That day, the two of them went to Kaiwo Maru Park.
When they reached the harbor, a white sailing ship floated there. Four masts rose straight into the blue summer sky. The sails were still furled. But even the bare frame of it had an overwhelming presence.
Before long, the unfurling began.
Sailors climbed the masts and spread the sails, one after another. White sails opened, one, and another, into the summer sky. When every sail was open, it was like twenty-nine white wings. A vast white bird, floating on the sea. Against the blue sea and the blue sky, that whiteness was almost blinding.
"It's beautiful," Haruka said, without thinking.
"They call her the Lady of the Sea," Minato said, raising his camera. "When you see her like this, you understand why."
Minato pressed the shutter.
Haruka stood beside him, looking at the white ship.
And then, suddenly, she looked at Minato.
The profile of a man raising his camera was serious. The single-minded concentration of peering into the viewfinder. The quiet heat of trying to capture the white sails of the Kaiwo Maru. Haruka wanted to keep looking at that profile. More than the ship, more than the sails — at that profile.
Her own feeling, she knew, was already clear.
That thing that had begun to glow deep in her chest in spring, on the night of the firefly squid. She already knew the name of that light.
I love him, Haruka thought.
I love this man.
But she could not say it aloud.
Minato and his memory of his brother. Those two years of prayer toward the sea beyond Meiwa Rock. Minato was still in the middle of that prayer. He had not yet taken the most beautiful morning. His heart might still be full with waiting for it. Was there room, there, for Haruka's feeling to enter? Haruka did not know.
That night, the Kaiwo Maru was lit up.
The white sails, lit, rose out of the dark like an apparition. On the water's surface the light was reflected, and swayed. As if two ships of light floated upon the sea.
Haruka and Minato looked at those ships of light, side by side.
"It's beautiful," Haruka said.
"Yes," Minato said.
For a while, they watched the light in silence.
"Konno-san," Minato said, suddenly. "That is—"
He faltered.
Haruka looked at him. His face was faintly lit by the light of the ships.
"Since you came to this town," Minato said, choosing his words, "my mornings have changed. Before, I only shot as if it were a duty. Like a kind of atonement to my brother. But now—"
He stopped.
Haruka waited for the rest. Her heart pounded.
"Now," Minato said quietly, "every morning, I look forward to you coming to the shop. It's a strange way to put it. I take the photographs of the sea, go back to the shop, and wait for you to come. That's my morning, now."
Haruka's chest filled.
"So do I," Haruka said. Her voice shook. "Every morning, I look forward to going to Migiwa. I wake, look at the sea, go down the hill. And you're there, brewing coffee, waiting. That morning — it feels like it's keeping me alive."
Minato looked at her.
The light of the ships lit their two faces, faintly.
Minato leaned, a little, toward her.
Haruka began to close her eyes.
And then—
"Minato-kun!"
A voice called, from far off.
The two of them started apart.
When they looked, Matsuzo was walking toward them, waving. He had come to see the Kaiwo Maru unfurl, it seemed. "Well now — you two came too, did you," Matsuzo said, without a care. "Fine thing you saw. Those sails of the Kaiwo Maru — never tire of them, however many times."
Haruka and Minato looked at each other, and laughed, just a little.
It had been so close. So close, and the two of them would have confirmed their feelings with something other than words.
But that "so close" stayed in Haruka as a sweet ache.
There's no need to hurry, she thought.
There is still time until winter comes. While they waited, together, for the coldest, most beautiful morning of the year, surely they would cross that "so close."
So Haruka believed.
But that at the end of summer a single phone call from Tokyo would come to her — this, the Haruka of that night did not yet know.
