第十章　寒ぶり起こし

　十一月の末、初めて、雷が鳴った。
　夜中だった。遥は、雷の音で、目を覚ました。冬の雷は、夏の雷とは、違った。夏の雷が、空の高いところで鳴るのに対して、冬の雷は、海のほうから、低く、腹に響くように、轟いた。稲光が、雨戸の隙間から、青白く、部屋を照らした。
　翌朝、汀へ行くと、松蔵が、いつもより、機嫌がよかった。
　「鰤起こしや」松蔵は言った。「ゆうべの雷。……あれが鳴ると、寒ぶりの季節や。海が、いよいよ、冬になる」
　「鰤起こし?」遥は訊いた。
　「冬の初めに鳴る雷を、この辺じゃ、そう呼ぶがや」湊が、コーヒーを淹れながら、説明した。「この雷が鳴ると、寒ぶりが、獲れはじめる。……昔の人は、雷が、寝てる鰤を、起こすんだと思ってたんです。だから、鰤起こし」
　遥は、その言葉の美しさに、心を打たれた。
　鰤起こし。冬の雷が、海の底で眠る鰤を、起こす。なんて、豊かな想像力だろう。この町の人たちは、自然の一つ一つに、こんなふうに、物語を、与えてきたのだ。
　「氷見の寒ぶりは、日本一やちゃ」松蔵は、胸を張った。「脂が、のっとる。あれを食わんと、冬が来た気が、せん」
　その日の帰り、遥は、漁港の直売所で、寒ぶりの、切り身を買った。
　そして、その夜、遥は、湊を、祖母の家に、招いた。
　湊が、祖母の家に来るのは、初めてだった。遥は、少し、緊張した。祖母の家は、遥にとって、いちばん、大切な場所だった。その場所に、湊を招くということは、遥にとって、自分の、いちばん柔らかいところを、見せる、ということだった。
　湊は、玄関で、少し、立ち止まった。
　「お邪魔します」湊は、静かに言った。「……佐和子さんの、家ですね」
　「はい」遥は言った。
　湊は、家のなかを、ゆっくりと、見回した。
　居間の、座卓。壁の、手書きのカレンダー。そして、遺影と、その隣の、若い頃の写真。湊は、その写真の前で、立ち止まり、深く、頭を下げた。
　「佐和子さん」湊は、写真に語りかけた。「お孫さんを、大切に、します」
　遥の胸が、熱くなった。
　その夜、遥は、寒ぶりで、料理をした。
　まず、ぶり大根。祖母の作り方を、遥は、なんとか、思い出そうとした。昆布出汁を取り、大根を、下茹でして。ぶりの、あらを、霜降りにして。醤油と、みりんと、酒で、じっくりと、煮込む。台所に、甘辛い、いい匂いが、広がった。
　そして、ぶりしゃぶ。
　薄く切った、寒ぶりの刺身を、昆布出汁の鍋に、さっと、くぐらせる。脂ののった身が、湯のなかで、ふわりと、白くなる。それを、ポン酢で、食べる。口に入れると、寒ぶりの脂が、じゅわりと、溶けた。

【English (opening)】

CHAPTER TEN — The Thunder That Wakes the Yellowtail

At the end of November, thunder rang for the first time.
It was the middle of the night. Haruka woke to the sound of it. Winter thunder was different from summer thunder. Where summer thunder cracks high in the sky, winter thunder rolled up from the direction of the sea, low, striking deep in the belly. Lightning, through the gap in the shutters, lit the room pale blue.
The next morning, at Migiwa, Matsuzo was in better spirits than usual.
"Buri-okoshi," he said. "The thunder last night. When that rings, it's kanburi season. The winter yellowtail. The sea's turning to winter, at last."
"Buri-okoshi?" Haruka asked.
"The thunder at the start of winter — around here, that's what we call it," Minato explained, brewing coffee. "When it rings, the kanburi start to be caught. The old people believed the thunder woke the yellowtail sleeping on the sea floor. So — the yellowtail-waking."
Haruka was struck by the beauty of the phrase.
Buri-okoshi. The winter thunder wakes the yellowtail asleep at the bottom of the sea. What a rich imagination. The people of this town had given each thing of nature a story like this.
"Himi's kanburi is the best in Japan," Matsuzo said, chest out. "The fat's on it. Eat that, and only then does it feel like winter's come."
On the way home that day, Haruka bought fillets of kanburi at the harbor's direct-sale stall.
And that night, she invited Minato to her grandmother's house.
It was the first time Minato had come. Haruka was a little nervous. Her grandmother's house was, to Haruka, the most precious place there was. To invite Minato there meant showing him her softest place.
Minato paused in the entrance.
"Excuse me," he said quietly. "Sawako-san's house."
"Yes," Haruka said.
Minato looked slowly around the house.
The low table in the living room. The handwritten calendar on the wall. And the memorial portrait, and the young photograph beside it. Minato stopped before the photograph, and bowed, deeply.
"Sawako-san," he said to it. "I'll take good care of your granddaughter."
Haruka's chest went warm.
That night, Haruka cooked with the kanburi.
First, buri-daikon. She tried to recall her grandmother's method. Draw the kombu stock, parboil the daikon. Blanch the yellowtail scraps. Simmer, slowly, with soy sauce, mirin, sake. A sweet-savory, good smell spread through the kitchen.
And buri-shabu. Thin slices of kanburi sashimi, swished briefly through a pot of kombu stock. The fatty flesh turned, softly, white in the broth. Eaten with ponzu. In the mouth, the fat of the kanburi melted, all at once.
"It's good," Minato said. "Really good."
"Still far from my grandmother's taste, though," Haruka said.
"No," Minato said, shaking his head. "This is your taste. Your taste, inherited from Sawako-san. It's more than good enough."
The two of them, around her grandmother's low table, ate the kanburi.
Outside, the buri-okoshi thunder rang again, far off.
To the sound of it, they sat around a warm pot. It was the happiest night of Haruka's life. Happier than any glittering night in Tokyo. This one — in this old, small house of her grandmother's, sharing a pot of kanburi with Minato.
"Yuzuki-san," Haruka said. "May I call you Minato?"
Minato looked a little surprised. Then he smiled.
"Of course," he said. "May I call you Haruka?"
"Yes," Haruka said.
"Haruka," Minato said, trying the name.
"Minato," Haruka said, trying his.
They looked at each other, and laughed.
Just calling each other by name brought them so much closer. The last thin wall between them vanished, now.
That night, Minato stayed late.
They talked of many things.
Minato spoke of his brother. Childhood memories. How, though they quarreled constantly, he had truly respected him. The guilt of never becoming a man of the sea, as his brother was.
Haruka spoke of her Tokyo days. The seven years of wearing down. The work that vanished with one line — "not quite what we had in mind." The dream of her grandmother's kitchen on the night of the fever. And the funeral she had not attended.
They told each other their most painful places.
Telling them did not make the pain vanish. But by telling, they no longer had to carry it alone. They could share it, between the two of them.
"Minato," Haruka said. "I think — my grandmother called me to this town. To bring me to you."
Minato looked at her.
"I've thought the same," he said. "Sawako-san sent you to me. To set my stopped time moving again."
Outside, the buri-okoshi thunder rang once more.
It was the thunder that wakes the yellowtail sleeping on the sea floor.
And that thunder, Haruka thought, might be the thunder that had woken Minato's stopped time, and her own frozen heart.
Winter was about to come.
The coldest, most beautiful morning of the year drew near.
The two of them were waiting for that morning, together.
There was no more fear.
There was only a quiet anticipation for that morning's coming.
When Minato was leaving, at the entrance, Haruka said, "Minato. When the most beautiful morning of the year comes—"
"Yes," Minato said.
"When you get the photograph," Haruka said, "when you go to lay it before your brother's grave — may I come too?"
Minato looked surprised.
Then his eyes filled with tears.
"Yes," he said, his voice shaking. "Come with me. I want to introduce you to my brother."
Haruka smiled.
"Yes," she said.
Minato went home.
Haruka saw off his back from the entrance. His back vanished into the dark at the bottom of the hill.
She went inside and looked at her grandmother's portrait, and the young photograph beside it.
"Grandma," she said. "Thank you. For calling me here."
Outside, the winter sea sounded.
And on its floor, the yellowtail were waking.
