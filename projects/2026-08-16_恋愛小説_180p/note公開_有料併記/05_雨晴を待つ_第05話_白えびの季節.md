第五章　白えびの季節

　五月になると、白えびの季節がやってきた。
　富山湾でしか、まとまって獲れないという、透きとおった小さなえび。海の宝石、と呼ばれることを、遥は湊から聞いた。
　「佐和子さんが、毎年、買ってたやつです」湊は言った。「孫のために、って」
　その言葉が、遥の胸に、また、小さな痛みを残した。来ない孫のために、祖母が毎年買っていた白えび。遥は、その白えびを、今年こそ、食べようと思った。祖母のためではなく、祖母と一緒に、という気持ちで。もう、祖母はいないけれど。
　その週末、遥は、祖母の家の片づけを始めた。
　帰ってきてから二か月近く、遥はずっと、この片づけを先延ばしにしていた。祖母のものに触れるのが、こわかったのだ。触れてしまえば、祖母の不在が、もっと確かなものになってしまう気がして。
　けれど、いつまでも先延ばしにはできなかった。
　遥は、まず、台所から始めた。
　祖母の台所は、狭かった。けれど、そこには、祖母の暮らしのすべてが、ぎゅっと詰まっていた。使い込まれた木のまな板。柄の取れかけた包丁。何十年も使われてきたであろう、黒く煤けた鍋。棚には、大小さまざまの器が、きちんと重ねて置かれていた。
　引き出しを開けると、乾物が、几帳面に仕分けされて入っていた。昆布。煮干し。干し椎茸。切り干し大根。祖母は、出汁の人だった。何を作るにも、まず出汁を取ることから始める人だった。
　遥は、その昆布を、一枚、手に取った。
　利尻昆布だ、と、袋に書いてあった。祖母は、いい昆布を使っていた。決して裕福ではなかったはずなのに、出汁のもとになる昆布だけは、いいものを使っていた。それが、祖母の、たったひとつの贅沢だったのかもしれない。
　遥は、その昆布で、出汁を取ってみようと思った。
　東京にいた頃、遥は、まともに料理をしなかった。作っても、コンビニで買ったものを、皿に移す程度だった。出汁なんて、取ったことがなかった。パックの顆粒だしすら、使ったことがなかったかもしれない。
　遥は、鍋に水を張り、昆布を沈めた。
　しばらく置いてから、火にかけた。弱火で、ゆっくりと。祖母が、そうしていたのを、遥はぼんやりと覚えていた。沸騰させてはいけない。沸騰する直前に、昆布を引き上げる。それが、祖母のやり方だった。
　鍋のなかの水が、少しずつ、色を変えていった。透明だった水が、うっすらと、黄金色になった。
　昆布出汁の匂いが、台所に広がった。
　その匂いを嗅いだ瞬間、遥の目から、涙が、こぼれた。
　この匂いだ。子どもの頃、夏休みに祖母の家で目を覚ますと、いつも、この匂いがしていた。この匂いは、祖母そのものだった。祖母は、もういないのに、この匂いだけは、こうして、遥の台所に、よみがえってきた。
　遥は、その出汁で、味噌汁を作った。
　具は、冷蔵庫にあった豆腐と、わかめだけだった。けれど、その味噌汁は、遥がこれまで飲んだどんな味噌汁よりも、おいしかった。それは、祖母の味だった。いや、祖母の味には、まだ遠かったかもしれない。でも、確かに、その方向にある味だった。

【English (opening)】

CHAPTER FIVE — The Season of White Shrimp

When May came, the season of shiraebi arrived.
The white shrimp — small and transparent, caught in quantity, they say, only in Toyama Bay. Jewels of the sea, they are called; Haruka learned that from Minato.
"They're the ones Sawako-san bought every year," Minato said. "For her grandchild."
The words left another small ache in Haruka's chest. The white shrimp her grandmother had bought every year for a grandchild who never came. This year, Haruka decided, she would eat them — not for her grandmother, but with the feeling of eating them together with her. Even though her grandmother was gone.
That weekend, Haruka began to clear out her grandmother's house.
For nearly two months since coming back, she had kept putting off this clearing. She had been afraid to touch her grandmother's things. Once she touched them, her grandmother's absence would become something more certain.
But she could not put it off forever.
She began with the kitchen.
Her grandmother's kitchen was small. But into it, the whole of her grandmother's living had been pressed. A well-used wooden cutting board. A knife with a loosening handle. A pot, blackened, used for decades. On the shelves, bowls and plates of every size, stacked neatly.
When she opened a drawer, the dried goods were sorted with meticulous care. Kombu. Dried sardines. Dried shiitake. Strips of dried radish. Her grandmother had been a person of stock. Whatever she made, she began by drawing the stock.
Haruka picked up a sheet of the kombu.
Rishiri kombu, the bag said. Her grandmother had used good kombu. Never well off, and yet the one thing she used well was the kombu at the base of the stock. Perhaps her single luxury.
Haruka decided to try drawing stock with that kombu.
In Tokyo, she had barely cooked. Even what she made was mostly transferring convenience-store food onto a plate. She had never drawn stock. Perhaps had never even used granulated dashi.
She filled a pot with water and sank the kombu.
After letting it stand, she set it over a low flame, slowly. She remembered, dimly, her grandmother doing it that way. Do not let it boil. Just before it boils, lift out the kombu. That was her grandmother's method.
The water in the pot changed color little by little. What had been clear turned, faintly, to gold.
The smell of kombu stock spread through the kitchen.
The instant she caught that smell, tears spilled from Haruka's eyes.
This smell. As a child, waking in her grandmother's house on summer mornings, this smell had always been there. This smell was her grandmother herself. Her grandmother was gone, and yet this smell alone had come back, like this, to Haruka's kitchen.
With that stock, Haruka made miso soup.
The only ingredients were tofu and wakame from the refrigerator. And yet that miso soup was more delicious than any she had ever tasted. It was her grandmother's taste. Or — still far from her grandmother's taste, perhaps. But certainly a taste that lay in that direction.
She wanted to tell Minato about it.

The next morning, at Migiwa, Haruka told him about the stock.
"I drew kombu stock," she said. "With my grandmother's kombu. Properly, for the first time in my life. And then I cried."
Minato stopped brewing and listened.
"Smell is unfair, isn't it," Haruka said. "Photographs, voices — those you can brace yourself to see and hear. But smell comes without warning. There's no guarding against it. The instant I caught the smell of that kombu stock, it was as if my grandmother were right there. Even though she's gone."
Minato nodded quietly.
"I understand," he said. "I have a smell like that, too."
"What smell?"
Minato hesitated, then answered.
"The smell of tide and machine oil, mixed," he said. "The smell of a fixed-net boat. It's my brother's smell."
Haruka started.
Minato, for the first time, had spoken of a brother.
"You have a brother?" she asked, carefully.
Minato was silent a while. Then he set the coffee in front of her.
"Had," he said. Past tense. "He died two years ago. At sea."
Haruka lost her words.
Two years ago. It overlapped with the time Minato had begun photographing the Tateyama beyond Meiwa Rock every morning. The "number of things" that had brought him home — this was it.
"I'm sorry," Haruka said. "I didn't know."
"It's all right," Minato said. "I didn't tell you. For some reason, today I feel like telling it. Because you told me about the smell of kombu stock."
Minato looked at the sea outside the window.
"My brother was a fixed-net fisherman," he began, slowly. "He took after our grandfather. A man of the sea. I was the opposite — I never much liked the sea. So I left for Tokyo and took photographs. My brother and I didn't get along well. Opposites since childhood. The last time we met, we quarreled. Over something trivial. He said, 'You're always running away.' And I said, 'What would you know — all you know is the sea.' That was our last conversation."
Haruka listened, holding still.
"The day my brother went out and didn't come back," Minato said, "I was in Tokyo. On a shoot. Word came, and I flew home, but I didn't make it in time. His body was found in the sea, near this Meiwa Rock."
Haruka looked, in spite of herself, at Meiwa Rock outside the window.
That black rock, standing quiet in the morning light. In the sea near that rock, Minato's brother had been found. That was why Minato photographed what lay beyond it, every morning. Haruka understood, at last.
"The reason I shoot there every morning," Minato said, as if reading her thoughts, "is that my brother is there. In that sea. I was running — my brother was right. I ran from the sea my whole life. So at least I'd come home and face it. Every morning I photograph that sea. Trying to see the last view my brother saw."
Haruka stood beside him and looked at the sea.
"The photograph you want — the coldest morning of the year," she said quietly. "Is it a photograph you want to show your brother?"
Minato looked at her, surprised.
Then he lowered his eyes.
"Maybe it is," he said. "The most beautiful morning my brother never got to see. I want to take it, and someday lay it before his grave. Maybe that's what I'm thinking. I don't quite know myself."
Haruka laid her hand, gently, over Minato's.
Her body moved before she thought.
Minato did not pull his hand away.
For a while, they stayed like that. Beyond the window, the Tateyama beyond Meiwa Rock shone quiet in the morning light.
"That morning," Haruka said, "we promised to wait for it together, didn't we."
"Yes," Minato said.
"I'll wait too," Haruka said. "For your morning — yours and your brother's, the most beautiful one. Together."
Minato closed his hand around hers.
His hand was warm.
On her way home that day, Haruka bought shiraebi at the harbor's direct-sale stall.
A mound of small, transparent shrimp. Jewels of the sea, that her grandmother had bought every year for a grandchild who never came.
Haruka made them into fritters, in her grandmother's kitchen.
In a pot of kombu stock she made a dipping broth, and dipped the fresh-fried shiraebi fritters into it, gently. They gave a crisp, sweet sound. In the mouth, the sweetness of the shiraebi spread, all at once. It was the taste she had eaten as a child.
Haruka set a plate of the shiraebi, too, before her grandmother's photograph.
"Grandma," she said to it. "This year, I made them. And I came home — properly — to eat them."
In the photograph, her grandmother smiled, gently, as she always had.

"Grandma," she said to it. "This year, I made them. And I came home — properly — to eat them."
In the photograph, her grandmother smiled, gently, as she always had.
