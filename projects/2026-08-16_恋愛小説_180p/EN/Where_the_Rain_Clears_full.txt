PROLOGUE — The March Light

When the train swung into the curve along the coast, the world outside the window turned suddenly bright.
It was Toyama Bay.
Haruka lifted her eyes from the phone she had been gripping in her lap. The March sea, which she had assumed would be grey, was bluer than she expected, and small points of light leapt and scattered across the waves. Beyond that light, far past the horizon, something white was floating.
At first she thought it was a cloud.
But it was too sharply drawn to be a cloud, and it did not move. On top of the blue sea, like a rampart cut into the sky, a range of snow-covered mountains stood in a long line. Beyond the sea, there were mountains. And such high mountains, at that. In the seven years she had lived in Tokyo, Haruka had forgotten this completely — that here, the sea and the mountains could fit inside the same view.
The Tateyama Range, said a voice somewhere deep in her chest. It was her grandmother's voice.
When she was a child, she had ridden this train with her grandmother. She had sat in the same seat, looking at the same sea. Her grandmother had rested Haruka's small hand on her own lap and said, "That's Tateyama, that is." Tsurugi, Dainichi, Yakushi — she had read off the names one by one, the way you might recite the names of relatives. Haruka remembered almost none of them. What she remembered was the dry warmth of her grandmother's hand, and how the fingertips she pressed to the window glass had been surprisingly cold.
That grandmother had died last month.
She had been eighty-three. As if falling asleep, the relatives said. And in fact, her grandmother had simply not woken up one morning in her own bed. In the kitchen, rice she had rinsed the night before was soaking in water, and in the pot a single sheet of kombu had been left sunk to draw the stock. Her grandmother had meant to make miso soup that morning too, as she always did.
Haruka had not gone home for the funeral.
To be precise, she had not been able to. That very week, she had a major presentation she had spent three months preparing. She told her manager, "My grandmother has died," and for just an instant his face softened — and then, as though it were the most natural thing in the world, he said, "That's… can't the schedule be moved?" Haruka said, "It's fine." Even now she does not know why she answered that way.
The presentation did not go through.
Before the client stood up, he did not so much as glance at the three months of materials Haruka had made. "It's just — not quite what we had in mind," was all he said. Not quite what we had in mind. For that one sentence, Haruka had sat at her desk through the New Year holidays and every weekend. She had no strength left to protest. Only, walking out of the meeting room, she caught her own face in the corridor window and thought: whose face is that? There were dark hollows under the eyes, the cheeks were sunken, and the corners of the mouth turned down, like a person who had forgotten how to smile.
That night, Haruka ran a fever.
In the middle of a thirty-nine-degree fever, she dreamed of her grandmother's kitchen. A morning kitchen that smelled of kombu. The light of the sea through the window. Her grandmother's back. The voice that said, "It's ready." When she woke, her pillow was soaked through, and Haruka realized she had been crying.
She handed in her resignation.
A small part of her had hoped to be stopped. But her manager took the letter and said, "I see. Well — I did push you too hard," and that was all. Whatever Haruka the person had left behind in that company over seven years weighed exactly as much as that single sentence.
And so, in the spring of her twenty-ninth year, Haruka came home with nothing.
The official reason was to clear out the house her grandmother had left. Her parents had long since built a new life in a distant place and wanted nothing to do with this house. "If you'll go, then it's yours to handle," her mother said over the phone. "You were always the one closest to Grandma, anyway."
The one closest to Grandma. That phrase, overlapping with the funeral she had not attended, pierced Haruka's chest in a thin, fine line.
The train entered a tunnel, and the sea vanished.
Haruka's face appeared in the window glass. It was a little better, at least, than that stranger's face she had seen on the night of the fever. And yet, deep in the eyes, the white of the fluorescent lights of that Tokyo meeting room still lingered.
The train came out of the tunnel, and the sea appeared again.
And then a station drew near.
It was a small, unstaffed station. Just beyond the platform was the sea, and between the platform and the water there was only a low concrete rail and a few pines. On the station sign, the name was written in hiragana.
Amaharashi.
The rain clears.
Haruka repeated the name quietly in her mouth. As a child she had thought this station name a strange phrase. The rain clears — as if it were a wish.
The train stopped.
She was the only one getting off.
When the doors opened, the smell of the sea came pouring into the car. The smell of the tide, and a little of seaweed, and from somewhere far off, the smell of coffee.
Haruka stepped down onto the platform, pulling her suitcase.
The March light was scattered across the whole surface of the sea. Beyond that light, the white range she had seen from the train stood now closer, and far more clearly. There was the sea, and beyond it the snow mountains, and the light filled the space between them.
For a while, Haruka simply stood there.
She could not move, her hand still on the handle of the suitcase.
She did not know why she could not move. Only that, looking at that view, she felt something that had been tied fast for a long time deep in her chest loosen, just a little. It resembled pain. Like the ache of frozen fingertips beginning to warm.
The wind blew, and the pine branches rang.
Haruka narrowed her eyes and looked at the mountains beyond the sea.
"I'm home," she said, to no one.
The words were swallowed at once by the sound of the waves. Even so, once she had said them, she felt a little lighter.
And so the days of Haruka's homecoming began.
That in this town she would meet a certain man — this, the Haruka of that day did not yet know.
That a morning would come when she would stand beside someone and look at the Tateyama Range across the sea — that, too, she did not yet know.

CHAPTER ONE — The Station by the Sea

Her grandmother's house stood halfway up a hill, about ten minutes' walk from the station.
It was an old single-story wooden house; the color of the roof tiles had faded entirely, and the front sliding door had warped in its frame. When Haruka pulled it with all her strength, the door resisted stubbornly at first, and then, as though suddenly giving up, opened with a rattling bang.
Inside, the house was cold.
Cold — but the smell was exactly the smell in her memory. The smell of tatami, the smell of old wood, and beneath all of it, sunk to the bottom, a faint smell of kombu. From the kitchen, Haruka thought.
She took off her shoes and stepped up, and the floorboards of the hallway rang cold against the soles of her feet.
In the living room, her grandmother's life remained just as it had been. On the low table, a half-drunk teacup sat overturned. A handwritten calendar hung on the wall, its dates stopped at February. From the day her grandmother died, time here had stopped moving.
Haruka stood in the kitchen.
Her grandmother's kitchen was small. But into it the whole of her grandmother's living had been pressed, tight. A well-used wooden cutting board. A knife whose handle was coming loose. A pot, blackened with soot, that must have been used for decades. On the shelf, bowls and plates of every size were stacked neatly.
When she opened a drawer, dried goods were sorted inside with meticulous care. Kombu. Dried sardines. Dried shiitake. Strips of dried radish. Her grandmother had been a person of stock. Whatever she made, she began by drawing the stock.
Haruka picked up a single sheet of the kombu.
Rishiri kombu, the bag said. Her grandmother had used good kombu. She had never been well off, and yet the one thing she used well was the kombu that formed the base of the stock. Perhaps it had been her grandmother's single luxury.
In the corner of the sink was a small pot.
When Haruka looked inside, there was water, and a single sheet of kombu. The last kombu her grandmother had sunk. Someone might have cleared it away, and yet the relatives had left this kitchen untouched. Or perhaps they simply could not bring themselves to touch it.
Haruka could not throw that kombu away.
She started to set the pot on the stove, but noticing that the gas valve was closed, she stopped. Instead, Haruka gently stroked the kombu in the pot with her fingertip. The softened kombu was more tender than she expected.
That night, Haruka slept in her grandmother's futon.
The reason was that laying out her own futon felt like too much trouble. But the truth, she thought later, was that she had wanted to sleep in the futon that smelled of her grandmother. Looking up at the ceiling, Haruka remembered the ceiling of her Tokyo apartment. There, the sounds of the next room, the footsteps of the floor above, the cars running outside, had all been endless. Here, all she could hear was the sound of the waves. And even that was so distant she had to strain to hear it.
For the first time in a long while, Haruka slept without dreaming.

The next morning, she woke early.
In Tokyo, on her days off, she had slept like mud until noon. But here, the light through the paper screens got a person up whether she liked it or not. The shadows of the screen's frame fell sharply across the tatami. From inside her futon, Haruka watched for a while as those shadows moved, little by little.
She got up and opened the storm shutters.
The sea was there.
The same sea as yesterday. But the light was clearer than yesterday. The morning sea was a different blue from the daytime sea. Deeper, quieter. Beyond it, the snow-covered range, catching the morning light, was faintly tinged with rose.
Haruka held her breath.
She had grown up in a place where she could see a view like this every morning. And yet she had forgotten it. Forgotten it, and for seven years looked at the grey view of a Tokyo window as though it were the natural order of things.
She was seized with an overwhelming urge for coffee.
But in the kitchen there was nothing to drink, let alone coffee. The refrigerator was empty. Her grandmother had, to the very end, kept the refrigerator meticulously empty — as if waiting at home for someone to come back, though no one ever would.
Haruka threw on a jacket and went outside.
Yesterday, when she had stepped down at the station, she remembered a smell of coffee drifting from somewhere. She did not think there would be a shop open at this hour, but she felt like walking. In any case, sitting still inside this house, she noticed nothing but her grandmother's absence.
She went down the hill, toward the station.
The early-morning town was still asleep. There were no people; all she could hear was the sound of the waves and the cries of gulls somewhere far off. She passed in front of the station, and walking along the coast road, she saw, in a gap between the pines, a single small building.
Two stories, wooden.
The window of the first floor opened wide toward the sea, and inside it a light was lit.
Above the entrance hung a wooden sign. A single character in ink.
Migiwa.
The water's edge, Haruka thought it must mean.
Beyond the window, someone was moving. Inside the counter stood a man. Tall, thin, his hair a little long. The man was making coffee. From a pot with a slender spout he was pouring hot water, letting it fall slowly, as though drawing a thread. His movements were so serious, so quiet, that for a while Haruka stood there outside the window, absorbed in watching him.
The man looked up suddenly.
Their eyes met.
Like a child caught peeping, Haruka hurriedly looked away. But it seemed unnatural to flee, and, thinking better of it, she put her hand on the entrance door.
A small bell rang.
"Welcome," the man said. His voice was low and a little hoarse. "We're not quite open yet, though."
"Ah — I'm sorry," Haruka faltered. "I thought you were open."
"No," the man said, not stopping the fall of the water. "We're open. When I say 'not quite,' I only mean that I'm not quite awake yet."
Haruka laughed a little at the way he said it.
"Sit down," the man said, indicating a counter stool with his eyes. "Do you drink coffee? The one I'm brewing now will be through in a moment."
Haruka took the stool at the end of the counter.
Through the window behind the man, she could see the sea. The sea, and the mountains beyond it, and the morning light. This shop was built facing the sea. Just below the window was the water, and far out, standing alone, was a single large rock — as if left behind and forgotten in the sea.
"That rock — what's it called?" Haruka asked.
Following the line of her gaze, the man said, "That's Meiwa." "It reads meiwa. Beyond that rock you can see the Tateyama Range, can't you. People who take photographs often come for that composition."
"Photographs."
"Yes." The man set the coffee on the counter in front of Haruka. "I take them too. Every morning."
From the steaming cup, a deep fragrance rose. Haruka wrapped both hands around it. It was warm. From her fingertips, that warmth traveled slowly to the core of her body.
She took a sip.
It was bitter, and yet, behind the bitterness, something sweet was hidden. It felt like a taste she had drunk long, long ago, though she could not remember where.
"It's good," Haruka said, without thinking.
The man looked a little surprised. Then, just slightly, the corner of his mouth eased. Not quite a smile — but the sleepy hardness of a moment before vanished, softly, from his face.
"Yuzuki," the man said. "Yuzuki Minato. I'm the owner here."
"Konno," Haruka gave her own name. "Konno Haruka. I've — moved into the house up the hill past here. Or rather, come back to it."
"Konno-san," the man repeated, as if confirming the name. Then, as though noticing something, he went still. "The Konno up the hill… you don't mean Sawako-san's?"
Haruka looked up, startled.
"You knew my grandmother?"
"Yes," Minato said quietly. "She came often. A regular of ours."
Haruka lost her words.
Her grandmother, at this shop. At the counter of this shop by the sea, she had sat, as Haruka was sitting now. When she pictured it, her chest tightened. Haruka had known nothing of her grandmother's life. How she had spent her days in this town, at what shop, drinking what coffee. Her own grandchild, and she had known nothing.
"Sawako-san always sat at that seat," Minato said, indicating with his eyes the seat at the very back of the counter, near the window. "From there, she'd look at the sea. She'd take her time over a single cup of coffee, and then she'd go home."
"…I see."
"I heard she passed last month," Minato said, his voice lowering a little. "My condolences. I'd been thinking she hadn't come by, lately."
Haruka dropped her eyes to the cup. Inside it, the morning light was reflected in a small circle.
"I—" Haruka began, and stopped. She did not know what she had meant to say. I didn't go home for the funeral. I know nothing about my grandmother. I — every word caught in her throat and would not come.
Minato asked nothing more.
He only said, when Haruka's cup was empty, "Shall I brew you another? Today's is a dark roast. I think the second cup is when the flavor comes through."
Haruka nodded.
Minato began again to let the water fall like a thread. Watching those quiet movements, Haruka felt, for no reason she could name, tears rising. She did not understand it. Only that, inside this shop, inside this light, this smell, she could feel something she had held taut in Tokyo unravel, slowly.
Beyond the window, the mountains past Meiwa Rock shone white in the morning light.
"Those mountains," Haruka said. "My grandmother taught me their names when I was small. Tsurugi, Dainichi. But I don't remember."
"If you like," Minato said, setting the second cup before her, "I'll teach you again. On a clear morning, you can see all of them."
Haruka gave a small nod.
That morning, she drank two cups of coffee and left the shop.
As she left, Minato said, "Come again." It might have been a courtesy. But all the way up the hill and home, Haruka held that one phrase, warm, in her hands.
Back at the house, the kombu in the pot in the kitchen sat quietly in the water, catching the morning light.
Haruka set the pot on the stove. The gas valve she had opened before going out.
The water in the pot, before long, began faintly to steam.
The smell of kombu stock spread through the whole kitchen.
It was the smell of her grandmother.

CHAPTER TWO — The Coffee Shop Called Migiwa

After that, Haruka began going to Migiwa every morning.
"Going," though, was too grand a word. She would simply wake, open the storm shutters, look at the sea — and her feet would turn that way of their own accord. There was still no equipment in her grandmother's house for making coffee. She knew, herself, that she was using this as an excuse.
Minato was always there before her.
By the time Haruka arrived, the shop's window would already be open toward the sea, and behind the counter came the sound of coffee beans being ground. It was a hand mill. The low, grinding rhythm of it rang out steadily in the morning quiet.
"Good morning," Haruka would say.
And Minato, without stopping his grinding, would answer only, "Morning." Somewhere along the way, the politeness had dropped out of his speech. It tickled her, a little.
Migiwa was a strange shop.
There was no menu. Nothing with a price on it was posted, on the walls or the tables. When a customer came, Minato did not ask, "What will you have today?" He simply brewed coffee with the best bean he had that day. Some customers, Haruka thought, would find that irritating. But the customers who came to this shop, it seemed, were not that kind of customer.
The regulars were all elderly.
The first to arrive each morning was an old man named Matsuzo. Over eighty now, he said. In his youth he had been a fixed-net fisherman, and his whole face was burned by sun and salt, cut with deep wrinkles. Matsuzo always sat at the very front of the counter, and before touching his coffee he would gaze at the sea outside the window. Then, quietly, he would say something about the sea that day.
"Calm today," he might say. Or, "West wind's come in. It'll turn rough by afternoon."
Minato would answer only, "That so."
Haruka loved that exchange. In Matsuzo's words were seventy years of the sea. More surely than any forecast, Matsuzo named the sea of the next day.
"You're Sawako-san's granddaughter, aren't you," Matsuzo said to her on the third day.
"Yes," Haruka nodded.
"You resemble her," Matsuzo said, looking her over. "Around the eyes. Sawako had eyes like that too, when she was young."
"You knew my grandmother? When she was young?"
"Same age, we were," Matsuzo said, and his wrinkled face smiled a little. "Went to the same primary school. That woman — she had a daughter who married off to Tokyo, and every year she looked forward, so much, to that daughter coming home in summer with a grandchild."
Realizing that the grandchild was herself, Haruka's words caught.
"But that grandchild, too, once she grew up, stopped coming home," Matsuzo went on, as if to himself, still looking at the sea. "And even so, every year when summer came, Sawako bought shiraebi. Because the grandchild liked them, she said. Even though the grandchild never came."
Something sharp ran through Haruka's chest.
Shiraebi. The white shrimp. She remembered them — the shiraebi fritters she had eaten at her grandmother's house as a child, sweet and crisp, dissolving in the mouth. Every year, her grandmother had gone on buying them for a grandchild who never came.
"Matsuzo-san," Minato cut in, quietly. "That's enough of that."
Matsuzo shut his mouth as if startled. "Sorry," he said, not looking at Haruka. "Bad habit of an old man. Nothing but old stories."
"No," Haruka said, shaking her head. Her voice would not come out right. "…Thank you. For telling me about her."
That day, even when she had finished her coffee, Haruka could not rise from her seat for a while.
Matsuzo left, and the other regulars, and the shop was left with only Haruka and Minato. Behind the counter, Minato was washing up. The sound of the water rang quietly.
"Matsuzo-san means no harm," Minato said, in a gap between the water. "He's just a man who says what he thinks, as he thinks it."
"I know," Haruka said. "It only hurt more because it's true."
Minato stopped washing.
Haruka looked at the sea outside the window. Meiwa Rock stood quiet in the morning light.
"I didn't go home for my grandmother's funeral," she said.
The words she had not meant to speak came out. "There was work. An important presentation. But the presentation didn't go through. What I'd made — no one even glanced at it. So I quit the company too. My grandmother's funeral, and the work that mattered — I lost both, and came here."
Minato said nothing.
He only set a new cup of coffee in front of her. A coffee she had not ordered.
"This," Minato said, "is the bean Sawako-san liked."
Haruka looked at the cup.
"Every year, when summer came, Sawako-san ordered a slightly finer bean," Minato said. "Because she wanted to serve it to her grandchild, when the grandchild came. So she could say her coffee was better than the cafés in Tokyo — she said she practiced with a good bean, to be ready."
Tears spilled from Haruka's eyes.
However she tried to stop them, they would not stop. They fell onto the counter, drop by drop. Haruka wiped them with the back of her hand. Wipe as she might, more came, and more. In seven years — in Tokyo meeting rooms, on packed trains, in her lonely apartment — she had never once cried. Could not. And now, in this shop by the sea, before a coffee she had not even ordered, it came out as though a dam had broken.
Minato, seeing it, did not panic.
He only waited, quietly, for Haruka to cry herself out. Beyond the window the waves came and went. A gull cried. Far off, a fishing boat passed, trailing a white wake.
"Drink it slowly," Minato said, when she had calmed a little, and withdrew to the back of the shop. He was leaving her alone, Haruka understood. And she was grateful.
She drank the coffee her grandmother had loved, slowly.
It was bitter, and yet, behind the bitterness, the taste she had drunk long ago was certainly there. And now she could remember what taste it was. It was the taste of her grandmother's kitchen. In that morning kitchen that smelled of kombu stock, on special days, her grandmother had brewed her real coffee — not instant — just for her.
Her grandmother, Haruka understood, had kept this taste alive. For a grandchild who never came.
That day, for the first time, Haruka stayed a long time in the shop. Until nearly noon, she passed the time watching the sea outside the window. Minato came out now and then, ground beans, tended to the regulars. He left her be. And yet she did not feel left alone. It felt more like being, quietly, together in the same space.
On her way out, Haruka asked, "Why did you take on this shop?"
Minato paused before answering.
"It was my grandfather's shop," he said. "I was in Tokyo, studying photography. I wanted to be a news photographer. But — a number of things happened, and I came back, and took over the shop."
"A number of things."
"Yes." Minato said no more.
And Haruka asked no more.
Just as she could not yet tell anyone, in detail, about the funeral she had not attended, so Minato must have "a number of things" he could not speak of. Everyone carries something they cannot say aloud, and still, every morning, wakes, brews coffee, looks at the sea, and lives.
When she stepped outside, the sky had begun to cloud.
"It'll turn rough in the afternoon," Haruka said, remembering Matsuzo's words.
"Yes," Minato said, looking up at the sky. "If Matsuzo-san says so, it will. His forecasts come true."
That afternoon, the sea did turn rough.
From the window of her grandmother's house, Haruka watched the sea whiten with waves. The sea that had been calm only that morning now heaved like a different living thing. White spray struck Meiwa Rock again and again.
The sea changes its face so much, she thought.
She remembered the Tokyo scenery. That town's scenery had always been the same. Rain or shine, buildings were buildings, roads were roads, people were people. The scenery did not change.
Here, the sea wore a different face every day.
And that day, for the first time, Haruka thought that living in this town, a little, might not be so bad.

CHAPTER THREE — The Night of the Firefly Squid

When April came, the air of the town softened, little by little.
The color of the sea shifted from the deep blue of winter to the pale blue of spring. The buds of the pines in the grove stretched into a bright green. And at night, from the direction of the fishing harbor, a liveliness that had not been there before began to reach her on the wind.
"It's hotaru-ika season," Matsuzo said.
Firefly squid. Haruka knew the name. But she had never seen the real thing. In a Tokyo supermarket she had seen them packed in trays — boiled, dressed with vinegar-miso, those tiny squid.
"Those are the corpses of firefly squid," Matsuzo said, and gave a snort. "The real thing glows. The whole night sea glows blue. You've never seen it, have you."
"No."
"Then go and see," Matsuzo said. "Tonight, there might be a beaching."
"A beaching?"
"The firefly squid come up onto the shore at night, to spawn," Minato explained from beside them. "On a calm night around the new moon. The whole beach glows blue. Even the locals don't get to see it often."
Haruka tried to picture it, and could not quite. The whole beach, glowing blue. It sounded like something not of this world.
"Shall we go and see it tonight?" Minato asked her.
Haruka looked at him, surprised. It was the first time Minato had invited her to anything himself.
"Is that all right?" she asked.
"Tonight's the new moon," Minato said. "No wind. If Matsuzo-san says there'll be a beaching, there probably will be. I want to take photographs, too."
That night, Haruka bundled up in warm clothes and set off for the shore.
At the appointed time, she met Minato in front of Migiwa. He was carrying a tripod and camera. The night beach was an entirely different place from the day one. There were no streetlights and no moon; the whole world was dark, and only the sound of the waves came out of the black.
"Watch your feet," Minato said, lighting the ground at her feet with a flashlight. "There are a lot of rocks along here."
The two of them walked the beach.
For a while, nothing happened. The sea was simply black, the waves lifting only their white edges into the dark before they vanished. Little by little, Haruka grew cold. She began to think the firefly squid would not come.
"They'll come," Minato said, as if reading her mind. "Let's wait just a little longer."
The two of them sat on a piece of driftwood and looked at the sea.
In the dark, Minato's profile was almost invisible. Only the fact of his presence, close beside her, was there. Had she ever, Haruka wondered, sat beside someone like this, silently gazing into the dark? In Tokyo, the gaps had always been filled — with words, with sound, with light. Silence was an awkward thing. But now, the silence with Minato was not awkward. If anything, it was comfortable.
"Do you always shoot alone?" Haruka asked.
"Yes," Minato said. "Every morning. The Tateyama across the sea, beyond Meiwa Rock. Alone, at an hour when no one comes."
"Every morning?"
"For two years now," Minato said. "And I still haven't taken one that I can call the one."
"Two years. The same place, for two years."
"Even the same place is never the same morning twice," Minato said. "The light, the clouds, the waves, how the mountains show — every morning is different. Someday the morning I want to photograph will come. I'm waiting for it."
"What kind of morning do you want to photograph?"
Minato was quiet for a while.
Then, slowly, he said, "Probably — the coldest morning of the year. A deep-winter morning, windless, clear. The air frozen, the sea gone perfectly still, and beyond Meiwa Rock, the whole Tateyama Range, sharp and complete. There are only a few such mornings in a year. I want to photograph the light of one of them."
Haruka looked at his profile in the dark.
"Ah," Minato said softly.
Haruka followed his gaze.
The sea was glowing.
At the water's edge, points of blue light had appeared. At first only a few. But when the next wave came in, the light increased. A pale, phantom-blue light, carried by the waves, washing in toward the shore, and in, and in.
"The beaching," Minato whispered.
Haruka stood. She ran to the water's edge.
There, countless firefly squid had been cast ashore. Each one gave off a blue light. It was as if the stars of the night sky had come down whole to the beach. Haruka crouched and lifted one gently onto her palm.
Cold, tiny — and yet, certainly, glowing.
It was the light of a life. A light of small creatures that had come to the shore at the cost of their lives, to spawn — their last light. Haruka gazed at it in her palm.
For some reason, tears rose.
This was a light for death. And yet, at the same time, it was a light to bring forth new life. Ending and beginning were in the same single light.
"It's beautiful," Haruka whispered.
The word was not enough. But there was no other word.
A little away, Minato had set up his tripod. The sound of the shutter rang quietly in the dark.
Haruka returned the firefly squid on her palm gently to the sea. The blue light was carried away by the waves and vanished into the dark.
"Yuzuki-san," she said, turning to him. "Did you get it? The photograph — the one?"
Minato was peering into the camera's monitor.
"No," he said, with a small laugh. "This isn't it either. But — it's not bad."
For a while, the two of them looked at the glowing sea.
Before long, the beaching subsided. The blue lights thinned, and when the last one was returned to the waves, the sea went back to being an ordinary black sea. As if what they had seen had been a dream.
On the way back, Haruka and Minato walked the dark beach side by side.
"Thank you for showing me," Haruka said. "I'd never seen anything like it."
"I see it every year," Minato said. "And every year I'm astonished, as if for the first time. I'm glad I could show you something good."
In front of Migiwa, they parted.
"Good night," Haruka said.
"Good night," Minato said. And then, as though hesitating, he added, "There'll be coffee in the morning, too."
Haruka smiled.
"I'll come."
Climbing the hill home, Haruka could not sleep for a while.
When she closed her eyes, that blue light floated up behind them. The single light of ending and beginning.
And, beside that light, Minato's profile.
Deep in her chest, Haruka felt something begin to glow, small, like a firefly squid.
She did not yet know the name of that light.
Or perhaps she knew it, and was pretending not to.


--------------------------------------------------------

CHAPTER FOUR — The Promise at Meiwa Rock

After the night of the firefly squid, something between Haruka and Minato shifted, just a little.
It was not a visible change. As before, Haruka went to Migiwa every morning, and Minato brewed coffee. Matsuzo spoke of the sea, and the regulars looked at it quietly. Nothing seemed to have changed. But Haruka could tell. That Minato exchanged a few more words than before. That when Haruka arrived, something like a faint relief crossed his face. It was a small change only Haruka noticed.
One morning, she woke earlier than usual.
The night had not yet fully lifted. The sky was moving, little by little, from indigo toward a thin pale blue. For some reason, Haruka wanted to go to Migiwa. At this hour Minato would be photographing the Tateyama beyond Meiwa Rock — alone, at an hour when no one came, as he had said.
That "hour when no one came," Haruka found she wanted to see.
She threw on a jacket and went down the hill.
The shop was still dark. Haruka passed it and went down to the beach.
On the beach, Minato stood. Tripod set, camera raised, he was looking out past the sea. His back did not stir. Haruka hesitated to call out. In that back there was a quiet concentration one must not break.
She stood a little apart and looked in the same direction.
Meiwa Rock stood quiet in the pale dawn light. Beyond it, the Tateyama Range showed — dimly, but certainly. The ridgeline was beginning, little by little, to take the morning light. What had been indigo turned to pale violet, then flushed, faintly, with rose.
Haruka caught her breath.
The color of the sky changed moment by moment. The same view became, minute by minute, an entirely different view. She remembered Minato's words: even the same place is never the same morning twice. It was true. Truly.
Before long, a single point on the horizon blazed.
The sun.
It rose not from beyond the mountains, but from the hills at Haruka's back, on this side of the sea. And its light struck the Tateyama Range across the water head-on. The snow-covered peaks, taking the morning sun, shone gold. The sea reflected the light, and set countless ripples glittering gold.
In that instant, Minato's shutter fired, again and again.
Kasha, kasha, kasha.
Then Minato lifted his face from the camera. And exhaled, a great breath — as if he had held it a long time.
"Yuzuki-san," Haruka called.
Minato turned. He seemed surprised to find her there.
"Konno-san," he said. "How long have you been there?"
"A little while," Haruka said. "I thought I shouldn't interrupt."
Minato looked slightly at a loss. Then he asked, "Did you watch?"
"I did," Haruka said. "It was beautiful. To think you see something this beautiful, alone, every morning."
Minato said nothing. He only dropped his eyes to the monitor.
"Did you get it?" Haruka asked. "The one?"
Still gazing at the screen, Minato shook his head.
"Not today either," he said. "Today there were too few clouds. I wanted a little cloud. Cloud on the mountains makes the light more complex. But — it's not bad."
Haruka stood beside him and looked into the monitor.
On the small screen, the Tateyama Range shone gold. The black silhouette of Meiwa Rock stood quiet before it. The sea glowed gold. It was the very view she had just seen with her eyes. And yet the one in the photograph looked, somehow, more urgent than the one before her — as if Minato had folded some kind of prayer into this view.
"To me it looks perfect," Haruka said.
"Perfect is no good," Minato said. "What I want to shoot isn't a perfect view. It's — how to put it — a morning so beautiful it hurts. A morning you know will never come again, and that even so, being there, feels like a miracle."
Haruka looked at his profile.
In those words, the "number of things" Minato carried showed through, faintly. Something that would never come again. In this morning shoot, perhaps Minato was searching for something that would never return. Or waiting for it.
She did not ask.
Instead, looking at the mountains beyond the sea, Haruka said, "My grandmother taught me the names of those mountains, when I was a child. But I don't remember them. Yuzuki-san, you said you'd teach me."
Minato looked a little surprised. Then he nodded.
"Yes," he said, and pointed out beyond the sea. "The sharpest one there — that's Tsurugi. 2,999 meters. One of the hardest mountains to climb in Japan. To its left, Dainichi. Behind it, that gentler one — that's Onanji, the main peak of Tateyama."
One by one, Minato named the mountains. Yakushi. Kuwasaki. The three Kedomaru peaks. Even hearing the names, Haruka could not immediately tell one from another. But hearing those names in Minato's voice, the mountains began to look less like mere scenery and more like presences, each with a name and a face of its own.
"Amazing," Haruka said. "You remember them all."
"I look at them every morning," Minato said. "Look for two years and you remember them whether you like it or not. Those mountains are my conversation partners, every morning."
Haruka laughed.
Minato laughed a little too.
The morning light filled, slowly, the beach where they stood.
"Say," Haruka said, in that light. "The photograph you want to take — the coldest morning of the year. If you get it, will you show me too?"
Minato looked at her.
"If you're still in this town by then," he said.
"I'll be here," Haruka said — more clearly than she had expected of herself. "I'll be in this town."
Having said it, she felt a little embarrassed.
Because it sounded like a promise. A promise to be in this town. To be here until the morning Minato wanted to photograph came.
Minato looked at her for a while.
Then he turned his gaze back to the Tateyama Range across the sea.
"Then," he said quietly, "I promise. When that morning comes, you'll be the first I show it to. No — more than that."
He paused, choosing his words.
"Let's see it together," Minato said. "The coldest, clearest morning of the year. The morning when the whole Tateyama Range shows beyond Meiwa Rock. Won't you wait for that morning — with me?"
Haruka's heart struck, hard.
"Yes," she said. "I'll wait. Together."
Beyond Meiwa Rock, the Tateyama Range shone in the morning sun.
Haruka and Minato looked at those mountains, side by side.
Between them, a promise had been born. A promise with no name yet — and yet a certain one.
From that day, Haruka began to wait for winter.
For the coldest morning of the year. For the morning she would see the Tateyama across the sea, standing beside Minato.
Spring had only just begun, and already Haruka was thinking of a still-distant winter.


--------------------------------------------------------

CHAPTER FIVE — The Season of White Shrimp

When May came, the season of shiraebi arrived.
The white shrimp — small and transparent, caught in quantity, they say, only in Toyama Bay. Jewels of the sea, they are called; Haruka learned that from Minato.
"They're the ones Sawako-san bought every year," Minato said. "For her grandchild."
The words left another small ache in Haruka's chest. The white shrimp her grandmother had bought every year for a grandchild who never came. This year, Haruka decided, she would eat them — not for her grandmother, but with the feeling of eating them together with her. Even though her grandmother was gone.
That weekend, Haruka began to clear out her grandmother's house.
For nearly two months since coming back, she had kept putting off this clearing. She had been afraid to touch her grandmother's things. Once she touched them, her grandmother's absence would become something more certain.
But she could not put it off forever.
She began with the kitchen.
Her grandmother's kitchen was small. But into it, the whole of her grandmother's living had been pressed. A well-used wooden cutting board. A knife with a loosening handle. A pot, blackened, used for decades. On the shelves, bowls and plates of every size, stacked neatly.
When she opened a drawer, the dried goods were sorted with meticulous care. Kombu. Dried sardines. Dried shiitake. Strips of dried radish. Her grandmother had been a person of stock. Whatever she made, she began by drawing the stock.
Haruka picked up a sheet of the kombu.
Rishiri kombu, the bag said. Her grandmother had used good kombu. Never well off, and yet the one thing she used well was the kombu at the base of the stock. Perhaps her single luxury.
Haruka decided to try drawing stock with that kombu.
In Tokyo, she had barely cooked. Even what she made was mostly transferring convenience-store food onto a plate. She had never drawn stock. Perhaps had never even used granulated dashi.
She filled a pot with water and sank the kombu.
After letting it stand, she set it over a low flame, slowly. She remembered, dimly, her grandmother doing it that way. Do not let it boil. Just before it boils, lift out the kombu. That was her grandmother's method.
The water in the pot changed color little by little. What had been clear turned, faintly, to gold.
The smell of kombu stock spread through the kitchen.
The instant she caught that smell, tears spilled from Haruka's eyes.
This smell. As a child, waking in her grandmother's house on summer mornings, this smell had always been there. This smell was her grandmother herself. Her grandmother was gone, and yet this smell alone had come back, like this, to Haruka's kitchen.
With that stock, Haruka made miso soup.
The only ingredients were tofu and wakame from the refrigerator. And yet that miso soup was more delicious than any she had ever tasted. It was her grandmother's taste. Or — still far from her grandmother's taste, perhaps. But certainly a taste that lay in that direction.
She wanted to tell Minato about it.

The next morning, at Migiwa, Haruka told him about the stock.
"I drew kombu stock," she said. "With my grandmother's kombu. Properly, for the first time in my life. And then I cried."
Minato stopped brewing and listened.
"Smell is unfair, isn't it," Haruka said. "Photographs, voices — those you can brace yourself to see and hear. But smell comes without warning. There's no guarding against it. The instant I caught the smell of that kombu stock, it was as if my grandmother were right there. Even though she's gone."
Minato nodded quietly.
"I understand," he said. "I have a smell like that, too."
"What smell?"
Minato hesitated, then answered.
"The smell of tide and machine oil, mixed," he said. "The smell of a fixed-net boat. It's my brother's smell."
Haruka started.
Minato, for the first time, had spoken of a brother.
"You have a brother?" she asked, carefully.
Minato was silent a while. Then he set the coffee in front of her.
"Had," he said. Past tense. "He died two years ago. At sea."
Haruka lost her words.
Two years ago. It overlapped with the time Minato had begun photographing the Tateyama beyond Meiwa Rock every morning. The "number of things" that had brought him home — this was it.
"I'm sorry," Haruka said. "I didn't know."
"It's all right," Minato said. "I didn't tell you. For some reason, today I feel like telling it. Because you told me about the smell of kombu stock."
Minato looked at the sea outside the window.
"My brother was a fixed-net fisherman," he began, slowly. "He took after our grandfather. A man of the sea. I was the opposite — I never much liked the sea. So I left for Tokyo and took photographs. My brother and I didn't get along well. Opposites since childhood. The last time we met, we quarreled. Over something trivial. He said, 'You're always running away.' And I said, 'What would you know — all you know is the sea.' That was our last conversation."
Haruka listened, holding still.
"The day my brother went out and didn't come back," Minato said, "I was in Tokyo. On a shoot. Word came, and I flew home, but I didn't make it in time. His body was found in the sea, near this Meiwa Rock."
Haruka looked, in spite of herself, at Meiwa Rock outside the window.
That black rock, standing quiet in the morning light. In the sea near that rock, Minato's brother had been found. That was why Minato photographed what lay beyond it, every morning. Haruka understood, at last.
"The reason I shoot there every morning," Minato said, as if reading her thoughts, "is that my brother is there. In that sea. I was running — my brother was right. I ran from the sea my whole life. So at least I'd come home and face it. Every morning I photograph that sea. Trying to see the last view my brother saw."
Haruka stood beside him and looked at the sea.
"The photograph you want — the coldest morning of the year," she said quietly. "Is it a photograph you want to show your brother?"
Minato looked at her, surprised.
Then he lowered his eyes.
"Maybe it is," he said. "The most beautiful morning my brother never got to see. I want to take it, and someday lay it before his grave. Maybe that's what I'm thinking. I don't quite know myself."
Haruka laid her hand, gently, over Minato's.
Her body moved before she thought.
Minato did not pull his hand away.
For a while, they stayed like that. Beyond the window, the Tateyama beyond Meiwa Rock shone quiet in the morning light.
"That morning," Haruka said, "we promised to wait for it together, didn't we."
"Yes," Minato said.
"I'll wait too," Haruka said. "For your morning — yours and your brother's, the most beautiful one. Together."
Minato closed his hand around hers.
His hand was warm.
On her way home that day, Haruka bought shiraebi at the harbor's direct-sale stall.
A mound of small, transparent shrimp. Jewels of the sea, that her grandmother had bought every year for a grandchild who never came.
Haruka made them into fritters, in her grandmother's kitchen.
In a pot of kombu stock she made a dipping broth, and dipped the fresh-fried shiraebi fritters into it, gently. They gave a crisp, sweet sound. In the mouth, the sweetness of the shiraebi spread, all at once. It was the taste she had eaten as a child.
Haruka set a plate of the shiraebi, too, before her grandmother's photograph.
"Grandma," she said to it. "This year, I made them. And I came home — properly — to eat them."
In the photograph, her grandmother smiled, gently, as she always had.


--------------------------------------------------------

CHAPTER SIX — The Sails of the Kaiwo Maru

Summer came.
Toyama's summer was hotter than Haruka had imagined. She had assumed, without thinking, that with the sea it would be cool, but a damp heat flowed in from the direction of the inland basin and wrapped the town. Still, toward evening a wind came off the sea, and it grew somewhat cooler. Yūsuzumi — the evening cool — was a word Haruka learned, for the first time, with her body, in this town.
A wind bell was hung at Migiwa.
Minato had brought out an old glass one from somewhere and hung it under the eaves. Each time the sea wind blew, it rang, chirin, a clear note. That sound did not break the shop's quiet. If anything, it deepened it.
"I hang it every summer," Minato said. "My grandfather used to."
Haruka loved the sound of that wind bell.
Through the summer, Haruka's life, little by little, began to take a shape.
The clearing of her grandmother's house went slowly. There was no need to hurry. One by one, Haruka took up her grandmother's things, imagined their histories, and then divided them into what to keep and what to let go. There was little to let go. Her grandmother's things were all still usable. Her grandmother had been a person who cared for things.
And Haruka had begun to work.
Not much of a job, admittedly. A small print shop in town had been looking for someone to design flyers and posters, Matsuzo had told her. When she said she had been a graphic designer in Tokyo, the shop's owner all but leapt for joy. "To think a person like that is here in this town."
What Haruka designed were posters for the town's festivals, flyers for shopping-street sales, notices for fishery-cooperative events. Compared to the work she had done in Tokyo, the scale and the pay were beyond comparison. And yet Haruka found she liked the work more than she had expected. A flyer she had made would be posted on the town notice board, and someone who saw it would come to the festival. Her design was, certainly, reaching someone's eyes, moving someone. That made Haruka happy.
In Tokyo, what Haruka made had always been waiting on someone's approval. The manager's, the client's. And that approval was often withheld. "Not quite what we had in mind" — and three months of work vanished.
Here, what Haruka made reached the townspeople's eyes, straight.
"That poster you made was good," the fishmonger's wife said. "My grandkid saw it and won't stop pestering me to take him to the festival."
That one sentence warmed Haruka's chest more than any high pay in Tokyo ever had.

One day in August, Minato invited Haruka to Kaiwo Maru Park.
"The Kaiwo Maru is going to unfurl all her sails," Minato said. "Every one of them. You only get to see it a few times a year. I'm going to photograph it — come with me, if you like."
The Kaiwo Maru. Haruka knew the name. A great sailing ship once used to train sailors, now moored in the harbor like a museum on the water. Haruka too had seen it once as a child, taken by her grandmother.
That day, the two of them went to Kaiwo Maru Park.
When they reached the harbor, a white sailing ship floated there. Four masts rose straight into the blue summer sky. The sails were still furled. But even the bare frame of it had an overwhelming presence.
Before long, the unfurling began.
Sailors climbed the masts and spread the sails, one after another. White sails opened, one, and another, into the summer sky. When every sail was open, it was like twenty-nine white wings. A vast white bird, floating on the sea. Against the blue sea and the blue sky, that whiteness was almost blinding.
"It's beautiful," Haruka said, without thinking.
"They call her the Lady of the Sea," Minato said, raising his camera. "When you see her like this, you understand why."
Minato pressed the shutter.
Haruka stood beside him, looking at the white ship.
And then, suddenly, she looked at Minato.
The profile of a man raising his camera was serious. The single-minded concentration of peering into the viewfinder. The quiet heat of trying to capture the white sails of the Kaiwo Maru. Haruka wanted to keep looking at that profile. More than the ship, more than the sails — at that profile.
Her own feeling, she knew, was already clear.
That thing that had begun to glow deep in her chest in spring, on the night of the firefly squid. She already knew the name of that light.
I love him, Haruka thought.
I love this man.
But she could not say it aloud.
Minato and his memory of his brother. Those two years of prayer toward the sea beyond Meiwa Rock. Minato was still in the middle of that prayer. He had not yet taken the most beautiful morning. His heart might still be full with waiting for it. Was there room, there, for Haruka's feeling to enter? Haruka did not know.
That night, the Kaiwo Maru was lit up.
The white sails, lit, rose out of the dark like an apparition. On the water's surface the light was reflected, and swayed. As if two ships of light floated upon the sea.
Haruka and Minato looked at those ships of light, side by side.
"It's beautiful," Haruka said.
"Yes," Minato said.
For a while, they watched the light in silence.
"Konno-san," Minato said, suddenly. "That is—"
He faltered.
Haruka looked at him. His face was faintly lit by the light of the ships.
"Since you came to this town," Minato said, choosing his words, "my mornings have changed. Before, I only shot as if it were a duty. Like a kind of atonement to my brother. But now—"
He stopped.
Haruka waited for the rest. Her heart pounded.
"Now," Minato said quietly, "every morning, I look forward to you coming to the shop. It's a strange way to put it. I take the photographs of the sea, go back to the shop, and wait for you to come. That's my morning, now."
Haruka's chest filled.
"So do I," Haruka said. Her voice shook. "Every morning, I look forward to going to Migiwa. I wake, look at the sea, go down the hill. And you're there, brewing coffee, waiting. That morning — it feels like it's keeping me alive."
Minato looked at her.
The light of the ships lit their two faces, faintly.
Minato leaned, a little, toward her.
Haruka began to close her eyes.
And then—
"Minato-kun!"
A voice called, from far off.
The two of them started apart.
When they looked, Matsuzo was walking toward them, waving. He had come to see the Kaiwo Maru unfurl, it seemed. "Well now — you two came too, did you," Matsuzo said, without a care. "Fine thing you saw. Those sails of the Kaiwo Maru — never tire of them, however many times."
Haruka and Minato looked at each other, and laughed, just a little.
It had been so close. So close, and the two of them would have confirmed their feelings with something other than words.
But that "so close" stayed in Haruka as a sweet ache.
There's no need to hurry, she thought.
There is still time until winter comes. While they waited, together, for the coldest, most beautiful morning of the year, surely they would cross that "so close."
So Haruka believed.
But that at the end of summer a single phone call from Tokyo would come to her — this, the Haruka of that night did not yet know.


--------------------------------------------------------

CHAPTER SEVEN — The Locked Drawer

The call came on a humid night at the end of August.
It was from Yoko. A colleague from Haruka's Tokyo days. No — more than a colleague; in seven years, the one person Haruka had been able to open her heart to. They had been in the same department, worked the same late nights, worn down the same way. Even after Haruka left the company, she and Yoko had kept in touch now and then.
"Haruka, how are you?" Yoko's voice was familiar. "How's life out there?"
"Good," Haruka said. "Not bad. The sea's beautiful."
"The sea, huh," Yoko said, a little envious. "Nice. Here it's the same as ever. Buildings, trains, deadlines."
They talked of this and that. Then Yoko changed her tone, a little, and said, "Actually — there's something I wanted to talk to you about."
Yoko was starting a small design office of her own, she said. She was gathering people. Designers she could trust, who were good. And the first to come to mind had been Haruka.
"Won't you come back?" Yoko said. "This time, instead of being used by someone, let's do what we want to do. Good work — nobody telling us 'not quite what we had in mind.' Your talent, Haruka — wearing it down at a place like that was a waste. I always thought so."
Haruka could not answer right away.
"Sorry to spring it on you," Yoko said. "You don't have to answer now. But think about it. I want to launch by the end of the year, so — if you could let me know by autumn."
After she hung up, Haruka could not move for a while.
Outside, the sound of the waves.
She felt her own heart wavering.
Yoko's words had struck at the softest place in her. "Wearing your talent down was a waste." No one had said such a thing to Haruka. Not her manager, not the client. Haruka herself had thought her talent long since worn out. And Yoko still believed in it. More — this time, instead of being used, they would do what they wanted. That was what Haruka had dreamed of for seven years.
But—
Haruka looked at the dark sea outside the window.
Beyond that sea, there was Minato. There was Migiwa, and the smell of coffee, and Meiwa Rock, and the promise to wait for the most beautiful morning of the year.
Haruka was confused.
What did she want? She did not know.

That weekend, Haruka pushed on with the clearing.
She was trying to divert her tangled feelings by moving her body. She turned to the old chest of drawers in her grandmother's bedroom, which she had not yet touched.
She opened the drawers from the top. Clothing. Hand towels. Her grandmother's kimono, meticulously folded. All of it, like her grandmother, neatly arranged.
Only the bottom drawer would not open.
It was locked.
Haruka was surprised. That her grandmother had owned something worth locking. Her grandmother's life had seemed to hold no secrets. And yet this one drawer was locked.
Haruka searched for the key.
The key was in an unexpected place — in the back of the dried-goods drawer in the kitchen, beneath the bag of dried radish strips, a single small key was hidden. Her grandmother had hidden the key to her most precious thing in the place most her own.
With that key, Haruka opened the locked drawer.
Inside was an old wooden box.
When she opened the box, a bundle of letters came out.
Yellowed, old letters. There were dozens. All in the same hand, addressed to her grandmother — Sawako — under her maiden name. Which meant they were letters from before her grandmother married. And the sender's name was a man Haruka did not know.
Haruka opened a letter, gently.
It was a love letter.
A bundle of love letters, addressed to her grandmother when she was young. The man, it seemed, had been a fisherman. The letters were full of the sea. What fish were caught today. What color the sea was today. And, between these, a plain, direct feeling for Sawako.
The oldest letter was from when her grandmother was eighteen.
And the newest—
Haruka read its date and caught her breath.
A winter, in a certain year of the Shōwa era. The letter began like this:
"Sawako-san. The sea has turned rough, and I may not be able to see you for a while. But when spring comes, I will come for you. I promise. When the coldest winter clears, I will come to you first of all."
That letter was the last.
Haruka noticed that there was one more sheet of paper in the box.
It was an old newspaper clipping.
An article reporting a disaster at sea. In a winter sea, a fishing boat had capsized, and the fisherman aboard had died. The name of that fisherman — was the same as the name of the sender of the letters.
Haruka sank down where she stood.
Her grandmother had, before she married, loved someone. A fisherman. A man who promised, "When the coldest winter clears, I will come for you." But that man had died in the winter sea. Without keeping his promise.
Her grandmother had kept that letter, and that clipping, in a locked drawer, for more than sixty years.
Without telling a soul.
Haruka held the bundle of letters to her chest.
The tears would not stop.
She had known nothing of her grandmother. Kind, a good cook, devoted to her grandchild — that was the whole of the grandmother Haruka knew. But her grandmother had been a woman. A woman who had loved, and been loved, and had that love taken from her by a winter sea.
Haruka noticed a single photograph at the very bottom of the box.
Her grandmother, young, stood beside a young man.
The place was the Amaharashi coast.
Meiwa Rock was in the background. And beyond it, the snow-covered Tateyama Range, sharp and clear.
It was a photograph taken on a clear winter morning.
Her grandmother and the man, with the Tateyama beyond Meiwa Rock at their backs, were laughing, happily.
Haruka gazed at the photograph.
The winter Tateyama Range beyond Meiwa Rock.
The view Minato wanted to photograph. The coldest, most beautiful morning of the year.
Her grandmother had seen that view, more than sixty years ago, with the person she loved.
And this single photograph of that view she had kept in a locked drawer, all along. Even after she lost the one she loved.
Haruka gazed at the photograph.
And she thought of Minato.
Minato too was waiting for the Tateyama beyond Meiwa Rock. For a brother he had lost. For a reconciliation he never got.
Inside Haruka, something connected.
Her grandmother, and Minato. Different eras, different circumstances. And yet both of them waited, on and on, for the most beautiful morning of the year beyond Meiwa Rock, for someone they had lost.
And Haruka.
Haruka now held both of those longings in her hands.
Go back to Tokyo, or stay here.
The answer to that question was beginning, little by little, to take shape inside her.
Haruka set her grandmother's photograph, gently, beside the memorial portrait.
"Grandma," she said to it. "I've decided. I'll wait — with him — for the morning you waited for. So that this time, someone will come for us, properly."
Outside the window, the sound of the waves went on, quiet.


--------------------------------------------------------

CHAPTER EIGHT — A Misunderstanding in Autumn

When September came, the color of the sea shifted again, a little.
The bright blue of summer left, and the deep blue of autumn returned. The sky grew high, the wind dry. At some point the wind bell under Migiwa's eaves had been taken down.
Haruka had made up her mind.
She would not go back to Tokyo. She would stay in this town. She would wait for winter, with Minato. The promise of the morning her grandmother could not keep — this time she would keep it.
All that remained was to tell Minato. And to give Yoko her refusal.
But Haruka had done neither, yet.
She was afraid to tell Minato how she felt. To tell him, and find that his heart was still full of his brother, with no room for her — the thought kept her from taking that last step.
And she kept putting off her answer to Yoko, too. She had decided to refuse, and yet could not make the call. To make it would close, completely, the road to Tokyo. That last step, too, Haruka could not take.
And Haruka's suspended state gave birth to a misunderstanding.

One afternoon, Haruka was at Migiwa.
That day there were few customers. Matsuzo came, but left early. The other regulars did not come. The shop held only Haruka and Minato.
Haruka's phone rang.
It was from Yoko.
Haruka hesitated a moment. But she could not go on avoiding it forever. "Excuse me a moment," she said to Minato, and went outside.
On the beach, Haruka answered.
"Haruka, did you think it over?" Yoko's voice was bright with hope. "The office. I was hoping to hear from you soon."
Haruka meant to refuse.
But the words would not come out right.
"Yoko," she said. "Thank you for the offer. Really. But—"
"But?"
"I'm still thinking about it," Haruka said.
It was a lie. She was no longer thinking. She had decided to stay. And yet she said, "I'm still thinking about it." Why? Perhaps Yoko's voice, believing in her, was too welcome to cut off entirely.
"I see," Yoko said. "It's a big decision. Take your time. But, Haruka — I want to work with you again. Your talent — forgive me for saying so, but it's a waste out there. I really believe that."
"Thanks," Haruka said.
She hung up and went back inside.
When she came back, something about Minato was off.
He wore the same quiet face as ever. But that quiet was a different kind of quiet. Like a wall.
"Yuzuki-san?" Haruka called.
"Ah," Minato said, not looking at her, washing up. "Finished your call?"
"Yes."
Minato said nothing more.
Looking at his back, Haruka felt a foreboding.
Had he heard it — the call? The window had been open. Her voice on the beach might have carried into the shop. "Still thinking about it," "want to work with you again," "a waste" — heard only in fragments, it would sound as if Haruka were considering going back to Tokyo.
She tried to explain.
"Yuzuki-san, that call just now—"
"It's fine," Minato cut in. "It has nothing to do with me."
His voice was cold.
Haruka lost her words.
She had never heard Minato make a voice like that.
"Konno-san," Minato said, stopping his hands, though he still did not look at her. "You have your own life. In Tokyo there are people who need you, a place where your talent can live. That's a good thing. You don't have to be bound to this town."
"Yuzuki-san, that's not it," Haruka said. "I—"
"I," Minato cut in again, "said a selfish thing. About waiting for winter together. As if to tie you to this town. Forget it. That was my selfishness."
The words emptied Haruka's mind.
Forget it. The promise they had made before Meiwa Rock. The promise to wait, together, for the most beautiful morning of the year.
"Why," Haruka said, her voice shaking. "Why would you say that?"
Minato looked at her, for the first time.
His eyes were quiet. But behind that quiet something deep was sinking.
"I," Minato said slowly, "am afraid of losing, now."
Haruka started.
"Losing my brother," Minato said, "I faced that sea for two years. I finally began to be able to look forward again — because you came. But for that very reason, I'm afraid. To hope in you, to need you, and then to have you gone. This time I wouldn't recover. So it's better not to hope from the start. If you're going to go back to Tokyo someday, then before that, I want to have prepared my heart."
Haruka listened.
Minato's fear, she understood. The fear of losing someone you love. Never again to taste that loss. That fear had him bracing himself.
But, Haruka thought.
Minato was mistaken.
"I," Haruka said, "am not going back to Tokyo."
Minato looked at her.
"I'm staying in this town," Haruka said, clearly. "That call just now — it was to refuse. No — I couldn't refuse. I told a lie, that I was still thinking. But that was because I hadn't been able to tell you how I feel. I had no courage to tell you, and yet I was afraid to close the road to Tokyo, all by myself. I was afraid too — just like you."
Minato gazed at her.
"But now I understand," Haruka said. "Because I was afraid, you became afraid. Because I stayed suspended, it reached you. I'm sorry. I've already decided. I'll stay in this town. I'll wait for winter, with you. That's what I want."
Silence fell in the shop.
Outside the window, the sound of the waves.
Minato was quiet a long time.
Then, faintly, he said, "I'm sorry."
The cold was gone from his voice.
"I decided on my own," Minato said, "that you'd go back to Tokyo. Without even asking how you felt. The one who was afraid was me. Afraid of losing you, so I tried to push you away before I could lose you. I'm the worst."
"You're not the worst," Haruka said. "I was the same. Afraid, and couldn't tell you. We're even."
The two of them looked at each other.
And, from neither one first, laughed a little.
But in that laughter, an awkwardness still remained.
A misunderstanding, once born, is not wiped away completely even when words undo it. A thin film stayed between them.
From that day, Haruka and Minato went back to how they had been.
Haruka went to Migiwa every morning. Minato brewed coffee. On the surface, nothing changed.
But the intimacy of that summer night, when their feelings had been a single step from touching, did not come back. The two of them grew a little more careful with each other. Afraid to wound, afraid to be wounded, each unable to take that last step.
Autumn deepened.
Winter drew, little by little, nearer.
The coldest, most beautiful morning of the year was drawing, surely, nearer.
But whether the two of them could meet that morning together, Haruka had come no longer to know.


--------------------------------------------------------

CHAPTER NINE — Dusk at Shinminato

One Sunday in October, Haruka invited Minato to the market at Shinminato.
It took courage to ask. Since the day of the misunderstanding, that thin film had stayed between them, and Haruka had grown hesitant to invite Minato to anything personal. But she had made up her mind: this film had to be broken. And for that, there was something she wanted to show him.
Her grandmother's photograph.
"Would you go to the Shinminato market with me?" she said, one morning, at Migiwa. "The shiraebi, the red snow crab. My grandmother used to go often — Matsuzo-san told me. I wanted to see it. It's a little daunting on my own, so — if you'd come."
Minato looked a little surprised. Then he said, "All right. The shop's closed on Sundays anyway."
That afternoon, the two of them set off for Shinminato.
The Shinminato market was full of life. Fresh-caught fish were lined up on ice. Red snow crab. Shiraebi. Whelk. Barracuda. The market people called out in brisk, spirited voices. The bustle of it overwhelmed Haruka.
"Amazing," she said. "So much fish."
"They call Toyama Bay a natural fish tank," Minato said. "Deep sea and shallow sea, close together. So all kinds of fish are caught. It's been a rich sea since long ago."
They walked the market.
Minato knew the fish well. This one's good cooked like this. This shellfish is at its best now. To walk listening to his commentary was pleasant. The awkwardness that had lingered since the day of the misunderstanding began, little by little, to loosen.
Evening came.
They left the market and walked along an old canal called the Uchikawa.
Shinminato's Uchikawa was a place Haruka had never seen. Old rows of houses lined both banks of the canal, and fishing boats lay moored, quiet. The town of water, they call it. Several bridges spanned the canal, each with a grace of its own.
And dusk came.
The surface of the canal took the setting sun and turned gold. The old houses became silhouettes. The moored boats floated quiet on the golden water. It was a beautiful scene, somewhere out of stopped time.
"It's beautiful," Haruka said.
"Yes," Minato said. "This is the hour I like the Uchikawa best."
They stood on a bridge and watched the dusk.
Now, Haruka thought.
"Yuzuki-san," she said. "There's something I want to show you."
From her bag, she took a single photograph.
Her grandmother's. The young Sawako and the fisherman, on the Amaharashi coast, laughing with the Tateyama beyond Meiwa Rock at their backs.
Minato took it. He gazed, a long while.
"This is… Sawako-san?" he said. "When she was young."
"Yes," Haruka said. "It came out of my grandmother's locked drawer. The man beside her is someone she loved, before she married. A fisherman."
Haruka told Minato her grandmother's secret.
The love of more than sixty years ago. The promise — "When the coldest winter clears, I will come for you." The fisherman who died in a winter sea, leaving that promise behind. And her grandmother, who had kept the letter and the photograph in a locked drawer, all her life. Who had gone on looking, from the back seat of Migiwa, at the Tateyama beyond Meiwa Rock.
Minato listened, in silence.
"My grandmother," Haruka said, "was waiting, all along. For a promise that would never be kept. For more than sixty years. Looking at that view, from Migiwa's window. Just the way you photograph that sea every morning — she too kept looking at that view, for someone she had lost."
Minato gazed at the photograph.
His hand shook, a little.
"So that's why Sawako-san always looked at the sea from that seat," he said quietly.
"Yes."
Minato was quiet a while.
The setting sun turned the canal gold.
"I always thought," Minato said at last, "that waiting for someone you'd lost was a backward thing. The act of a weak person who can't move forward. That's why I was ashamed, somewhere, of shooting that sea every morning. How long am I going to keep this up, I'd think — my brother isn't coming back."
He looked at the young Sawako in the photograph.
"But Sawako-san waited more than sixty years," Minato said. "And it wasn't a backward thing. Sawako-san, while she waited, lived — properly. She married, raised a child, loved her grandchild, loved you. She made good food, drank coffee, looked at the sea. Waiting and living don't contradict each other."
Tears came to Haruka's eyes.
"I think so too," she said. "My grandmother never forgot that man. But not forgetting, and being unable to live the present, are different things. My grandmother waited for him, and still lived her present, properly. She waited for me too — a grandchild who never came — buying shiraebi every year. My grandmother was a master of waiting."
Minato handed the photograph back to her.
And looked at her.
"Konno-san," he said. "I was afraid. Of losing you. So I tried to push you away. But that was wrong. Sawako-san wasn't afraid to lose. She loved, and lost, and loved again. Her child, her grandchild. If you're so afraid of losing that you stop loving — that's the same as not being alive at all."
The setting sun lit the two of them gold.
"Let me say it again," Minato said. "The promise I made before Meiwa Rock. I'm sorry for saying 'forget it.' That was fear talking. The truth is, I never wanted to forget it."
He looked at her, straight.
"Won't you wait with me?" Minato said. "For the coldest, most beautiful morning of the year. No — not only that. After that morning, too. Spring, summer, autumn. Always — together."
Haruka's chest filled.
Tears ran down her cheeks.
"Yes," she said. "I'll wait. Always — together."
The sun was setting beyond the canal.
The last light wrapped the two of them.
Minato reached out his hand toward her.
Haruka took it.
Their hands joined. The feeling that had been a single step from touching on the summer night before the ships of light was, now, at last, one.
The last of the sun set beyond the canal.
Dusk wrapped everything.
But inside Haruka's chest, it was bright.
That small light that had begun to glow deep within her on the night of the firefly squid — now it was lit, clear.
The thin film was broken.
Between the two of them there was no longer hesitation, no longer fear.
There was only the sure promise that, from here on, they would wait for winter together.
On the way back, Haruka and Minato walked the dark canal, hand in hand.
"About Yoko-san," Minato asked. "Don't you need to answer her?"
"I will," Haruka said. "The moment I get home. I'll refuse, properly. My place is here. I have no hesitation left."
Minato held her hand a little tighter.
That night, home again, Haruka called Yoko.
And refused, clearly.
"I'm sorry, Yoko," she said. "I'm staying in this town. There's someone here I care about. And I've found work I can only do here. Your offer made me truly happy. But my place is here now."
Yoko sounded a little wistful. But in the end she laughed.
"I see," she said. "I'm glad you're happy. Your voice, Haruka — it's completely different from your Tokyo days. Brighter. Take care of that person."
"I will," Haruka said. "Thank you. And come visit, sometime. The sea is beautiful. And the shiraebi are delicious."
She hung up and looked at her grandmother's memorial portrait, and the young photograph beside it.
"Grandma," she said. "I've decided. The morning you waited for — this time I'll wait for it with someone who'll come for me, properly."
Outside, the autumn sea sounded, quiet.


--------------------------------------------------------

CHAPTER TEN — The Thunder That Wakes the Yellowtail

At the end of November, thunder rang for the first time.
It was the middle of the night. Haruka woke to the sound of it. Winter thunder was different from summer thunder. Where summer thunder cracks high in the sky, winter thunder rolled up from the direction of the sea, low, striking deep in the belly. Lightning, through the gap in the shutters, lit the room pale blue.
The next morning, at Migiwa, Matsuzo was in better spirits than usual.
"Buri-okoshi," he said. "The thunder last night. When that rings, it's kanburi season. The winter yellowtail. The sea's turning to winter, at last."
"Buri-okoshi?" Haruka asked.
"The thunder at the start of winter — around here, that's what we call it," Minato explained, brewing coffee. "When it rings, the kanburi start to be caught. The old people believed the thunder woke the yellowtail sleeping on the sea floor. So — the yellowtail-waking."
Haruka was struck by the beauty of the phrase.
Buri-okoshi. The winter thunder wakes the yellowtail asleep at the bottom of the sea. What a rich imagination. The people of this town had given each thing of nature a story like this.
"Himi's kanburi is the best in Japan," Matsuzo said, chest out. "The fat's on it. Eat that, and only then does it feel like winter's come."
On the way home that day, Haruka bought fillets of kanburi at the harbor's direct-sale stall.
And that night, she invited Minato to her grandmother's house.
It was the first time Minato had come. Haruka was a little nervous. Her grandmother's house was, to Haruka, the most precious place there was. To invite Minato there meant showing him her softest place.
Minato paused in the entrance.
"Excuse me," he said quietly. "Sawako-san's house."
"Yes," Haruka said.
Minato looked slowly around the house.
The low table in the living room. The handwritten calendar on the wall. And the memorial portrait, and the young photograph beside it. Minato stopped before the photograph, and bowed, deeply.
"Sawako-san," he said to it. "I'll take good care of your granddaughter."
Haruka's chest went warm.
That night, Haruka cooked with the kanburi.
First, buri-daikon. She tried to recall her grandmother's method. Draw the kombu stock, parboil the daikon. Blanch the yellowtail scraps. Simmer, slowly, with soy sauce, mirin, sake. A sweet-savory, good smell spread through the kitchen.
And buri-shabu. Thin slices of kanburi sashimi, swished briefly through a pot of kombu stock. The fatty flesh turned, softly, white in the broth. Eaten with ponzu. In the mouth, the fat of the kanburi melted, all at once.
"It's good," Minato said. "Really good."
"Still far from my grandmother's taste, though," Haruka said.
"No," Minato said, shaking his head. "This is your taste. Your taste, inherited from Sawako-san. It's more than good enough."
The two of them, around her grandmother's low table, ate the kanburi.
Outside, the buri-okoshi thunder rang again, far off.
To the sound of it, they sat around a warm pot. It was the happiest night of Haruka's life. Happier than any glittering night in Tokyo. This one — in this old, small house of her grandmother's, sharing a pot of kanburi with Minato.
"Yuzuki-san," Haruka said. "May I call you Minato?"
Minato looked a little surprised. Then he smiled.
"Of course," he said. "May I call you Haruka?"
"Yes," Haruka said.
"Haruka," Minato said, trying the name.
"Minato," Haruka said, trying his.
They looked at each other, and laughed.
Just calling each other by name brought them so much closer. The last thin wall between them vanished, now.
That night, Minato stayed late.
They talked of many things.
Minato spoke of his brother. Childhood memories. How, though they quarreled constantly, he had truly respected him. The guilt of never becoming a man of the sea, as his brother was.
Haruka spoke of her Tokyo days. The seven years of wearing down. The work that vanished with one line — "not quite what we had in mind." The dream of her grandmother's kitchen on the night of the fever. And the funeral she had not attended.
They told each other their most painful places.
Telling them did not make the pain vanish. But by telling, they no longer had to carry it alone. They could share it, between the two of them.
"Minato," Haruka said. "I think — my grandmother called me to this town. To bring me to you."
Minato looked at her.
"I've thought the same," he said. "Sawako-san sent you to me. To set my stopped time moving again."
Outside, the buri-okoshi thunder rang once more.
It was the thunder that wakes the yellowtail sleeping on the sea floor.
And that thunder, Haruka thought, might be the thunder that had woken Minato's stopped time, and her own frozen heart.
Winter was about to come.
The coldest, most beautiful morning of the year drew near.
The two of them were waiting for that morning, together.
There was no more fear.
There was only a quiet anticipation for that morning's coming.
When Minato was leaving, at the entrance, Haruka said, "Minato. When the most beautiful morning of the year comes—"
"Yes," Minato said.
"When you get the photograph," Haruka said, "when you go to lay it before your brother's grave — may I come too?"
Minato looked surprised.
Then his eyes filled with tears.
"Yes," he said, his voice shaking. "Come with me. I want to introduce you to my brother."
Haruka smiled.
"Yes," she said.
Minato went home.
Haruka saw off his back from the entrance. His back vanished into the dark at the bottom of the hill.
She went inside and looked at her grandmother's portrait, and the young photograph beside it.
"Grandma," she said. "Thank you. For calling me here."
Outside, the winter sea sounded.
And on its floor, the yellowtail were waking.

CHAPTER ELEVEN — The Forecast of Snow

When December came, the snow began to fall.
Toyama's snow was heavy. Different from the dry, fluttering snow that fell now and then in Tokyo. Damp snow, full of water, piled up soundlessly. In a single night, the town was buried white.
The sea, seen from Migiwa's window, changed too.
The sky filled with grey cloud. The sea, reflecting the grey, heaved dark. Meiwa Rock, capped with snow, turned white. And the Tateyama Range beyond it — vanished.
In winter, the Tateyama Range was almost never visible.
"What?" Haruka was surprised. "You can't see it in winter? In the coldest season of the year?"
"Winter's mostly cloudy," Minato said. "On the Japan Sea side there are few clear days. Cloud hides the mountains. So the morning when the whole Tateyama Range shows sharp beyond Meiwa Rock — even in winter, it almost never comes."
For the first time, Haruka understood how rare the view Minato wanted to photograph was.
The coldest, windless, clear morning of the year. Air so pure it froze, the sea gone perfectly still, the whole Tateyama Range visible beyond Meiwa Rock. It was a miracle of a morning, one that came on perhaps a handful of days in a whole winter.
That was why, in two years of shooting every morning, Minato had not yet taken it.
"Will it come this year?" Haruka asked, uneasy.
"I don't know," Minato said. "Some years it doesn't come. But there's nothing to do but wait. That's the winter of this land."
Every morning, Haruka and Minato waited.
Every morning, before dawn, they stood on the beach and looked beyond Meiwa Rock.
And every morning, the sky was clouded. Snow fell. Grey cloud hid the Tateyama Range.
A week passed.
Two weeks passed.
Not once did the Tateyama Range show.

Then one day, a letter came for Minato.
From Tokyo.
It was from the photographer who had been Minato's teacher in his student days. A famous name in the world of news photography. The man Minato most respected.
The letter said this:
He had been asked to judge a major overseas photography prize. He wished to recommend a young, promising photographer. He had remembered Minato's sea photographs — the ones Minato had shown him before he left Tokyo. Those he could not forget. If Minato had the will, he should send the photographs he was taking now. It was a chance to face the world.
Minato showed the letter to Haruka.
Reading it, her chest stirred.
This was a great chance for Minato. A road to being a photographer who faced the world. The road Minato had once dreamed of. The one he could not give up, even after his brother said, "You're running away."
"Minato," Haruka said. "This is an incredible thing, isn't it?"
"I don't know," Minato said. "I've left the world of news photography. Now I only shoot this sea."
"But," Haruka said. "The photograph you want — the most beautiful morning of the year. If you get it, you could send that too, couldn't you."
Minato looked at her.
"Haruka," he said. "Are you telling me to go back to Tokyo?"
Haruka started.
She realized she was, at that moment, urging Minato to leave.
That autumn misunderstanding. Minato, afraid of losing her, had tried to push her away. Now she understood his feeling. The fear of sending away someone you love. The fear of letting go.
But, Haruka thought.
Her grandmother had not been afraid.
Had not been afraid to lose the one she loved. Had loved, and lost, and loved again.
And Haruka remembered. Her own talent, worn down in Tokyo. The work that vanished with one line — "not quite what we had in mind." No one had believed in her talent. Only Yoko had said "a waste."
Haruka did not want Minato to feel the same.
She did not want to shut Minato's talent up in this small seaside shop. If Minato had the talent to become a photographer who faced the world, then to make him give it up for her sake was wrong.
"Minato," Haruka said. "I don't want you to go. Truly. I want you to stay in this town, always. To brew coffee in this shop, and live with me. But—"
Minato gazed at her.
"But I don't want to shut your talent away," Haruka said. "What wore me down in Tokyo was that no one believed in my talent. I believe in yours. So if you want to face the world — if you want to take that road — I'll send you off. The courage my grandmother couldn't find, I want to have."
Tears stood in her eyes.
As she spoke, her chest felt as if it would tear. She didn't want Minato to go — that was her true heart. But even crushing that true heart, Haruka wanted to believe in Minato's talent. That, she thought, was love. Not to bind the other to your side, but to send them off to the place where they can shine brightest. That.
Minato was quiet a while.
Then, quietly, he said, "Haruka. Thank you. That you believe in my talent. That you'd even send me off. How happy that makes me."
He took her hand.
"But," Minato said. "I'm not going to Tokyo."
Haruka looked at him.
"I'm grateful to my teacher," Minato said. "For remembering me. For trying to give me a chance. But what I want to photograph isn't a picture the world will praise. What I want to photograph is this sea. The Tateyama beyond Meiwa Rock. The sea my brother last saw. I don't shoot it for a prize. I shoot it for my brother. And now — I shoot it with you."
He held her hand tight.
"My place is here," Minato said. "Just as you refused Tokyo and chose this place, I choose too. This sea, this shop, and you. I don't need the world. As long as I have the morning beyond Meiwa Rock, and you — that's enough."
Tears spilled from Haruka's eyes.
This time, they were not tears of sorrow.
Minato drew her close.
Haruka buried her face in his chest.
His body was warm. It smelled of coffee. It smelled of the sea, too. In that smell, Haruka closed her eyes.
For a while, they stayed like that.
Outside, the snow fell, quiet.
That night, Minato wrote his reply to his teacher.
Words of thanks, and words of refusal.
"I will stay by this sea. The photograph I want to take is not for a prize, but to offer to a single person. If someday I take it, I will show it to you. Not for a prize, but only to be seen."
After he had posted the letter, Minato said to Haruka, "Now my hesitation is gone too. All that's left is to wait for that morning."
The next morning.
Matsuzo came bursting into Migiwa.
"Minato!" Matsuzo was excited. "Day after tomorrow. Day after tomorrow morning, it'll clear."
"What?" Minato looked up.
"The color of the sky this morning, and the smell of the wind," Matsuzo said. "Tomorrow night, the coldest air of the winter comes in. Radiative cooling — the morning after next will freeze. And the wind will drop dead still. That morning will be the coldest, and the clearest, of this whole winter. The morning you've been waiting for."
Minato and Haruka looked at each other.
The morning after next.
The coldest, most beautiful morning of the year.
At last — it was coming.


--------------------------------------------------------

CHAPTER TWELVE — The Winter Daybreak

That morning, Haruka woke before the day had broken.
She needed no alarm. Her body woke of its own accord. As if her whole body had known this morning was coming.
She opened the shutters, just a little.
Outside, it was still dark.
But the air was different.
Frozen air poured in through the gap. A sharpness she had never known. A cold that seemed to freeze the depths of her lungs. And that cold was, endlessly, clear. A dry cold, with not a single cloud.
She looked up at the sky.
The stars were out.
It was the first full sky of stars she had seen that winter. All winter the sky had been covered with cloud. This morning, that cloud was gone without a trace. The whole sky was scattered with stars, twinkling as if frozen in place.
Matsuzo was right.
This morning, it would clear.
Haruka put on her warmest clothes. She threw on her grandmother's old shawl, too. And she left the house.
Outside, snow had piled. But it was not the damp snow of the days before. Frozen, it glittered, reflecting starlight. With each step Haruka took, the snow gave a dry sound — kyu, kyu.
She went down the hill.
On the beach, Minato was there.
He had already set his tripod, camera raised. Beside him, Matsuzo stood too, bundled against the cold, gazing out beyond the sea.
"Haruka," Minato said, noticing her.
His voice shook. Not from the cold alone.
"I've come," Haruka said, standing beside him.
The sky was still dark.
But the eastern sky, near the horizon, was beginning, faintly, to brighten. The indigo sky shifted, little by little — indigo to navy, navy to ultramarine.
And beyond the sea—
The Tateyama Range showed.
Still dark.
But certainly there. Beyond the black silhouette of Meiwa Rock. Against the indigo sky, the snow-covered range stood, still.
Haruka caught her breath.
For the first time since winter came, she saw the Tateyama Range.
Two weeks of waiting. Every morning hidden by cloud. This morning, sharp and clear, it was there.
"You can see it," Haruka whispered. "Minato, you can see it. Tateyama."
"Yes," Minato said. "You can see it. So clearly."
The sky brightened, little by little.
Ultramarine to indigo to blue. Blue to pale blue. And near the horizon, it began to flush rose.
The light began to touch the Tateyama Range.
First the highest point caught it. The sharp peak of Tsurugi, taking the morning sun, flushed faintly rose. Then the light came, little by little, down the mountains. Dainichi. Onanji. Yakushi. One peak and then another, the snow flushed rose.
The sea was still dark.
Still.
The wind had dropped, dead still.
The surface of the sea, like a mirror, lay quiet. And on that mirror, the rose-flushed Tateyama Range was reflected, upside down. In the sky, the true Tateyama. In the sea, the inverted Tateyama. Two ranges, facing each other across Meiwa Rock.
"Minato," Haruka whispered. "The mountains — reflected in the sea."
Minato did not answer.
Peering into the viewfinder, he did not stir.
Before long, the sun rose.
From the hills at Haruka's back, the sun showed its face.
And its light struck the Tateyama Range head-on.
The rose-flushed peaks blazed, all at once, gold.
The snow-covered mountains, taking the morning sun, shone blinding. And that light reflected on the mirror of the sea. The sea set countless golden ripples glittering. Meiwa Rock stood quiet on that golden sea.
It was a scene not of this world.
Haruka's tears would not stop.
Never in her life had she seen anything so beautiful. Sea, and mountains, and light. All of it become one — a morning. A morning that would never come again, and yet, now, was certainly there, like a miracle.
Minato's words came back to her.
A morning so beautiful it hurts.
This was it, exactly.
Minato's shutter fired.
Kasha.
Once.
And that was all. He did not fire it again.
Minato lifted his face from the camera.
And with his own two eyes, not the viewfinder, he looked at the scene.
Tears were spilling from Minato's eyes.
"Brother," he whispered, toward the Tateyama Range across the sea. "Are you watching? This is the morning you wanted, to the last, to see. I finally took it. It took two years. Sorry — for that time. When you said I was running, and I threw it back — 'all you know is the sea.' You were seeing something this beautiful, every day. The one who was running was me."
Haruka took his hand.
Minato closed his around hers.
"Brother," he went on. "Let me introduce her. This is Haruka. Sawako-san's granddaughter. The person I care about. She's the one who let me live again. I took this photograph with her."
Beyond the sea, the Tateyama Range shone gold.
The light seemed to answer his words.
Matsuzo, quietly, slipped away.
He was leaving the two of them alone.
Haruka and Minato stood side by side, looking at the sea and the mountains shining gold.
"Minato," Haruka said. "My grandmother saw this morning too. Sixty years ago. With the one she loved."
"Yes," Minato said. "The same morning, surely. And now, we're seeing it."
Haruka took her grandmother's photograph from her bag.
The young Sawako and the fisherman, laughing with the Tateyama beyond Meiwa Rock at their backs.
She held the photograph up against the scene before her.
The Tateyama beyond Meiwa Rock, sixty years ago. The Tateyama beyond Meiwa Rock, now. Across time, the same view was there.
"Grandma," she said to the photograph. "Look. It's the same morning. The morning you saw with him. I'm seeing it now, with someone I care about."
The wind blew.
Frozen, clear wind.
It dried the tears on Haruka's cheeks.
Minato turned to her.
"Haruka," he said.
"Yes," Haruka said.
"I love you," Minato said. Straight. "Two years ago, when I lost my brother, my time stopped. Every morning, shooting this sea, I lived like a dead man. But you came. You set my stopped time moving again. I want to live with you. In this town by the sea. Always."
Tears welled again in Haruka's eyes.
"So do I," she said. "I love you. I came back to this town worn down in Tokyo, a hollow shell. I expected nothing. Only to clear out my grandmother's house. But I met you. Drank Migiwa's coffee. Saw the firefly squid. And I took back the warmth of living, once more. I want to live with you. Here. Always."
Minato drew her close.
The two of them, with the sea and mountains shining gold at their backs, held each other.
And kissed.
It was a frozen, cold morning.
But their lips were warm.
Beyond Meiwa Rock, the Tateyama Range shone in the morning sun, on and on.
It was a morning when the rain had cleared.
A morning when the rain, waited for two years — no, sixty years — had, at last, cleared.
The single photograph Minato took that morning—
The Tateyama Range shining gold beyond Meiwa Rock. The mirror sea. And, gazing at that view, Haruka's tear-wet profile.
Minato gave the photograph a title.
"Amaharashi."
The rain clears.
That was the title of the photograph.


--------------------------------------------------------

EPILOGUE — And Again, the Rain Clears

A year passed after that.
Migiwa changed, a little.
On the wall by the seat at the very back of the counter, near the window — the seat where her grandmother had once sat — a single photograph came to be hung.
"Amaharashi."
The Tateyama Range shining gold beyond Meiwa Rock. The mirror sea. And, gazing at that view, Haruka's profile. The single photograph Minato had taken on that winter daybreak.
Customers who came to the shop all stopped before it.
"Where's this?" they would ask.
"Just out there," Minato would answer, pointing beyond the window. "Beyond Meiwa Rock. On the coldest mornings of winter, once in a while, you can see it like this."
"Well," the customer would say, impressed. "A view like that, right there. I never knew."
That photograph, Minato sent to his teacher as well.
Not for a prize — only to be seen.
A short reply came from the teacher.
"A fine photograph. This is not a photograph that wins prizes. It is something better. This is love."
Minato showed the letter to Haruka, and smiled, a little shyly.

After that winter morning, Haruka and Minato visited the grave of Minato's brother.
Haruka met him for the first time. The young fisherman in the photograph, sun-browned, smiling. He resembled Minato, a little. But his face was far bolder, far more open, than Minato's.
"How do you do," Haruka said before the grave, bowing. "I'm Konno Haruka. I'll live my life with Minato."
Minato laid the "Amaharashi" photograph before the grave.
"Brother," he said. "The photograph I promised. The morning you wanted, to the last, to see. I finally took it. I'm not running anymore. I'll live by this sea. Watch over us."
The wind blew.
The branches of the trees in the graveyard rang.
It was as if his brother had answered.

Haruka lives on in her grandmother's house.
The photograph and letters that came from the locked drawer, she keeps with care.
And she has decided: someday she will find the grave of the man who sent those letters, and lay her grandmother's photograph before it.
The man who promised, "When the coldest winter clears, I will come for you." The man who could not keep it.
She wants to tell him.
Sawako-san never forgot you. For more than sixty years she waited for you. And she never forgot that morning, the one she saw with you, the Tateyama beyond Meiwa Rock. So there is nothing left to regret. Sawako-san lived fully, for your share as well. And her granddaughter has seen your morning, again.

Haruka came to work at Migiwa.
Though brewing the coffee remained, as ever, Minato's task. Haruka helped run the shop. And she added, to it, one small new thing.
Shiraebi fritters.
Miso soup drawn from kombu stock.
The tastes of Toyama she had inherited from her grandmother, Haruka added to Migiwa's small menu.
When a customer who came in the morning for coffee said, "I'm hungry," Haruka would fry shiraebi fritters. Crisp, sweet jewels of the sea. She would dip them in the kombu-stock broth and set them out.
"Good," the customer would say. "These fritters — they taste like Sawako-san's."
That was the finest praise Haruka could receive.

Matsuzo came to Migiwa every morning, as ever.
He would sit at the very front, look first at the sea, and say something about the sea that day.
"Calm today." "West wind's in. It'll turn rough by afternoon."
And now and then, looking at the "Amaharashi" photograph, he would say:
"A fine photograph. Sawako-san saw that morning too. Sixty years ago. She too looked at those mountains from this shop. And round and round it goes — comes to this."
Haruka nodded at his words.
Round and round it goes.
Sixty years ago, her grandmother had seen the Tateyama beyond Meiwa Rock with the one she loved. That view her grandmother had kept looking at for sixty years, for the one she lost. And her granddaughter had come back to this town, and seen the same view, with the one she loved.
Round and round it goes.
Across time, longing carries on.
In this small town by the sea.

That winter, the coldest morning of the year came again.
Matsuzo said, "Tomorrow morning, it'll clear."
And the next morning, Haruka and Minato stood on the beach again.
Beyond Meiwa Rock, the Tateyama Range showed, sharp and clear.
The sun rose, and the snow peaks shone gold. On the mirror sea, the view was reflected, upside down.
It was the same morning as the year before.
And yet, a different morning too.
This year, Minato had not brought his camera.
"You're not shooting?" Haruka asked.
"This year, I'll just look," Minato said. "With you. Not through a lens — with these eyes. To see this morning that will never come again, with you — that matters more."
Haruka smiled.
And she took Minato's hand.
On the ring finger of Minato's left hand, a ring gleamed. On the ring finger of Haruka's left hand, the same ring gleamed. In spring, the two of them had become husband and wife.
"Minato," Haruka said.
"Yes," Minato said.
"I'm happy," Haruka said.
"So am I," Minato said.
Beyond Meiwa Rock, the Tateyama Range shone gold.
The sea was still. The wind had dropped. It was a frozen, clear morning.
But their joined hands were warm.
It was a morning when the rain had cleared.
And, from here on, however many times, the rain will clear again.
In this town by the sea.
As long as the two of them stand side by side, and wait for the morning.


THE END
