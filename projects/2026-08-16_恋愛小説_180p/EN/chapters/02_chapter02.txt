CHAPTER TWO — The Coffee Shop Called Migiwa

After that, Haruka began going to Migiwa every morning.
"Going," though, was too grand a word. She would simply wake, open the storm shutters, look at the sea — and her feet would turn that way of their own accord. There was still no equipment in her grandmother's house for making coffee. She knew, herself, that she was using this as an excuse.
Minato was always there before her.
By the time Haruka arrived, the shop's window would already be open toward the sea, and behind the counter came the sound of coffee beans being ground. It was a hand mill. The low, grinding rhythm of it rang out steadily in the morning quiet.
"Good morning," Haruka would say.
And Minato, without stopping his grinding, would answer only, "Morning." Somewhere along the way, the politeness had dropped out of his speech. It tickled her, a little.
Migiwa was a strange shop.
There was no menu. Nothing with a price on it was posted, on the walls or the tables. When a customer came, Minato did not ask, "What will you have today?" He simply brewed coffee with the best bean he had that day. Some customers, Haruka thought, would find that irritating. But the customers who came to this shop, it seemed, were not that kind of customer.
The regulars were all elderly.
The first to arrive each morning was an old man named Matsuzo. Over eighty now, he said. In his youth he had been a fixed-net fisherman, and his whole face was burned by sun and salt, cut with deep wrinkles. Matsuzo always sat at the very front of the counter, and before touching his coffee he would gaze at the sea outside the window. Then, quietly, he would say something about the sea that day.
"Calm today," he might say. Or, "West wind's come in. It'll turn rough by afternoon."
Minato would answer only, "That so."
Haruka loved that exchange. In Matsuzo's words were seventy years of the sea. More surely than any forecast, Matsuzo named the sea of the next day.
"You're Sawako-san's granddaughter, aren't you," Matsuzo said to her on the third day.
"Yes," Haruka nodded.
"You resemble her," Matsuzo said, looking her over. "Around the eyes. Sawako had eyes like that too, when she was young."
"You knew my grandmother? When she was young?"
"Same age, we were," Matsuzo said, and his wrinkled face smiled a little. "Went to the same primary school. That woman — she had a daughter who married off to Tokyo, and every year she looked forward, so much, to that daughter coming home in summer with a grandchild."
Realizing that the grandchild was herself, Haruka's words caught.
"But that grandchild, too, once she grew up, stopped coming home," Matsuzo went on, as if to himself, still looking at the sea. "And even so, every year when summer came, Sawako bought shiraebi. Because the grandchild liked them, she said. Even though the grandchild never came."
Something sharp ran through Haruka's chest.
Shiraebi. The white shrimp. She remembered them — the shiraebi fritters she had eaten at her grandmother's house as a child, sweet and crisp, dissolving in the mouth. Every year, her grandmother had gone on buying them for a grandchild who never came.
"Matsuzo-san," Minato cut in, quietly. "That's enough of that."
Matsuzo shut his mouth as if startled. "Sorry," he said, not looking at Haruka. "Bad habit of an old man. Nothing but old stories."
"No," Haruka said, shaking her head. Her voice would not come out right. "…Thank you. For telling me about her."
That day, even when she had finished her coffee, Haruka could not rise from her seat for a while.
Matsuzo left, and the other regulars, and the shop was left with only Haruka and Minato. Behind the counter, Minato was washing up. The sound of the water rang quietly.
"Matsuzo-san means no harm," Minato said, in a gap between the water. "He's just a man who says what he thinks, as he thinks it."
"I know," Haruka said. "It only hurt more because it's true."
Minato stopped washing.
Haruka looked at the sea outside the window. Meiwa Rock stood quiet in the morning light.
"I didn't go home for my grandmother's funeral," she said.
The words she had not meant to speak came out. "There was work. An important presentation. But the presentation didn't go through. What I'd made — no one even glanced at it. So I quit the company too. My grandmother's funeral, and the work that mattered — I lost both, and came here."
Minato said nothing.
He only set a new cup of coffee in front of her. A coffee she had not ordered.
"This," Minato said, "is the bean Sawako-san liked."
Haruka looked at the cup.
"Every year, when summer came, Sawako-san ordered a slightly finer bean," Minato said. "Because she wanted to serve it to her grandchild, when the grandchild came. So she could say her coffee was better than the cafés in Tokyo — she said she practiced with a good bean, to be ready."
Tears spilled from Haruka's eyes.
However she tried to stop them, they would not stop. They fell onto the counter, drop by drop. Haruka wiped them with the back of her hand. Wipe as she might, more came, and more. In seven years — in Tokyo meeting rooms, on packed trains, in her lonely apartment — she had never once cried. Could not. And now, in this shop by the sea, before a coffee she had not even ordered, it came out as though a dam had broken.
Minato, seeing it, did not panic.
He only waited, quietly, for Haruka to cry herself out. Beyond the window the waves came and went. A gull cried. Far off, a fishing boat passed, trailing a white wake.
"Drink it slowly," Minato said, when she had calmed a little, and withdrew to the back of the shop. He was leaving her alone, Haruka understood. And she was grateful.
She drank the coffee her grandmother had loved, slowly.
It was bitter, and yet, behind the bitterness, the taste she had drunk long ago was certainly there. And now she could remember what taste it was. It was the taste of her grandmother's kitchen. In that morning kitchen that smelled of kombu stock, on special days, her grandmother had brewed her real coffee — not instant — just for her.
Her grandmother, Haruka understood, had kept this taste alive. For a grandchild who never came.
That day, for the first time, Haruka stayed a long time in the shop. Until nearly noon, she passed the time watching the sea outside the window. Minato came out now and then, ground beans, tended to the regulars. He left her be. And yet she did not feel left alone. It felt more like being, quietly, together in the same space.
On her way out, Haruka asked, "Why did you take on this shop?"
Minato paused before answering.
"It was my grandfather's shop," he said. "I was in Tokyo, studying photography. I wanted to be a news photographer. But — a number of things happened, and I came back, and took over the shop."
"A number of things."
"Yes." Minato said no more.
And Haruka asked no more.
Just as she could not yet tell anyone, in detail, about the funeral she had not attended, so Minato must have "a number of things" he could not speak of. Everyone carries something they cannot say aloud, and still, every morning, wakes, brews coffee, looks at the sea, and lives.
When she stepped outside, the sky had begun to cloud.
"It'll turn rough in the afternoon," Haruka said, remembering Matsuzo's words.
"Yes," Minato said, looking up at the sky. "If Matsuzo-san says so, it will. His forecasts come true."
That afternoon, the sea did turn rough.
From the window of her grandmother's house, Haruka watched the sea whiten with waves. The sea that had been calm only that morning now heaved like a different living thing. White spray struck Meiwa Rock again and again.
The sea changes its face so much, she thought.
She remembered the Tokyo scenery. That town's scenery had always been the same. Rain or shine, buildings were buildings, roads were roads, people were people. The scenery did not change.
Here, the sea wore a different face every day.
And that day, for the first time, Haruka thought that living in this town, a little, might not be so bad.
