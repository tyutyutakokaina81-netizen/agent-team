EPILOGUE — And Again, the Rain Clears

A year passed after that.
Migiwa changed, a little.
On the wall by the seat at the very back of the counter, near the window — the seat where her grandmother had once sat — a single photograph came to be hung.
"Amaharashi."
The Tateyama Range shining gold beyond Meiwa Rock. The mirror sea. And, gazing at that view, Haruka's profile. The single photograph Minato had taken on that winter daybreak.
Customers who came to the shop all stopped before it.
"Where's this?" they would ask.
"Just out there," Minato would answer, pointing beyond the window. "Beyond Meiwa Rock. On the coldest mornings of winter, once in a while, you can see it like this."
"Well," the customer would say, impressed. "A view like that, right there. I never knew."
That photograph, Minato sent to his teacher as well.
Not for a prize — only to be seen.
A short reply came from the teacher.
"A fine photograph. This is not a photograph that wins prizes. It is something better. This is love."
Minato showed the letter to Haruka, and smiled, a little shyly.

After that winter morning, Haruka and Minato visited the grave of Minato's brother.
Haruka met him for the first time. The young fisherman in the photograph, sun-browned, smiling. He resembled Minato, a little. But his face was far bolder, far more open, than Minato's.
"How do you do," Haruka said before the grave, bowing. "I'm Konno Haruka. I'll live my life with Minato."
Minato laid the "Amaharashi" photograph before the grave.
"Brother," he said. "The photograph I promised. The morning you wanted, to the last, to see. I finally took it. I'm not running anymore. I'll live by this sea. Watch over us."
The wind blew.
The branches of the trees in the graveyard rang.
It was as if his brother had answered.

Haruka lives on in her grandmother's house.
The photograph and letters that came from the locked drawer, she keeps with care.
And she has decided: someday she will find the grave of the man who sent those letters, and lay her grandmother's photograph before it.
The man who promised, "When the coldest winter clears, I will come for you." The man who could not keep it.
She wants to tell him.
Sawako-san never forgot you. For more than sixty years she waited for you. And she never forgot that morning, the one she saw with you, the Tateyama beyond Meiwa Rock. So there is nothing left to regret. Sawako-san lived fully, for your share as well. And her granddaughter has seen your morning, again.

Haruka came to work at Migiwa.
Though brewing the coffee remained, as ever, Minato's task. Haruka helped run the shop. And she added, to it, one small new thing.
Shiraebi fritters.
Miso soup drawn from kombu stock.
The tastes of Toyama she had inherited from her grandmother, Haruka added to Migiwa's small menu.
When a customer who came in the morning for coffee said, "I'm hungry," Haruka would fry shiraebi fritters. Crisp, sweet jewels of the sea. She would dip them in the kombu-stock broth and set them out.
"Good," the customer would say. "These fritters — they taste like Sawako-san's."
That was the finest praise Haruka could receive.

Matsuzo came to Migiwa every morning, as ever.
He would sit at the very front, look first at the sea, and say something about the sea that day.
"Calm today." "West wind's in. It'll turn rough by afternoon."
And now and then, looking at the "Amaharashi" photograph, he would say:
"A fine photograph. Sawako-san saw that morning too. Sixty years ago. She too looked at those mountains from this shop. And round and round it goes — comes to this."
Haruka nodded at his words.
Round and round it goes.
Sixty years ago, her grandmother had seen the Tateyama beyond Meiwa Rock with the one she loved. That view her grandmother had kept looking at for sixty years, for the one she lost. And her granddaughter had come back to this town, and seen the same view, with the one she loved.
Round and round it goes.
Across time, longing carries on.
In this small town by the sea.

That winter, the coldest morning of the year came again.
Matsuzo said, "Tomorrow morning, it'll clear."
And the next morning, Haruka and Minato stood on the beach again.
Beyond Meiwa Rock, the Tateyama Range showed, sharp and clear.
The sun rose, and the snow peaks shone gold. On the mirror sea, the view was reflected, upside down.
It was the same morning as the year before.
And yet, a different morning too.
This year, Minato had not brought his camera.
"You're not shooting?" Haruka asked.
"This year, I'll just look," Minato said. "With you. Not through a lens — with these eyes. To see this morning that will never come again, with you — that matters more."
Haruka smiled.
And she took Minato's hand.
On the ring finger of Minato's left hand, a ring gleamed. On the ring finger of Haruka's left hand, the same ring gleamed. In spring, the two of them had become husband and wife.
"Minato," Haruka said.
"Yes," Minato said.
"I'm happy," Haruka said.
"So am I," Minato said.
Beyond Meiwa Rock, the Tateyama Range shone gold.
The sea was still. The wind had dropped. It was a frozen, clear morning.
But their joined hands were warm.
It was a morning when the rain had cleared.
And, from here on, however many times, the rain will clear again.
In this town by the sea.
As long as the two of them stand side by side, and wait for the morning.


THE END
