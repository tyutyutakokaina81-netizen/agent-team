CHAPTER EIGHT — A Misunderstanding in Autumn

When September came, the color of the sea shifted again, a little.
The bright blue of summer left, and the deep blue of autumn returned. The sky grew high, the wind dry. At some point the wind bell under Migiwa's eaves had been taken down.
Haruka had made up her mind.
She would not go back to Tokyo. She would stay in this town. She would wait for winter, with Minato. The promise of the morning her grandmother could not keep — this time she would keep it.
All that remained was to tell Minato. And to give Yoko her refusal.
But Haruka had done neither, yet.
She was afraid to tell Minato how she felt. To tell him, and find that his heart was still full of his brother, with no room for her — the thought kept her from taking that last step.
And she kept putting off her answer to Yoko, too. She had decided to refuse, and yet could not make the call. To make it would close, completely, the road to Tokyo. That last step, too, Haruka could not take.
And Haruka's suspended state gave birth to a misunderstanding.

One afternoon, Haruka was at Migiwa.
That day there were few customers. Matsuzo came, but left early. The other regulars did not come. The shop held only Haruka and Minato.
Haruka's phone rang.
It was from Yoko.
Haruka hesitated a moment. But she could not go on avoiding it forever. "Excuse me a moment," she said to Minato, and went outside.
On the beach, Haruka answered.
"Haruka, did you think it over?" Yoko's voice was bright with hope. "The office. I was hoping to hear from you soon."
Haruka meant to refuse.
But the words would not come out right.
"Yoko," she said. "Thank you for the offer. Really. But—"
"But?"
"I'm still thinking about it," Haruka said.
It was a lie. She was no longer thinking. She had decided to stay. And yet she said, "I'm still thinking about it." Why? Perhaps Yoko's voice, believing in her, was too welcome to cut off entirely.
"I see," Yoko said. "It's a big decision. Take your time. But, Haruka — I want to work with you again. Your talent — forgive me for saying so, but it's a waste out there. I really believe that."
"Thanks," Haruka said.
She hung up and went back inside.
When she came back, something about Minato was off.
He wore the same quiet face as ever. But that quiet was a different kind of quiet. Like a wall.
"Yuzuki-san?" Haruka called.
"Ah," Minato said, not looking at her, washing up. "Finished your call?"
"Yes."
Minato said nothing more.
Looking at his back, Haruka felt a foreboding.
Had he heard it — the call? The window had been open. Her voice on the beach might have carried into the shop. "Still thinking about it," "want to work with you again," "a waste" — heard only in fragments, it would sound as if Haruka were considering going back to Tokyo.
She tried to explain.
"Yuzuki-san, that call just now—"
"It's fine," Minato cut in. "It has nothing to do with me."
His voice was cold.
Haruka lost her words.
She had never heard Minato make a voice like that.
"Konno-san," Minato said, stopping his hands, though he still did not look at her. "You have your own life. In Tokyo there are people who need you, a place where your talent can live. That's a good thing. You don't have to be bound to this town."
"Yuzuki-san, that's not it," Haruka said. "I—"
"I," Minato cut in again, "said a selfish thing. About waiting for winter together. As if to tie you to this town. Forget it. That was my selfishness."
The words emptied Haruka's mind.
Forget it. The promise they had made before Meiwa Rock. The promise to wait, together, for the most beautiful morning of the year.
"Why," Haruka said, her voice shaking. "Why would you say that?"
Minato looked at her, for the first time.
His eyes were quiet. But behind that quiet something deep was sinking.
"I," Minato said slowly, "am afraid of losing, now."
Haruka started.
"Losing my brother," Minato said, "I faced that sea for two years. I finally began to be able to look forward again — because you came. But for that very reason, I'm afraid. To hope in you, to need you, and then to have you gone. This time I wouldn't recover. So it's better not to hope from the start. If you're going to go back to Tokyo someday, then before that, I want to have prepared my heart."
Haruka listened.
Minato's fear, she understood. The fear of losing someone you love. Never again to taste that loss. That fear had him bracing himself.
But, Haruka thought.
Minato was mistaken.
"I," Haruka said, "am not going back to Tokyo."
Minato looked at her.
"I'm staying in this town," Haruka said, clearly. "That call just now — it was to refuse. No — I couldn't refuse. I told a lie, that I was still thinking. But that was because I hadn't been able to tell you how I feel. I had no courage to tell you, and yet I was afraid to close the road to Tokyo, all by myself. I was afraid too — just like you."
Minato gazed at her.
"But now I understand," Haruka said. "Because I was afraid, you became afraid. Because I stayed suspended, it reached you. I'm sorry. I've already decided. I'll stay in this town. I'll wait for winter, with you. That's what I want."
Silence fell in the shop.
Outside the window, the sound of the waves.
Minato was quiet a long time.
Then, faintly, he said, "I'm sorry."
The cold was gone from his voice.
"I decided on my own," Minato said, "that you'd go back to Tokyo. Without even asking how you felt. The one who was afraid was me. Afraid of losing you, so I tried to push you away before I could lose you. I'm the worst."
"You're not the worst," Haruka said. "I was the same. Afraid, and couldn't tell you. We're even."
The two of them looked at each other.
And, from neither one first, laughed a little.
But in that laughter, an awkwardness still remained.
A misunderstanding, once born, is not wiped away completely even when words undo it. A thin film stayed between them.
From that day, Haruka and Minato went back to how they had been.
Haruka went to Migiwa every morning. Minato brewed coffee. On the surface, nothing changed.
But the intimacy of that summer night, when their feelings had been a single step from touching, did not come back. The two of them grew a little more careful with each other. Afraid to wound, afraid to be wounded, each unable to take that last step.
Autumn deepened.
Winter drew, little by little, nearer.
The coldest, most beautiful morning of the year was drawing, surely, nearer.
But whether the two of them could meet that morning together, Haruka had come no longer to know.
