CHAPTER NINE — Dusk at Shinminato

One Sunday in October, Haruka invited Minato to the market at Shinminato.
It took courage to ask. Since the day of the misunderstanding, that thin film had stayed between them, and Haruka had grown hesitant to invite Minato to anything personal. But she had made up her mind: this film had to be broken. And for that, there was something she wanted to show him.
Her grandmother's photograph.
"Would you go to the Shinminato market with me?" she said, one morning, at Migiwa. "The shiraebi, the red snow crab. My grandmother used to go often — Matsuzo-san told me. I wanted to see it. It's a little daunting on my own, so — if you'd come."
Minato looked a little surprised. Then he said, "All right. The shop's closed on Sundays anyway."
That afternoon, the two of them set off for Shinminato.
The Shinminato market was full of life. Fresh-caught fish were lined up on ice. Red snow crab. Shiraebi. Whelk. Barracuda. The market people called out in brisk, spirited voices. The bustle of it overwhelmed Haruka.
"Amazing," she said. "So much fish."
"They call Toyama Bay a natural fish tank," Minato said. "Deep sea and shallow sea, close together. So all kinds of fish are caught. It's been a rich sea since long ago."
They walked the market.
Minato knew the fish well. This one's good cooked like this. This shellfish is at its best now. To walk listening to his commentary was pleasant. The awkwardness that had lingered since the day of the misunderstanding began, little by little, to loosen.
Evening came.
They left the market and walked along an old canal called the Uchikawa.
Shinminato's Uchikawa was a place Haruka had never seen. Old rows of houses lined both banks of the canal, and fishing boats lay moored, quiet. The town of water, they call it. Several bridges spanned the canal, each with a grace of its own.
And dusk came.
The surface of the canal took the setting sun and turned gold. The old houses became silhouettes. The moored boats floated quiet on the golden water. It was a beautiful scene, somewhere out of stopped time.
"It's beautiful," Haruka said.
"Yes," Minato said. "This is the hour I like the Uchikawa best."
They stood on a bridge and watched the dusk.
Now, Haruka thought.
"Yuzuki-san," she said. "There's something I want to show you."
From her bag, she took a single photograph.
Her grandmother's. The young Sawako and the fisherman, on the Amaharashi coast, laughing with the Tateyama beyond Meiwa Rock at their backs.
Minato took it. He gazed, a long while.
"This is… Sawako-san?" he said. "When she was young."
"Yes," Haruka said. "It came out of my grandmother's locked drawer. The man beside her is someone she loved, before she married. A fisherman."
Haruka told Minato her grandmother's secret.
The love of more than sixty years ago. The promise — "When the coldest winter clears, I will come for you." The fisherman who died in a winter sea, leaving that promise behind. And her grandmother, who had kept the letter and the photograph in a locked drawer, all her life. Who had gone on looking, from the back seat of Migiwa, at the Tateyama beyond Meiwa Rock.
Minato listened, in silence.
"My grandmother," Haruka said, "was waiting, all along. For a promise that would never be kept. For more than sixty years. Looking at that view, from Migiwa's window. Just the way you photograph that sea every morning — she too kept looking at that view, for someone she had lost."
Minato gazed at the photograph.
His hand shook, a little.
"So that's why Sawako-san always looked at the sea from that seat," he said quietly.
"Yes."
Minato was quiet a while.
The setting sun turned the canal gold.
"I always thought," Minato said at last, "that waiting for someone you'd lost was a backward thing. The act of a weak person who can't move forward. That's why I was ashamed, somewhere, of shooting that sea every morning. How long am I going to keep this up, I'd think — my brother isn't coming back."
He looked at the young Sawako in the photograph.
"But Sawako-san waited more than sixty years," Minato said. "And it wasn't a backward thing. Sawako-san, while she waited, lived — properly. She married, raised a child, loved her grandchild, loved you. She made good food, drank coffee, looked at the sea. Waiting and living don't contradict each other."
Tears came to Haruka's eyes.
"I think so too," she said. "My grandmother never forgot that man. But not forgetting, and being unable to live the present, are different things. My grandmother waited for him, and still lived her present, properly. She waited for me too — a grandchild who never came — buying shiraebi every year. My grandmother was a master of waiting."
Minato handed the photograph back to her.
And looked at her.
"Konno-san," he said. "I was afraid. Of losing you. So I tried to push you away. But that was wrong. Sawako-san wasn't afraid to lose. She loved, and lost, and loved again. Her child, her grandchild. If you're so afraid of losing that you stop loving — that's the same as not being alive at all."
The setting sun lit the two of them gold.
"Let me say it again," Minato said. "The promise I made before Meiwa Rock. I'm sorry for saying 'forget it.' That was fear talking. The truth is, I never wanted to forget it."
He looked at her, straight.
"Won't you wait with me?" Minato said. "For the coldest, most beautiful morning of the year. No — not only that. After that morning, too. Spring, summer, autumn. Always — together."
Haruka's chest filled.
Tears ran down her cheeks.
"Yes," she said. "I'll wait. Always — together."
The sun was setting beyond the canal.
The last light wrapped the two of them.
Minato reached out his hand toward her.
Haruka took it.
Their hands joined. The feeling that had been a single step from touching on the summer night before the ships of light was, now, at last, one.
The last of the sun set beyond the canal.
Dusk wrapped everything.
But inside Haruka's chest, it was bright.
That small light that had begun to glow deep within her on the night of the firefly squid — now it was lit, clear.
The thin film was broken.
Between the two of them there was no longer hesitation, no longer fear.
There was only the sure promise that, from here on, they would wait for winter together.
On the way back, Haruka and Minato walked the dark canal, hand in hand.
"About Yoko-san," Minato asked. "Don't you need to answer her?"
"I will," Haruka said. "The moment I get home. I'll refuse, properly. My place is here. I have no hesitation left."
Minato held her hand a little tighter.
That night, home again, Haruka called Yoko.
And refused, clearly.
"I'm sorry, Yoko," she said. "I'm staying in this town. There's someone here I care about. And I've found work I can only do here. Your offer made me truly happy. But my place is here now."
Yoko sounded a little wistful. But in the end she laughed.
"I see," she said. "I'm glad you're happy. Your voice, Haruka — it's completely different from your Tokyo days. Brighter. Take care of that person."
"I will," Haruka said. "Thank you. And come visit, sometime. The sea is beautiful. And the shiraebi are delicious."
She hung up and looked at her grandmother's memorial portrait, and the young photograph beside it.
"Grandma," she said. "I've decided. The morning you waited for — this time I'll wait for it with someone who'll come for me, properly."
Outside, the autumn sea sounded, quiet.
