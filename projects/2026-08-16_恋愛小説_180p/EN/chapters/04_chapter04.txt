CHAPTER FOUR — The Promise at Meiwa Rock

After the night of the firefly squid, something between Haruka and Minato shifted, just a little.
It was not a visible change. As before, Haruka went to Migiwa every morning, and Minato brewed coffee. Matsuzo spoke of the sea, and the regulars looked at it quietly. Nothing seemed to have changed. But Haruka could tell. That Minato exchanged a few more words than before. That when Haruka arrived, something like a faint relief crossed his face. It was a small change only Haruka noticed.
One morning, she woke earlier than usual.
The night had not yet fully lifted. The sky was moving, little by little, from indigo toward a thin pale blue. For some reason, Haruka wanted to go to Migiwa. At this hour Minato would be photographing the Tateyama beyond Meiwa Rock — alone, at an hour when no one came, as he had said.
That "hour when no one came," Haruka found she wanted to see.
She threw on a jacket and went down the hill.
The shop was still dark. Haruka passed it and went down to the beach.
On the beach, Minato stood. Tripod set, camera raised, he was looking out past the sea. His back did not stir. Haruka hesitated to call out. In that back there was a quiet concentration one must not break.
She stood a little apart and looked in the same direction.
Meiwa Rock stood quiet in the pale dawn light. Beyond it, the Tateyama Range showed — dimly, but certainly. The ridgeline was beginning, little by little, to take the morning light. What had been indigo turned to pale violet, then flushed, faintly, with rose.
Haruka caught her breath.
The color of the sky changed moment by moment. The same view became, minute by minute, an entirely different view. She remembered Minato's words: even the same place is never the same morning twice. It was true. Truly.
Before long, a single point on the horizon blazed.
The sun.
It rose not from beyond the mountains, but from the hills at Haruka's back, on this side of the sea. And its light struck the Tateyama Range across the water head-on. The snow-covered peaks, taking the morning sun, shone gold. The sea reflected the light, and set countless ripples glittering gold.
In that instant, Minato's shutter fired, again and again.
Kasha, kasha, kasha.
Then Minato lifted his face from the camera. And exhaled, a great breath — as if he had held it a long time.
"Yuzuki-san," Haruka called.
Minato turned. He seemed surprised to find her there.
"Konno-san," he said. "How long have you been there?"
"A little while," Haruka said. "I thought I shouldn't interrupt."
Minato looked slightly at a loss. Then he asked, "Did you watch?"
"I did," Haruka said. "It was beautiful. To think you see something this beautiful, alone, every morning."
Minato said nothing. He only dropped his eyes to the monitor.
"Did you get it?" Haruka asked. "The one?"
Still gazing at the screen, Minato shook his head.
"Not today either," he said. "Today there were too few clouds. I wanted a little cloud. Cloud on the mountains makes the light more complex. But — it's not bad."
Haruka stood beside him and looked into the monitor.
On the small screen, the Tateyama Range shone gold. The black silhouette of Meiwa Rock stood quiet before it. The sea glowed gold. It was the very view she had just seen with her eyes. And yet the one in the photograph looked, somehow, more urgent than the one before her — as if Minato had folded some kind of prayer into this view.
"To me it looks perfect," Haruka said.
"Perfect is no good," Minato said. "What I want to shoot isn't a perfect view. It's — how to put it — a morning so beautiful it hurts. A morning you know will never come again, and that even so, being there, feels like a miracle."
Haruka looked at his profile.
In those words, the "number of things" Minato carried showed through, faintly. Something that would never come again. In this morning shoot, perhaps Minato was searching for something that would never return. Or waiting for it.
She did not ask.
Instead, looking at the mountains beyond the sea, Haruka said, "My grandmother taught me the names of those mountains, when I was a child. But I don't remember them. Yuzuki-san, you said you'd teach me."
Minato looked a little surprised. Then he nodded.
"Yes," he said, and pointed out beyond the sea. "The sharpest one there — that's Tsurugi. 2,999 meters. One of the hardest mountains to climb in Japan. To its left, Dainichi. Behind it, that gentler one — that's Onanji, the main peak of Tateyama."
One by one, Minato named the mountains. Yakushi. Kuwasaki. The three Kedomaru peaks. Even hearing the names, Haruka could not immediately tell one from another. But hearing those names in Minato's voice, the mountains began to look less like mere scenery and more like presences, each with a name and a face of its own.
"Amazing," Haruka said. "You remember them all."
"I look at them every morning," Minato said. "Look for two years and you remember them whether you like it or not. Those mountains are my conversation partners, every morning."
Haruka laughed.
Minato laughed a little too.
The morning light filled, slowly, the beach where they stood.
"Say," Haruka said, in that light. "The photograph you want to take — the coldest morning of the year. If you get it, will you show me too?"
Minato looked at her.
"If you're still in this town by then," he said.
"I'll be here," Haruka said — more clearly than she had expected of herself. "I'll be in this town."
Having said it, she felt a little embarrassed.
Because it sounded like a promise. A promise to be in this town. To be here until the morning Minato wanted to photograph came.
Minato looked at her for a while.
Then he turned his gaze back to the Tateyama Range across the sea.
"Then," he said quietly, "I promise. When that morning comes, you'll be the first I show it to. No — more than that."
He paused, choosing his words.
"Let's see it together," Minato said. "The coldest, clearest morning of the year. The morning when the whole Tateyama Range shows beyond Meiwa Rock. Won't you wait for that morning — with me?"
Haruka's heart struck, hard.
"Yes," she said. "I'll wait. Together."
Beyond Meiwa Rock, the Tateyama Range shone in the morning sun.
Haruka and Minato looked at those mountains, side by side.
Between them, a promise had been born. A promise with no name yet — and yet a certain one.
From that day, Haruka began to wait for winter.
For the coldest morning of the year. For the morning she would see the Tateyama across the sea, standing beside Minato.
Spring had only just begun, and already Haruka was thinking of a still-distant winter.
