# The Box Arrives Without the Dish in It

Everything I have ordered in so far could be eaten by heating it: the curry, the oden, both done by dropping a pouch in hot water. This one is different. You open the box and there is no dish inside. Motsu-nabe, from Fukuoka.

What comes is ingredients. Beef offal, a broth, garlic and chilli, and noodles for the end. That is all. No vegetables — the cabbage and the garlic chives you buy yourself. That is partly convenience for the sender and partly the nature of the dish: leaf vegetables do not keep, and you need a lot of them. So when the box arrives nothing is edible yet. You go shopping, you cut, you simmer, and only then is there a meal.

This is a first for the series: a parcel that makes you cook. With the curry, someone else had done all of it; with the oden, the simmering was already done. The receiver only had to warm it through. Motsu-nabe hands the work over. Why is that acceptable when it is more trouble? Probably because a hotpot was always like that. Even in a restaurant you add things to the pot in front of you and take out what has cooked. It is not a dish that arrives finished. That shape survives the box.

Motsu is beef offal, usually small intestine as I understand it. It needs preparing, and how the smell is dealt with is where it is won or lost; a mail-order set should arrive with that already done, which is the difference from buying it raw. It is fatty, and simmering brings the fat to the surface. Some people find that heavy and some find it the point. Which I am, I will not know until I eat it.

In photographs the pot is piled high with cabbage and chives before the heat goes on, and they collapse as they cook; the volume at the start and the volume on your plate are very different. The chives here are not a garnish but an ingredient — a whole bunch goes in. I cannot think of many dishes that use that much of them. Whether the cabbage should be cooked soft or kept with some bite is, I assume, a matter of opinion.

Sliced garlic goes into the broth in quantity, and rings of chilli are scattered over the cabbage. The garlic probably decides more of the character of this dish than anything else. It is strong, and it stays with you the next day — which makes it food for the evening. Eating it at lunch and then meeting people in the afternoon is not really an option. Not many dishes decide what time of day you can eat them.

The broth comes in soy and in miso, and ordering means choosing. Some sets contain both. Being able to choose is awkward: in a restaurant the house version arrives and that is that, whereas here the result changes at the point of ordering, and what exactly you have eaten becomes a little vague. I intend to order soy, for the reason that I already wrote about miso with the Nagoya oden, and for no reason connected to taste.

At the end you put noodles into what is left — champon noodles, thick ones that take up the broth. Some people make a rice porridge instead, but the sets seem to come with noodles. Having the ending included is particular to this one. Neither the curry nor the oden came with instructions for what to do afterwards.

A hotpot is food for a number of people. You can eat one alone, but the sets start at two or three portions, and a single serving of motsu-nabe is not a thing you see. The dish assumes people around it. As a condition for ordering in, that is demanding: a curry is one person and one pouch, while this starts with gathering people. Posting a dish that requires arrangements is, when you think about it, an odd thing to do. That it works at all may be because a hotpot belongs to the category of food that makes an evening into an occasion.

One of the rules here is that I do not name something I have not used or eaten. With motsu-nabe the shop matters more than usual — the cut of offal, the broth, the quantity all differ — so "whose" it is ought to be the most important information. Even so, naming it before eating would mean recommending something I have not tried, the same reason I did not name the knife workshop. When I have eaten it, I will write it.

What I will be looking at: how much fat comes to the surface and how I feel about it; whether a whole bunch of chives is right or too much; how much garlic is still with me the following day, which only someone who has eaten it can report; and how much broth the noodles take up at the end. Also the work itself — how long it takes counting the shopping, and what that effort adds compared with something you only heat. This series is looking for things that survive being put in a box. This one survives differently, and that is the part I want to write.

A hotpot ought to be hard to send. There is liquid, there are many components, some of them do not keep. None of that suits a parcel. It works, I think, because what is sent is not a finished dish but parts: the broth concentrated into a pouch, the offal prepared and frozen, noodles dried or fresh, and the vegetables not sent at all. Broken down like that, each piece travels. You reassemble it at the other end. The curry and the oden were finished things put in a box. This is taken apart, posted, and rebuilt. The same word, "mail-order", covering quite different operations.

Obviously, you need a pot. We have an earthenware one, brought out every winter for yudofu, but not every house has one, and then you are using a frying pan or an ordinary saucepan. You also need a portable burner if you want to eat it cooking in front of you; a kitchen hob will not do. Equipment becoming part of the order has not come up before — a retort curry needs a pan to boil water in, and every house has that. I wrote in the knife piece that a tool is not complete by itself. The same thing is happening with food.

There is also room to get it wrong, which is new. You can hardly fail at heating a pouch; you can overdo it slightly and that is about all. A hotpot you can fail at: the cabbage collapses, the offal goes tough, too much broth and it is thin, the noodles go in at the wrong moment. Which means that if what arrives is not good, I will not know whether that is the product or me. That is awkward for someone writing about it, so the first time I intend to follow whatever the packet says. Doing it my own way can wait for the second.

And the gap between eating it there and eating it at home is probably smaller here than with the others. The curry was eaten standing at a counter in a few minutes, and that speed was part of the taste, so sitting down changes it. A hotpot keeps its shape wherever it is: you sit around it, you wait for things to cook, you take them out, and the pot slowly empties. That passage of time is the same in a shop and at a table. In the sense that less is lost, it may be well suited to being posted — the troublesome-looking business of sending raw parts may be what preserves the original experience best. I will not know until I eat it. But I am writing down that this is what I expected beforehand, and if it turns out to be wrong, I will write that too.
