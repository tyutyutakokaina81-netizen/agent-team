On a summer-holiday morning, while it's still cool, neighborhood children shuffle to a square or park, rubbing sleepy eyes, and do exercises together to music from the radio — rajio taiso, "radio calisthenics." Around six a.m., set time, set place; when I was a child that was simply the morning scene. As that music almost everyone knows begins, a half-asleep body starts to move — arms wide, bend back, hop — nobody trying hard, but seeing it through. It usually comes with an attendance card hung round your neck, stamped each time; watching the squares fill was a childhood joy, and perfect attendance sometimes earned a small reward. Many kids came for the stamp more than the exercise — I was one — but wanting it kept me rising early all summer, a fine habit in its own right. And it isn't only children: adults and older folk join as morning exercise, generations who don't usually meet doing the same moves to the same music, then trading "good mornings." Only ten minutes, but in them the town's morning rises gently — less exercise than a small gathering. Fewer areas do it now as life changes, and around us too it's faded; yet that music still nearly moves my body, bringing back those mornings. A small but real scene of the Japanese summer. 【English】On a summer-holiday morning, while it's still cool, the neighborhood children gather at a square or park, shuffling along and rubbing sleepy eyes. Then, to that music coming from the radio, everyone does exercises together. This is rajio taiso — "radio calisthenics."

Around six in the morning, at a set time, to a set place. When I was a child that was simply the scene of a summer-holiday morning. As that music — the one almost everyone has heard at least once — begins, a still-half-asleep body slowly starts to move: swing the arms wide, bend back, hop in place. Nobody tries especially hard, but somehow you see it through to the end. Rajio taiso usually comes with an "attendance card": a small card hung around your neck, stamped by a helper each time the exercises finish. Watching the little squares fill in, one by one, was a childhood joy; go every day and fill them all, and you felt somehow proud, sometimes with a small reward at the end for perfect attendance. Honestly, plenty of kids were there for the stamp more than the exercise — I was one of them. But wanting that stamp kept me getting up early all summer, and looking back, that was a fine habit in its own right. What's interesting is that it isn't only children who gather: neighborhood adults and older folk join in as a bit of morning exercise. Generations that don't usually cross paths do the same movements to the same music in the morning square, then trade "good mornings" as each day begins. The exercise itself is only about ten minutes, but in those ten minutes the town's morning seems to rise gently to its feet — rajio taiso is less exercise than a small summer-morning gathering. These days, I hear, more areas no longer do it; as ways of living change, just gathering in the morning has grown harder, and around us too it's less than it was. Even so, hearing that music, my body still nearly moves on its own, and the air of those summer mornings — rubbing sleepy eyes on the way to the square — comes suddenly back. A small but real scene of the Japanese summer. I'd be glad if visitors could somewhere know this odd, peaceful custom of everyone doing the same exercises in a morning square. (This is my own, older memory; whether it's done, and the cards and rewards, vary greatly by area, era and generation, and fewer places do it now; this doesn't generalize any one area's custom.)
