Two Hundred Ordinary Things

I live in Takaoka, in Toyama, on the Japan Sea coast, and I have now written more than two hundred posts here. Almost none of them are about sights. They are about how many times people stir their natto, about the water that comes up out of the middle of the road in winter to melt the snow, about a harvested rice field that turns green a second time.

I started with the sights. There is a station here where you can see a 3,000-metre range across the sea; there is a waterfall that falls from a great height. I wrote those and then ran out. You go to a sight once a year, so you can write about it once: a photograph, the year it was built, how it felt. The things I see every day gave me far more to say — how many umbrellas are standing in the hall, where a paper screen always tears, what comes in with the laundry.

Adding English summaries taught me something. The sights are easy to look up: photographs, directions, opening hours, all of it. The ordinary things are not. Why does one house have both a Buddhist altar and a Shinto shelf? Why does music play from loudspeakers across the whole town in the early evening? Why does Japanese call the sound of insects a voice? People who live here have never had to think about it. You only notice when someone asks, and that moment of noticing is what I write down.

One rule I keep: I do not write "in Japan, we do this." The stirring count differs by household. So does the kind of miso, and how often a family visits a grave. I keep what I saw in my own house separate from what I was told, and every post ends with a note setting out what is certain, what I have not checked, and what is disputed. If I have not checked something, I say so rather than filling the gap with a guess. It means I can write less. I still think it is better than writing about things I do not know as though I did.

The posts are grouped loosely. There is the kitchen — the stirring of natto, the arguments about what belongs in pork miso soup, the moment a knife stops halfway through a pumpkin, the fact that chestnut rice is seasoned with nothing but salt. There is autumn food — the dried persimmons hanging under the eaves, made sweet by wind alone with no fire and no sugar; the ginkgo nuts people pick up off the pavement and eat, after the smell has warned you they are there. There is the house — the circular notice board that stops at my door, the umbrellas nobody can account for, the screen that tears at the same height every time, the boot you need your other foot to remove. There is what happens outside without anyone doing anything — insects at night, the smell of osmanthus arriving before the flower, red dragonflies coming down from the mountains, the birds you notice only once they have gone. And there is what belongs to this prefecture: pipes in the road that melt snow with water, fast water channels running through town with signs warning you not to fall in, a mountain that goes white while you are still in short sleeves.

If you have never been to Japan, I would start with the house and with what happens outside. Food you can picture from a photograph; a hallway full of umbrellas, or music at five in the evening, you cannot. If you have lived in Japan, the kitchen posts will probably land differently — you will have your own version of each, and the difference between your version and mine is the interesting part.

Two posts a day. Mostly seasonal, so autumn will turn into winter soon, and in a place with this much snow that means snow: clearing it, being stopped by it, waiting for it to melt. I wrote about the snow-melting pipes in autumn, but they have not run yet. When they do, I will write that too.

## 読む順番について

決まった順番はない。上から読む必要もない。

ただ、日本に来たことがない人には、三と四から読んでもらうのがいいと思う。食べものの記事は写真で想像がつくけれど、玄関の傘や、夕方に鳴るチャイムは、写真を見ても何が起きているのか分からないからだ。

逆に、日本に住んだことがある人には、一と二が通じやすいと思う。「うちもそうだった」と思える部分があるはずで、その「うちはこうだった」の違いのほうが、たぶん面白い。

五は、富山に来る予定がある人向けというより、来る予定がない人向けのつもりで書いている。雪国の道路に水が流れていることも、町の中を速い水が通っていることも、見に行くほどのものではない。ただ、ここでは毎日その横を歩いている。

## 書き方の決まり

ひとつの記事は、だいたい短い。ひとつのことだけを書く。

終わりには必ず「事実検証ノート」を付けている。確かなこと、確かめていないこと、諸説あること、を分けて書く。自分の家で見たのか、人から聞いたのか、も分ける。

数字を書くときは気をつけている。「何年から」「何人が」「何位」といったものは、確かめられないかぎり書かない。書かないことで記事が薄くなるなら、そのほうがいい。

英語の要約も毎回付けている。翻訳ではなく、同じことを英語で書き直したものだ。日本語で書いた細かい言い回しは落ちるけれど、何の話なのかは伝わると思う。

ひとつ、失敗していたことがある。しばらくのあいだ、英語の要約を記事の外に書いていて、公開した記事には入っていなかった。書いてあるのに届いていなかった、ということだ。数えて気づいて、直した。こういうことは、数えないと分からない。

題材が重ならないようにも気をつけている。同じようなものを続けて書くと、書き方まで似てくる。前の記事と同じ言い回しで始まっていないか、締めが同じ形になっていないか、出す前に見るようにしている。

【English】Two Hundred Ordinary Things

I live in Takaoka, in Toyama, on the Japan Sea coast, and I have now written more than two hundred posts here. Almost none of them are about sights. They are about how many times people stir their natto, about the water that comes up out of the middle of the road in winter to melt the snow, about a harvested rice field that turns green a second time.

I started with the sights. There is a station here where you can see a 3,000-metre range across the sea; there is a waterfall that falls from a great height. I wrote those and then ran out. You go to a sight once a year, so you can write about it once: a photograph, the year it was built, how it felt. The things I see every day gave me far more to say — how many umbrellas are standing in the hall, where a paper screen always tears, what comes in with the laundry.

Adding English summaries taught me something. The sights are easy to look up: photographs, directions, opening hours, all of it. The ordinary things are not. Why does one house have both a Buddhist altar and a Shinto shelf? Why does music play from loudspeakers across the whole town in the early evening? Why does Japanese call the sound of insects a voice? People who live here have never had to think about it. You only notice when someone asks, and that moment of noticing is what I write down.

One rule I keep: I do not write "in Japan, we do this." The stirring count differs by household. So does the kind of miso, and how often a family visits a grave. I keep what I saw in my own house separate from what I was told, and every post ends with a note setting out what is certain, what I have not checked, and what is disputed. If I have not checked something, I say so rather than filling the gap with a guess. It means I can write less. I still think it is better than writing about things I do not know as though I did.

The posts are grouped loosely. There is the kitchen — the stirring of natto, the arguments about what belongs in pork miso soup, the moment a knife stops halfway through a pumpkin, the fact that chestnut rice is seasoned with nothing but salt. There is autumn food — the dried persimmons hanging under the eaves, made sweet by wind alone with no fire and no sugar; the ginkgo nuts people pick up off the pavement and eat, after the smell has warned you they are there. There is the house — the circular notice board that stops at my door, the umbrellas nobody can account for, the screen that tears at the same height every time, the boot you need your other foot to remove. There is what happens outside without anyone doing anything — insects at night, the smell of osmanthus arriving before the flower, red dragonflies coming down from the mountains, the birds you notice only once they have gone. And there is what belongs to this prefecture: pipes in the road that melt snow with water, fast water channels running through town with signs warning you not to fall in, a mountain that goes white while you are still in short sleeves.

If you have never been to Japan, I would start with the house and with what happens outside. Food you can picture from a photograph; a hallway full of umbrellas, or music at five in the evening, you cannot. If you have lived in Japan, the kitchen posts will probably land differently — you will have your own version of each, and the difference between your version and mine is the interesting part.

Two posts a day. Mostly seasonal, so autumn will turn into winter soon, and in a place with this much snow that means snow: clearing it, being stopped by it, waiting for it to melt. I wrote about the snow-melting pipes in autumn, but they have not run yet. When they do, I will write that too.
