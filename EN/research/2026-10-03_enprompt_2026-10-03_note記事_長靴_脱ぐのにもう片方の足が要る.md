You Need the Other Foot to Get It Off

Back home, I end up standing on one leg in the entrance. Left toe pinned on the right heel, pull the foot out, and when the pin slips my hand goes to the wall. Putting them on takes a second — no laces, no fastenings, nothing to think about — and yet it is only the coming home that costs anything. The easy half and the awkward half are split neatly between leaving and returning. I could bend down and pull them off by hand, which would be quicker, except that boots back from outside are wet and there is mud on the part you would have to hold, so I try to manage it with feet alone and end up in that unsteady posture every time. They do not fit in the shoe cupboard either — too tall for the shelf — so they stay out, standing in the corner of the entrance, and a tool with nowhere to be put away stays in your field of view on the days you are not using it. There is one pair in this house. Other households presumably have one each; here, if somebody has gone out in them, the next person goes out in ordinary shoes. A queue forming on a rainy day reads oddly written down, but that is what happens. This is a place said to get a lot of rain and snow, so boots never feel like a winter-only thing — though I have never counted the days I actually wear them, and counting might well give a smaller number than I expect. Today, one leg again in the entrance, thinking the same thing.
