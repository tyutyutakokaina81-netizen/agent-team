【English】The electric fan — that sound of it turning its head.

Press the switch with a click and the blades start — heavy at first, a low drone, then faster, and a wind is born, the head swinging slowly left and right. Hear that click and that drone and some part of you answers "summer" on its own. We have air conditioning, of course, and lean on it on hot days; but a fan has a fan's own wind, unlike the AC's even, room-wide chill — it comes for a moment, then wanders off, and that fickle breeze somehow feels kinder to the body.

Plenty of us, as kids, played by making noise in front of the fan: aim an "aaah" at the spinning blades and it chops into a stuttering, robot voice — "I. am. an. alien." I did it endlessly. And got scolded — "don't put your fingers in the blades" — since the gap in the guard always tempted a finger closer; a risky game, looking back. To a child the fan was less a cooling tool than a bit of a toy. The head-swing has its own timing: the wind arrives, hits your face, feels good — and just as you think so, it slips away, then after a while comes back; that come-and-go, almost maddening, I somehow don't dislike. A steady blast, the whole time, tires you out anyway, so this now-and-then is just right. By efficiency you'd fix it on yourself and skip the swinging — but you don't; that timing, too, is part of what a fan is.

At night I leave it on low to sleep, head swinging so it doesn't hit me directly, and close my eyes to that far-off drone — like a lullaby, and before I know it I'm asleep. New appliances keep coming, and fans too have grown quiet and stylish; still, I'd like that slightly unfashionable drone and the click of the switch to stay as they are. As one of the sounds of summer, it has kept a place in my ears. (How cool or comfortable you feel varies by person; this is our one example. On hot days don't rely on a fan alone — use AC together, drink water, and take real care against heatstroke; mind blade and guard accidents too, especially with small children.)
