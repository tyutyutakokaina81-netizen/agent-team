【English】Gyozui — bathing in water from a tub.

You set a big tub down in the yard with a thud and fill it brimming with water. A hot summer evening: you put the child in it and pour water over them with a dipper, zabaa — "so cold!" comes a shriek that's half delight. That's gyozui, an old summer way of cooling off you don't see much now. In the old days there was no air conditioning, and not every house had a shower, so on hot days you filled a tub in the yard and poured it over yourself, rinsing off sweat, cooling a heated body — that simple, primitive way of cooling; and yet I remember it feeling quite good.

The water in the tub was well-water or tap water; left in the sun a while it goes a little tepid, but to a sweated body even water that mild felt plenty cool. You pour it over your head with the dipper, and the first scoop makes you flinch, ugh — but by the second and third the body grows used to it, and that stewing heat flows away with the water, a sense of the heat draining from the body, a plain pleasure unlike a shower. A child's gyozui is usually done stark naked, stripped bare in the yard and splashed with water; a child doesn't mind the neighbors' eyes, only that the water is cold and good, shrieking with glee, splashing each other, thrashing about in the tub.

That shrieking of children in the yard on a summer evening was, looking back, a very peaceful, idyllic scene. Done, you towel off and feel fresh, and wait for supper in a cooled body — that freshness after gyozui was a kind of reward of a summer evening. Once every house had AC and a shower became ordinary, gyozui slowly disappeared; we haven't done tub-gyozui in years either. It's grown convenient — but that plain way of pouring water in the yard I miss a little. A tub, a dipper, well-water — with just those tools you held your own against the summer heat, and looking back from a cool room now, that summer of sweating, pouring water, and sweating again seems, somehow, humanly good. (Whether a household does gyozui varies greatly by region and generation, and it's grown rare now; this is our one example and memory. When handling water, take great care with water accidents — never take your eyes off small children in particular.)
