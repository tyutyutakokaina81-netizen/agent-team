【English】Eating eel at the height of the heat.

In the hottest stretch of summer there's a day called doyo-no-ushi-no-hi, and on it people eat eel (unagi). The idea is to eat something nourishing to get through the heat when your body is flagging. There are various theories about its origin, but before any reasoning, "this day means eel" is somehow settled into the body. When grilled eel starts appearing in the supermarket, you think, ah, that time already — that savory, sweet-salty smell of the sauce. We usually eat eel on the day too.

Eel is above all kabayaki: split, brushed with a sweet-savory sauce, and grilled; the smell of that sauce charring makes rice disappear, and just catching it your stomach growls. We usually make una-don — kabayaki over hot rice, a little sauce, and a shake of sansho pepper. The plump eel, the sweet-savory sauce and the rice — that combination just works, and even with a heat-killed appetite the eel goes right down.

Whether to dust the bowl with powdered sansho divides people; we're the dusting kind. Against the eel's rich fat, sansho's sharp fragrance tightens the whole taste — a small thing, but it changes the impression of the eel. As a kid I disliked the sting; grown, I can't get enough of that fragrance, and tastes shifting like that may be one of the pleasures of getting older.

Honestly, eel isn't something you eat lightly anymore — prices have risen, and its stocks come up as an issue now and then — so in our house it's not an everyday thing but a small treat for the day of the ox. Yet because it's rare, it's welcome: once or twice a year the family eats eel on this day and vaguely thinks, "let's get through this summer too." Whether it truly works against summer fatigue, honestly, I don't know (views on the nutrition vary); but eating the same thing together at the height of the heat and resolving to get through — that feeling may be the heart of the custom. This is a food custom of a Japanese summer, an old small wisdom for living with the heat, and I'd be glad if a little of it came through. (Origins and nutrition views vary; take care with cooking and food safety, and eel's stocks and prices change year to year.)
