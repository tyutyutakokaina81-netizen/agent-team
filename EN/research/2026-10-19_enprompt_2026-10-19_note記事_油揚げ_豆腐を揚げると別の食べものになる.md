Fry the Tofu and It Stops Being Tofu

There is a packet on a shelf in the refrigerator holding something thin and pale brown. One sheet is a little larger than a palm, under a centimetre thick, and very light; held up, it bends like paper. **Aburaage** — fried tofu.

**It is made from tofu, and the kitchen treats it as a different ingredient entirely.** Thin slices of tofu, deep-fried. The material ought to be the same, yet tofu sits in the chilled case in a tub of water, while this sits beside it in a dry bag. You buy them separately. You use them for different things. **One step — frying — changed where it is kept and what it is for.**

**What changed is that the water left and oil came in.** Tofu is mostly water: cut it and water runs, it breaks, it spoils if left. Frying drives that water off and oil takes its place. What remains is **thin, light, and cloth-like** — you can tear it by hand, slice it finely, and it does not fall apart. That is where what tofu can do and what this can do separate.

**The most interesting part is that it is hollow.** It puffs while frying and a gap forms inside, and the gap stays after it cools. So if you cut one edge and open it, **it becomes a bag.** You can put things in it. Fill it with rice and you have a pouch of rice; some people tuck in a piece of mochi and simmer it. **The ingredient is also a container.** I cannot think of many others.

**Simmered, it drinks.** Used not as a bag but chopped — into a simmered dish, into miso soup, into rice cooked with other things — it **takes up the liquid.** Having been puffed by the oil, the inside is open, and cooking liquid goes in; bite it and the liquid comes back out. When I wrote about dried daikon strips, aburaage was one of the things simmered alongside. That also drinks, so the pot holds **two things that both absorb.**

**One sheet on top of the noodles.** There is a bowl of udon or soba with a single sweet-simmered sheet of aburaage laid on it, floating on the broth; lift it with chopsticks and it is heavy with liquid. It is called *kitsune*, fox. I have heard explanations for the name and have not verified any of them, so I will not write one here. What is interesting is that **one sheet is enough to make a named dish** — no meat, no tempura, just the sweet sheet, and the bowl has a name. The broth is soy-savoury and the topping is sweet: **the two run in different directions** and somehow settle together in the eating.

**Opening the pouch takes a knack.** Before you can fill one you have to open it, and that is harder than it sounds — cut the edge and the inner faces are often stuck together; force a finger in and the bottom tears. The trick is **to roll a chopstick over it**: lay the sheet on a board, rest a chopstick on top, press lightly and roll, and the inner surfaces separate. A torn one simply gets chopped instead and goes into a simmered dish, which amounts to the same thing. **Even failure has a use**, which makes it a forgiving ingredient.

**It also comes ready-shredded.** The shops carry bags of it already cut into thin strips: open and tip straight into the miso soup, no board required. Whole sheet or pre-cut — if you know what you are making, the cut one is faster; if you do not, the whole sheet keeps its options. **The same ingredient sits on the shelf twice, differing only in whether the cutting has been sold to you.**

**Some places sell it straight out of the fryer.** You see it now and then at the front of a tofu shop, and it does not look like the cold one in a bag: puffed, steaming. I am told you can eat it as it is, with a little soy sauce. I have never bought one — the times I pass are the times I have no use for it.

**Sometimes you take the oil back out.** Some households pour boiling water over it first — *abura-nuki*, de-oiling: set it in a sieve and run hot water over the top, and the surface oil leaves and it goes paler. Why? Because old oil smells, because flavour then enters more easily; that is what I have been told. We do it sometimes and not others — straight into miso soup, usually not; for a simmered dish, if I feel like it. **We deliberately remove oil from a thing that was deliberately fried in oil.** Said out loud it sounds absurd, and it is nonetheless sensible.

**There are thin ones and thick ones.** Most are the thin, bendy kind. There are also thicker ones with white tofu still in the middle, sometimes sold under another name; simmered, the liquid reaches the centre and it becomes its own food. **The same word covers different thicknesses**, so buying it means deciding which dish you are buying for.

**It freezes.** A bag holds several sheets and you use one or two at a time, so there is a surplus, and left in the refrigerator it does not keep. Frozen, it can go straight into a pot — thin and bendy, it needs no thawing. **Being thin is also what makes it easy to store.**

**It is fried, and it never gets to be the main dish.** This part is slightly strange. Fried things are usually the centre of the plate: a pork cutlet, fried chicken, tempura. Not this. A component of miso soup; alongside a simmered dish; inside rice. **It is always inside something else.** Fried, and never treated as a fried dish — that is hard to explain to a visitor.

**It decides what miso soup tastes like.** Soup with aburaage in it and soup without are noticeably different. A little oil floats, the broth gains body, and it is no longer the same thing as a bowl of wakame and tofu. With no meat and no fish in it, **the oil alone supplies the sense of having eaten.** I think it has long done the job of adding satisfaction cheaply.

**Grilled, it becomes something else again.** You need not simmer it. Put it in a pan or over a wire rack and brown both sides and **the outside goes crisp** while the inside stays soft. Cut it, a little soy sauce, some chopped spring onion on top, and that is a dish. Simmered it swells with liquid; grilled it crisps. **The same object takes opposite textures depending on how the heat arrives.**

**There is a soup whose only ingredient is this.** You might expect that to feel impoverished, and it does not: oil on the surface, flavour in the broth, and liquid coming back when you bite. **One ingredient is enough** because aburaage trades with the broth — it takes the broth's flavour and gives its own oil back. I once wrote about a hotpot whose only ingredient is tofu; the same shape exists in a soup bowl.

**Sweetened, the bag becomes sushi.** Back to filling it: before stuffing, the sheet is simmered hard in sugar and soy sauce until it browns and grows heavy with liquid. Then vinegared rice goes inside. **A sweet pouch holding sour rice** — an arrangement that, thought about, is peculiar, and works. Shops sell the simmered pouches on their own, so that all you do is fill them.

**It tells you it has gone off by smell, before the date does.** Oil oxidises, and opening the bag you know at once. **Things with oil in them announce their spoiling through smell**; tofu turns sour, this one speaks in the language of old oil. So you do not stockpile it.

**The hard part to explain to a visitor** starts with "fried tofu", which makes people picture a battered dish in sauce. It is not that: **thin slices of tofu, fried, and then used as an ingredient.** Then there is **the bag** — a food with a cavity you put other food into, which is an idea that does not travel easily. Then that **it is fried and still never the main dish**, which sits badly against the assumption that fried food is the centre of a meal. And finally **taking the oil out of something fried in oil**, which is probably the hardest of all.

**There are two sheets left in the open bag.** One will be chopped into tomorrow's miso soup, I expect. The other will probably go to the freezer. It sits beside the tofu and is not handled like tofu: from the same bean, and — by exactly the amount that frying changed it — living a different life.
