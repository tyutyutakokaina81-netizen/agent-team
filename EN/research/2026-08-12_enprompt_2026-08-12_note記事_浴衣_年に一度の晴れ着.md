【English】A yukata — a fine garment worn once a year.

You pull the folded yukata from the back of the chest — a year since last time. Unfolded, it's a little creased; slip your arms through, and the crisp cotton feels good against the skin. Festival day. Putting on this yukata, worn only once or twice a year, somehow straightens your spine a little — today is a special day, you feel. A yukata is summer's fine garment. Usually I loll about in a T-shirt and shorts; but on the day of a festival or fireworks, you wear the yukata, dress unlike usual, and that alone makes the day feel a little special — a yukata has that power to turn the everyday into something out of the ordinary.

Its great hurdle is the obi (sash) — tying that neatly is, it turns out, fairly hard, and every year, each time I wear it, I've forgotten how. "Wait, how does this go again?" — looking it up on the phone, getting a family member to help, and in the end, unable to tie it well, I get a slightly lopsided bow. Still, somehow the obi is tied and the yukata look is complete; unused to the garment, my movements are a bit awkward, my stride smaller than usual, but that unusual, slightly composed feeling is the good of it — coarse, ordinary me seeming a touch more refined. A yukata comes with geta (wooden clogs): you walk in those wooden clogs, karan, koron, a fine sound, ringing the geta down a festival night road, and hearing it, ah, a summer festival, the mood lifts all the more.

Then again, unaccustomed to geta, the thong makes the base of your toes sore, and by the festival's later half your feet usually hurt; still, for that karan-koron and the cool look of a yukata, you bear a little — you want to dress up a bit for a summer festival. You wear the yukata and head to the festival, peer at the stalls, look up at the fireworks; a summer night where yukata-clad people stroll, that festive air — precisely because the ordinary days are plain, such a fine day, once a year, is a joy. The festival over, home, you take off the yukata and fold it, to sleep again at the back of the chest until next year — once or twice a year; but for those few fine days the yukata is properly there, a cherished garment that colors a special summer day. (How to wear a yukata, tie the obi and the manners vary by view and school, and differ by region and household; this is our one example. With unfamiliar geta and a festival's crowds and heat, mind thong-rub, tripping and heatstroke.)
