【English】Living with the summer slump — without fighting it.

Some mornings my eyes are open but my body won't peel off the futon. The back of my head feels heavy, even lifting an arm seems like too much, and the usual breakfast just won't go down. This, I figure, is what we around here call natsubate — the summer slump.

I honestly don't know if the word has a firm medical definition. But when hot days drag on, a lot of us get vaguely worn out, lose our appetite, and sleep badly, and we've long called that natsubate. People blame sweating too much, too many cold things, too little sleep — probably it's not any one cause, but a pile of small strains.

The first thing I tell myself is: don't push it. There's no special trick that works. But forcing through it on willpower usually costs me more later. I drop one plan, move non-urgent errands to the cooler evening, and it eases a little. This is just my own case, though — tiredness differs from person to person, and what looks like a simple slump can be something else. When it feels strong or unusual, it's best not to tough it out but to ask a doctor.

What people generally say about hot spells is to take water and salt often, and not to force yourself in the heat — heat illness can be genuinely dangerous if you let your guard down. So I sip barley tea before I even feel thirsty. I keep out of the midday sun, cool my neck and wrists with water, rest where a breeze passes, use the air conditioning in moderation. All obvious things — and keeping the obvious ones is somehow the hardest.

When I've no appetite, something light and small works better than a big meal for me: cold noodles, something sour, something that slides down easily. I make no claims about it; it's just about eating what you can. And sleep — I take a lukewarm shower, cool the room first, anything to make dozing off a bit easier. No cure, just gentler conditions.

In the end, what I've learned is not to fight the tiredness. Tired is tired. Rather than crushing it with grit, I live alongside it for a while — take the cool, drink water, eat a little, drop one plan. Nothing flashy, but this quiet arithmetic feels like the surest way through the season. (This is a resident's everyday take, not medical advice; if you feel unwell, please don't push it — see a medical professional.)
