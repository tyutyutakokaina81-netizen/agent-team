【English】The car is a second room.

Live in the countryside and you spend a real chunk of the day inside the car. Commuting, shopping, ferrying kids around — the stretches between errands are far longer than in a city. So the inside of the car quietly becomes a second room. It isn't a dramatic subject, but you can't describe rural life here without this "time in the car." Here's roughly what ours is like; most families' cars are probably much the same.

Things that live in the car: a folding umbrella (in Hokuriku they say "forget your lunch, but never your umbrella" — rain comes suddenly), a lap blanket, a shopping bag, a few snacks for when a child gets fussy. In winter, add the snow kit — a scraper for the windscreen, rubber boots, work gloves — so that a car buried in snow at dawn doesn't throw you. Look inside a car here and you can more or less read the local climate.

It's also a place to wait. A rural car isn't only for moving; you sit in the parking lot with the engine off until a child's lesson ends, phone in hand or eyes half-closed; you wait outside a shop for family. Seat reclined, air-con on — cramped, but a space of your own, a spot that's neither home nor work, and anyone who's driven a lot out here knows the feeling.

And oddly, families talk more in the car. Facing forward instead of each other seems to make it easier — what happened at school, what's for dinner, small talk surfacing at a red light. Things that never come up at the table slip out on the move; in a region of long drives, the "conversation in the car" turns out to matter.

The car is taken for a mere means of transport. But out here the time spent inside it is part of daily life — a place to wait, to talk, to stash the season's gear. This is one way rural Japan lives with its cars, different from a city's train-centered life, and I'd be glad if a little of this "time in the car" came through.
