【English】You wake in the morning and hear cicadas from outside — not one or two, but from every tree, all at once, roaring. A Japanese summer morning usually begins with this sound.

Honestly, cicadas are loud. In the early afternoon of high summer especially, that oil-frying "jiri-jiri" piling up can make the heat feel doubled. And yet, strangely, a summer with none of that sound at all feels somehow lacking — loud, but lonely without them; that's what cicadas are. What's interesting is that the kind of cicada singing tells you roughly where in the summer you are. The ones that cry most busily at the height of summer; and then, as summer's end nears, a slightly forlorn call — the "tsuku-tsuku-boshi" — begins to mix in, and hearing it, every year I think, ah, summer is nearing its end. Cicadas carry no calendar, yet they tell the turning of the season by voice (the kinds and timing differ by area and year, so this is only how it feels around here). The peak of the heat, and the first hint of its ending, are announced by the cicada chorus. In summer, cast-off cicada shells cling to tree trunks and the undersides of leaves; as a child I liked collecting them. They come up from the soil, shed their shells, become adults — and the shed shell stays there, still shaped as if alive. Cicadas are said to spend a long time underground and to live only a short while once above; for all their vigorous crying, their time is unexpectedly short, and thinking of that, even the noisy racket starts to sound like real effort — singing with all they have through a short summer. As an adult I no longer catch them, but the sound still comes as a matter of course each summer, and every time I hear it, childhood summers come faintly back: the bug cage, the straw hat, the sweat-soaked afternoon. The cicada's cry is the most familiar sound of a Japanese summer — loud, stifling, and yet indispensable. How would visitors from abroad feel about this great chorus? They might find it noisy, or find it very much like a Japanese summer. But if you hear this sound, you are already right in the middle of one. (This is my own, older impression; the kinds, timing and number of cicadas differ greatly by area, environment and year, and this doesn't generalize; don't force-catch or weaken living things — observe, and gather shells, gently.)
