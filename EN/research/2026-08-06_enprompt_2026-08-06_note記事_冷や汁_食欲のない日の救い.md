【English】Hiyajiru — the rescue for a day with no appetite.

There are days when the heat kills any wish to eat — putting food in your mouth, even chewing, feels like a chore. On a day like that, the appetite dropped out from under you, set down a bowl of hiyajiru and, strangely, your chopsticks move: pour the cold miso soup over rice (cold rice, in our house) and slurp it down, and it slides down your throat — before you know it you've cleared a bowl. Hiyajiru, as the name says, is a cold soup: cold dashi with miso dissolved in, plus cucumber, myoga, shiso, and flaked fish or ground sesame, poured over rice — a food where soup and rice become one. Miyazaki is famous for it, they say, but a hot land's summer wisdom perhaps comes out similar everywhere.

Miso soup is a warm thing; to someone who thinks so, cold miso soup may be a little surprising. The first time I ate it I was half in doubt — "chill the miso soup and pour it on rice?" — but one bite changed my mind. The umami of cold dashi and the salt of miso; the crisp bite of cucumber; the clean scent of myoga and shiso; the toastiness of sesame — cold, and yet, or rather because it's cold, it goes down easily, entering even a summer-worn body that can't take anything else. I think hiyajiru is decided less by its "ingredients" than by its garnish: cucumber's texture, myoga's fragrance, shiso's freshness — those aromatic vegetables doing good work in the cold soup, and fish adding umami and body to make it a real feast. The method and ingredients differ greatly by house and region, so there's probably no "correct" hiyajiru; ours is our own, and that's fine — whatever garnish is in the fridge, chopped at random and tossed in, that looseness being part of what home cooking is.

Its chief charm is that you can shovel it in. Manners, honestly, aren't great: hold the bowl and slurp the soup-soaked rice into your mouth, a cold bowlful soaking through a body gone limp with heat. The less appetite you have, the more welcome something that goes down this smoothly — in fact I'd say it's good because you have no appetite: a heat-battered noon, and you go back for a second bowl, a third, as if that "I don't want anything" of a moment ago had never been. (Ingredients, method, garnish and origin vary by region and household; this is our one example. With raw fish and cold dishes, take great care with freshness and food safety.)
