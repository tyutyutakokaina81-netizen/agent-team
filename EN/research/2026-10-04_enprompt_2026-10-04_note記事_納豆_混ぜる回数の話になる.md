It Always Turns Into a Number

Bring up natto and the conversation goes to how many times you stir it. Fifty. A hundred. Four hundred. Everyone has a different figure and a reason to go with it. I do not count. I stop when the threads have gone white, which means the number is different every time, so when the counting talk starts I have nothing to offer and end up saying "a lot." The order of the sauce splits people too: I was taught to stir first and add the sauce after, so that is what I do, but plenty of people put it in at the start. I do not know which is right and have never tasted them side by side — I am simply continuing the order I was shown. What I have never got used to, despite eating it daily, is the packaging: the sachet, the film lid, and the difference between a day you peel it off cleanly and a day the threads end up on your fingers. Strands stay on the rim of the tray and you notice them again at the bin. There is no reason I could not eat it in the evening, but I rarely do; somewhere along the way it settled into being a breakfast thing, and I do not remember deciding that. It sits on the shelves here like anywhere else. People say the east of the country eats more of it — I have never found natto unusual in this prefecture, and I have never checked how big the difference actually is. Today, again, I did not count. I watched the threads and stopped.

何回混ぜるか。五十回か、百回か、四百回か。人によって数が違って、そのたびに根拠が出てくる。

## 自分では数えていない

私は数えていない。

糸が白くなってきたら止める、という止め方をしている。だから毎回ちがう回数のはずで、回数の話になると答えられない。答えられないまま「たくさん」と言って終わる。

## たれを入れる順番も割れる

先に混ぜてから、たれを入れる。

そう教わったので、そうしている。ただ、最初にたれを入れてから混ぜる人もいる。どちらが正しいのかは知らないし、食べ比べたこともない。教わった順番をそのまま続けているだけ。

## ふたの扱いが面倒

たれの袋と、ふたのフィルム。

あれをきれいに外せた日と、糸が指につく日がある。容器の縁に糸が残るので、捨てるときにもう一度気になる。毎日食べているのに、この手順だけは慣れない。

朝の食卓に出るものという位置づけが、どこかで固まっている。自分で決めた覚えはない。

Bring up natto and the conversation goes to how many times you stir it. Fifty. A hundred. Four hundred. Everyone has a different figure and a reason to go with it. I do not count. I stop when the threads have gone white, which means the number is different every time, so when the counting talk starts I have nothing to offer and end up saying "a lot." Strands stay on the rim of the tray and you notice them again at the bin. There is no reason I could not eat it in the evening, but I rarely do; somewhere along the way it settled into being a breakfast thing, and I do not remember deciding that. It sits on the shelves here like anywhere else. People say the east of the country eats more of it — I have never found natto unusual in this prefecture, and I have never checked how big the difference actually is. Today, again, I did not count.
