それに、夏は暑くて、台所に長く立ちたくない。だから、うちの夏の魚料理は、火を短く、さっぱりと、作りおきできるもの、が中心になる。今日は、うちの夏の、魚の食べ方を書いておく。どれも、ふつうの家の台所の話だ。

■ まずは、刺身

暑い日に、いちばん食べたくなるのは、やっぱり刺身だ。火も使わない。切って、盛るだけ。冷たくて、さっぱりしていて、暑さで落ちた食欲でも、するっと入る。

魚屋やスーパーで、その日に上がった魚を見て、良さそうなのを選ぶ。何が並ぶかは、その日の漁しだいだ。わさび醤油で食べてもいいし、富山には、刺身を昆布ではさんで少し寝かせる「昆布じめ」という食べ方もある。生ものだから、鮮度と、冷やす温度にはいちばん気をつける。

■ 漬け丼で、作りおき

刺身が少し余ったり、多めに買った日は、漬け丼にする。醤油とみりん(と、好みでしょうがやごま)に、刺身をしばらく漬けておくだけ。それを、熱いごはんにのせれば、漬け丼のできあがり。

漬けておくと、味がしみて、生とはまた違うおいしさになる。冷蔵庫で少し置けるので、忙しい日の昼にも、さっと出せる。暑い日に、火を使わずに、一杯でおなかが満ちる。夏の、たのもしい一品だ。

■ 南蛮漬けで、酢のさっぱり

もう少し手をかけるなら、南蛮漬け。小あじなどの小さい魚を、から揚げにして、酢・醤油・砂糖のタレに、玉ねぎと一緒に漬ける。揚げるときだけ火を使うけれど、あとは漬けておくだけだ。

酢が効いていて、暑い日でも、さっぱり食べられる。作りおきがきいて、冷蔵庫で数日もつ。むしろ、少し置いて味がなじんだほうがうまい。骨まで柔らかくなるので、小さい魚を、まるごといただける。夏の常備菜として、うちではよく作る。

焼きたてに、すだちやレモンをきゅっと。それだけで、夏のごちそうになる。手をかけすぎないのが、夏の魚のいいところだ。

【English】Summer fish, kept light.

Live in Toyama and fish is close at hand — Toyama Bay is right there, and each season brings something different up. Rich winter fish like buri (yellowtail) are fine, but in summer you want it light; on a hot day you don't much feel like sweating over a heavy simmered fish. And it's too hot to stand long at the stove, so our summer fish cooking leans toward short heat, light flavors, and make-ahead. Here's how we eat summer fish; it's all ordinary home-kitchen talk.

First, sashimi. On a hot day it's what you want most — no fire, just slice and arrange; cold and clean, it goes down even when the heat has killed your appetite. You look over what came in that day and pick what looks good — what's on offer depends on the day's catch — and eat it with wasabi and soy, or in Toyama's way of pressing sashimi between kelp to rest a little (kobujime). It's raw, so freshness and keeping it cold matter most of all.

If there's sashimi left over or you bought extra, make a zuke-don: just marinate the slices a while in soy and mirin (and ginger or sesame if you like), then lay them over hot rice. Marinating soaks in flavor, different from raw, and it keeps a bit in the fridge, so it's quick to serve on a busy day — a full bowl on a hot day with no fire, a reliable summer dish.

For a little more effort, nanban-zuke: small fish like ko-aji deep-fried, then marinated with onion in vinegar, soy and sugar. Fire only for the frying; after that it just soaks. The vinegar keeps it light even in the heat, it keeps for days in the fridge (better, in fact, once the flavor settles), and the bones soften so you eat the little fish whole — a summer make-ahead we often make. And if you grill, summer means shioyaki over a heavy simmer: salt and a quick grill, the fish's own taste at its clearest; hot while it cooks, but quickly done, with a squeeze of sudachi or lemon. Not overworking it is the point of summer fish.

Eating summer fish light — sashimi, zuke-don, nanban-zuke, shioyaki — none of it fancy, but eating fish that came up from the sea in front of you, in its season, is a plain but real luxury of living near the water. This is how a seaside Japanese home eats its summer fish, and I'd be glad if this light summer table came through. (Fish, availability and methods vary greatly by season, the day's catch and household; take care with raw-fish freshness and temperature, and with heat and oil.)
