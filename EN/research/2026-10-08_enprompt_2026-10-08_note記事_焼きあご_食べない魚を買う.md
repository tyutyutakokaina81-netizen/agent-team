Buying a Fish You Have No Intention of Eating

There is a fish in the vacuum-sealed bag. A whole one — head, tail, dried dark and shrunken. And wings: two large fan-shaped fins coming off the chest, ribbed with rays, folded like a closed fan. It is a flying fish, grilled and dried, and in Japanese the dried form is called ago.

I bought it, and I am not going to eat it.

It is not something you open the bag and eat, nor something you grill and eat. It is a dried good for making dashi. You put it in water and simmer; the savour moves into the liquid; and when it has finished moving, the fish itself is lifted out and thrown away. You buy a thing, use it, and discard it, and what remains is the water. You have bought food, and the food is not what you eat. Explaining it, I can hear how odd it sounds.

Dashi is the hardest part of Japanese cooking to explain. It is not soup — you do not drink it on its own, and on its own it is not finished. The nearest description I have is a taste laid underneath the cooking: underneath the miso in miso soup, underneath the soy in a simmered dish, underneath the broth in a bowl of udon. It never appears on the surface, and without it everything above it goes thin. People from abroad sometimes say Japanese food is light. There is certainly less oil. If it is light without being insufficient, I think this underlayer is why.

There is more than one way to make it. A Japanese kitchen keeps several dried things for the purpose. Kelp, which is dried seaweed — there is apparently a reason households around here keep so much of it, which I have written about. Katsuobushi, fish boiled, dried and cured until it is nearly as hard as wood, then shaved. Niboshi, small fish dried whole, which come out of the packet still fish-shaped. And ago. Different material, different water. So they are used for different things — "are used", though whether my own household ever observed the distinctions strictly is another matter.

About the wings: a flying fish breaks the surface and spreads its pectoral fins to glide, and that is what those fins are for. Dried, they stay attached, folded away in the bag like closed fans. A dried good for making stock, with the animal's way of moving still printed on it. It looks less like an ingredient than like a specimen. A child looking at it would not guess it was a fish.

Why dry it at all? Removing water stops it spoiling — that much I follow. It is also said that drying strengthens the savour, less by concentrating it than by turning it into something else. Said, because I do not properly understand the mechanism. Drying changes the taste of mushrooms and kelp too, and whether that is the same process I could not tell you. While I am at it, I cannot clearly explain what grilling before drying does that drying alone does not, beyond making it smell toasted.

Afterwards the fish is spent, hollow, its taste gone into the water, and most households throw it out. Some people do not: they flake the flesh, simmer it down, or simply eat it, mainly on the grounds that throwing food away is wasteful. By all accounts there is very little taste left. By all accounts, because I have never tried. Neither practice strikes me as the correct one.

The word ago itself I cannot account for. I have heard an explanation involving the jaw, and since I have not checked it I will leave it out. What I do know is that fish names vary from place to place; the same fish often carries different names in different regions, and even here there is confusion between sweet shrimp and white shrimp. So a bag labelled ago tells some people exactly what is inside and tells others nothing. I worked it out from the wings, not from the label.

Since the bag is unopened, I also have no idea how to use it. How much water, how many fish; whether to remove the heads and guts or put them in whole; whether to start from cold water or wait until it boils. All of that is written down somewhere, I am sure. But copying down a procedure I have never performed and presenting it as a procedure is precisely what I have decided not to do, so I will ask someone, or follow whatever the bag says, when the time comes.

One method I have heard of: before heating anything, put the fish in water and leave it overnight in the refrigerator, then bring it to the heat in the morning. Not hurrying is said to draw more out, and I have heard the same about kelp. I have never done it, so I cannot say whether it works. What strikes me is simply that "wait overnight" exists as a step at all — there are dishes where the waiting is longer than the cooking.

What I do remember is that making dashi changes the smell of the kitchen. Less a fish smell than a thickening of the steam, carrying to the back of the house. Granules smell too, but only at the moment they dissolve, and then it stops. From dried goods it goes on for as long as the pot is on, so you can follow the cooking with your nose. I suspect that is part of the reason people still do it. For taste alone the granules would do. The time spent smelling of something is not available in granule form.

Having written all that, I should be honest about how kitchens here actually work. Not many households make dashi from scratch every day. There are granules — a powder in a sachet or a jar that dissolves in hot water in seconds — and they are not bad; certainly not a problem on a weekday evening. My own household uses them most of the time. The dried goods come out when guests come, or at New Year, or when somebody feels like it. So if I wrote that people in Japan make dashi every day, that would be a lie. There was a time when they did. Now there isn't.

Then why buy the dried kind at all? Partly the taste: the granules are sufficient, but the aroma arrives differently. Partly, I suspect, the procedure itself — water, heat, a wait, then lifting it out. It takes time, and you make that time deliberately. On efficiency the granules win outright, and a few times a year the dried fish comes out anyway. It is that kind of object.

One more honest note: I have not opened this bag. So what is written here goes as far as "this is what ago is" and no further; there is not a word about what this particular ago tastes like. Writing about the taste of something you have not used turns the piece into an advertisement, and in this series I keep the two apart — I said the same thing about a knife. When I do use it, that will be its own piece, and it will be about how much water, how many fish, and how long. It will probably be shorter.

The bag is vacuum-sealed. Dried goods already keep, since keeping is what drying is for; sealed, they keep longer still. That suits sending things by post — nothing to refrigerate, nothing to break, and very little weight. This series keeps asking whether a thing survives being put in a box, and dried goods may be the clearest case of all, since they were invented to travel. Seen that way, the materials for dashi are a very old form of mail order: something taken from the sea, dried, and kept in a kitchen far from the sea.

When I took the photograph, a bottle of soy sauce and a small lidded pot happened to be beside the bag. The pot sits in a woven bamboo sleeve and has a character painted on the lid; inside are condiments. These sit on the table without anyone thinking about them, and lined up together they seem to face the same way: dashi, soy sauce, condiments — none of which is ever the main thing. Things that are never the main thing stay on the table permanently. The ago, I suspect, belongs with them.
