Toyama is a place where you can hear waves in the morning and be looking at a snow-topped ridgeline a bit over an hour later, and this piece is about using a car to do exactly that. 【English】Setting out on a drive — sea and mountains in a single day.

I woke at five, gave up on going back to sleep, and threw a cooler bag, a jacket and a towel onto the back seat while it was still half dark. Hardly enough to call luggage. When I started the engine, a low sound dropped into the quiet neighborhood and I felt faintly guilty about it. Sorry, everyone.

I hadn't decided where I was going.

Trips planned down to the minute are fine, but in my own case, car trips go better when I don't decide too much. I leave with something as rough as "toward the sea today," and work out the rest while driving. Often I pull into a roadside station, look at a map, and only then settle on where lunch will be. Strangely, the days that didn't go to plan are the ones I remember. Possibly that just means I'm bad at planning. I'd rather not examine it.

The thing I feel luckiest about, living in Toyama, is this: I can be driving along the coast, turn inland, and before long the mountains rise up ahead of me. From within earshot of the waves to a place where you can see snow on the ridgeline is a bit over an hour by car. There are far larger and grander landscapes in Japan, and I'm not about to claim this one is unique. Still — sea in the morning, mountains at midday, and home in time for my own bath in the evening. I hadn't imagined that scale of distance before I lived here. When I have a friend from out of the prefecture in the passenger seat, they almost always say the same thing at the same point: "Wait, mountains already?" Sometimes I take the long way around just to hear it.

On days when I'm not in a hurry, I get off the expressway one exit early. The ordinary roads have more lights and take longer. In exchange, you get to watch the color of the rice fields, the width of the rivers and the shape of the roofs change little by little. Even inside one prefecture, the face of the landscape shifts as you go. Once arriving quickly stops being the point, the road itself becomes the main character. Often the best part of the day turns out to be standing beside the car drinking vending-machine coffee. Nothing happens, and I remember it anyway.

A word about the car. Even after a long day of driving, my body isn't especially wrecked when I get out — it's quiet, the ride isn't jagged, and it stays composed on rough surfaces. That's what makes me think "maybe a little further." I suspect that's why my trips have gotten longer. But this is only what I feel, and I won't claim a good car is why fatigue doesn't come; condition, weather and traffic all mix into it. And a car that tires you less is no excuse to skip resting. I stop after roughly two hours, and if I'm sleepy I nap without arguing about it. However much the equipment helps, I'm the one holding the wheel. If I've had a drink, I don't drive. Ordinary rules, kept in the ordinary way.

Oddly, we talk on the way out and fall silent on the way back. The passenger seat is either asleep or watching the window. I put the radio on low and follow the scenery as it turns familiar again — the sea receding, the mountains moving around behind us, the signs I know starting to reappear. The days felt a little shorter than before. We don't say "that was fun" to each other. We just arrive home quietly. Lately I've come to think that silence is the best last part of a trip. When I pull into the garage and cut the engine, there's a faint sound from around the hood: the heat of one whole day, slowly leaving. From tomorrow, it goes back to being just the car I commute in.

(This is my personal impression, not a recommendation of any particular car. Please follow traffic law, take frequent breaks, and never drink and drive.)
