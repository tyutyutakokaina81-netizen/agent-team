【English】Fireflies light the riverbank — small, living lights in the dark.

On a summer night, small lights sometimes float over a dark riverbank — hotaru, fireflies. One glides along, glowing, then softly goes out; another lights up somewhere else. That soft light, several of them hovering in the dark — watching, you grow somehow quiet. Fireflies are said to be seen by clean-watered banks from early summer into summer; around here too they fly on summer nights where the water is clean, though the time and place vary a lot by the year and its weather.

Going to see them, you don't make much light — a bright flashlight makes their glow hard to see, and fireflies are said to dislike bright light, so you let your eyes adjust to the dark and wait quietly. After a while your eyes adjust and small lights appear in the dark, one, two, here and there; you follow a gliding light with your eyes, and reach out only to have it melt back into the dark. You don't try to catch it — you just watch. Their light is unlike fireworks or electricity: warm, soft, a living light, slowly brightening and dimming, never steady, and that flicker is beautiful past words. That it comes from a living body is, when you think of it, a wonder — a small insect making its own light, lighting up in the dark night and flying by it.

The season for them is short, just a brief stretch of early-to-mid summer, and by the year's weather and water there are years of many and years of few, they say — so when you do see them, you feel a little grateful. You leave them be; you don't catch and carry them home, since that light is beautiful because it flies over the dark bank. You watch a while in the dark, then go home — that much acquaintance suits fireflies. Watching, you notice the quiet — the river's murmur, insects in the grass, and the small lights in the dark; a quiet summer, apart from the noisy festivals and fireworks, is here. It's a quiet light of a Japanese summer, and I'd be glad if that stillness came through. (When, where and how many appear vary greatly by year, weather and habitat; a dark waterside can be dangerous underfoot — don't push your luck, be mindful of the creatures and the environment, and just watch quietly.)
