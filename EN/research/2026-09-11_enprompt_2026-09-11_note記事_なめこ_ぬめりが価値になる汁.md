Autumn puts mushrooms into the miso soup in Toyama kitchens, and the days with *nameko* in it are a little different: the soup itself goes thick. That thickness comes from the slime coating the mushroom — and slippery food, I gather, is not much of a selling point in most countries, so this is the dish I always stumble over when I try to explain it. The quality other people list as a drawback is the one being sold here. Nameko are small, brown-capped and wet-looking, already glossy inside the bag they come in. Dropped into miso soup they lend the whole bowl a viscous quality — closer to swallowing than to sipping — and they are usually paired with tofu, whose own smoothness blurs into theirs until it is hard to tell in the mouth where one stops. The point worth holding onto is that the slime is not something to be washed off; it is the reason the mushroom is there, and most people say they give nameko a quick rinse at most. Japan has a surprising number of foods like this — natto, grated yam, okra, mekabu seaweed — all valued for exactly the quality that would count against them elsewhere. Why that came about I genuinely don't know. Health claims get made and I will leave them alone. What does seem to be there is an extra axis for judging texture: not only firm and soft, but slick and thick as their own kind of good. It is, honestly, awkward at first — the mushrooms slide off your chopsticks, and the soup runs hotter than it looks. Nameko soup is also the shortest recipe of the season: stock, mushrooms, tofu, dissolve the miso, five minutes from deciding to eat. Set against chestnut rice that costs an hour of peeling, or persimmons left hanging for weeks, that is nearly the opposite proposition — and on a cold autumn morning the thickened version seems to hold its heat longer than the thin one. Japanese tables keep a category for things that are good precisely because they are slippery. Nameko sits at the near end of it, and comes out fastest.

## ぬめりが、目的である

なめこは小さくて、傘が茶色くて、全体が濡れたように光っている。売られている袋の中でも、すでにつるつるしている。

これを味噌汁に入れると、汁そのものにとろみがつく。飲むというより、少し飲み込む感じになる。豆腐と合わせることが多い。豆腐のつるっとした食感と、なめこのとろみが重なって、口の中でどこからどこまでが何なのか分からなくなる。

大事なのは、このぬめりが「洗い落とすべきもの」ではなく「食べるためのもの」だという点だ。だから、なめこは強く洗わない。さっとすすぐ程度にする、という人が多い。ぬめりを落としてしまったら、入れた意味が半分なくなる。

## 日本にはこの手のものが多い

考えてみると、日本の食卓にはぬるぬるしたものが妙に多い。納豆、とろろ、オクラ、めかぶ。どれも、粘りやぬめりがあることが価値になっている。

正直に言えば、慣れないうちは食べにくいと思う。汁の中で滑って箸から逃げるし、熱いのでうっかりすると口の中をやけどする。なめこの味噌汁は、思ったより熱い。

## 秋の、いちばん短い料理

なめこの味噌汁は、作るのに時間がかからない。だしを取って、なめこを入れて、豆腐を入れて、味噌を溶く。それだけだ。手のかかる秋の食べもの（栗ご飯や干し柿）とは正反対で、思い立ってから五分で出てくる。
