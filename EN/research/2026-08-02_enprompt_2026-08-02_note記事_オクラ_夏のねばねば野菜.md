Slice an okra into rounds and the cut face makes a little star — a vegetable, yet such a cute shape. Okra bears vigorously in summer; you eat the slim, fuzzy green pods young. Its defining trait is the "neba-neba" stickiness: chopped, it pulls threads, and that viscosity is oddly easy to eat in the heat, when appetite fails. Boil briefly, chop, add soy and bonito, and it slides down on rice or in somen, with popping seeds and a grassy scent. We often make a "neba-neba bowl" — okra with natto, yamaimo or myoga on rice, sticky things mingling so you can shovel it in on a hot day. Sticky vegetables are long said to help the summer body (details debated, but at least easy to eat). Okra takes many forms — ohitashi, raw sliced thin, tempura sealing the stickiness, grilled whole with salt; rubbing the fuzz with salt improves texture. Not a flashy vegetable, but stickiness and star slices make the table more fun, and on an appetite-less day the stickiness lets you eat — a small ally for summer. The pleasure of a sticky vegetable may be novel to visitors (some cultures dislike sliminess), but once you know that smooth slide in the heat you'll find it amusing Japanese summer wisdom — and do look at the little star. 【English】Slice an okra into rounds and the cut face makes the shape of a little star. I still remember how it somehow delighted me the first time I saw that cross-section — a vegetable, and yet such a cute shape.

Okra bears vigorously in summer; you harvest and eat the slim, fuzzy green pods while they're young. Its defining trait is, above all, that "neba-neba" stickiness: chop it and a distinctive, thread-pulling viscosity appears, and that stickiness is, oddly, easy to eat in the summer heat. In summer, heat kills the appetite, and that's when okra's stickiness helps: boil it briefly, chop it, add soy sauce and bonito flakes, and just that — on white rice or mixed into somen — slides right down, with the popping texture of the seeds and a fresh, grassy scent. At our house we often make a "neba-neba bowl" in summer, combining okra with natto, chopped yamaimo, or myoga and piling it on rice; the sticky things mingle, and even on a hot day you can shovel it in. Sticky vegetables are long said to have something that helps the summer body — the details seem debated, but at the least they're certainly easy to eat. Okra takes many forms: boiled as ohitashi is standard, but a fresh one can be sliced thin and eaten raw; as tempura the stickiness is sealed in for a different pleasure; grilled whole with just a sprinkle of salt tastes of the vegetable itself, which I like. If the fuzz bothers you, rubbing the pods with salt before washing improves the texture — a small extra step, but it makes them much easier to eat, and such prep is part of the summer kitchen scene. Okra isn't a flashy leading vegetable, but its stickiness and star-shaped slices make a summer table a little more fun; on a day with no appetite, the stickiness lets you eat — a small ally for getting through summer. For visitors from abroad, the sense of "eating a sticky vegetable with pleasure" may be novel — some cultures dislike sliminess, I hear — but once you know that smooth slide down the throat in the heat, I think you'll find it an amusing piece of Japanese summer wisdom. Do look at the little star shape, too. (Just my own way; uses and tastes vary by home; "stickiness beats summer fatigue" is loosely said, not stated here as fact; mind hygiene for raw eating and prep.)
