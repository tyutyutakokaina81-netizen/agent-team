【English】A summer rice field — a green wave and the frogs' chorus.

A summer rice field is blue-green enough to sting the eyes. The spindly seedlings planted in spring have grown, by summer, more than you'd think, the whole paddy filled with deep green; and when the wind sweeps through, the rice leaves all lean the same way at once, a great green wave running across the field. Toyama is rice country: step a little out of town and paddies spread here and there, so this blue-green summer scene is, to me, an utterly ordinary summer sight — yet look again, and that green is, for all that, a lovely thing.

The paddy's green isn't one steady green: each time the wind blows, the leaves show now their backs, now their faces, and the depth of green wavers; the green of a clear day and of a cloudy day differ too — the same field, yet each look a little different in expression. I especially love the paddy at evening, the rice bathed in the western sun glowing goldish, that mix of green and gold; heading home from an errand I sometimes stop short at the sight, over a paddy that's nothing special. A summer paddy is loud, especially from evening into night, when the frogs start up all at once — geko geko — that voice welling from all over the field into something like a great chorus; if you're not used to it, the sheer volume might surprise you a little. But to someone raised near paddies, that frog-voice is a kind of summer lullaby: you sleep to the geko geko at night, and though it should be noisy, somehow it settles you — too quiet, and you can't sleep — that lively voice of creatures on a summer night.

Watching the blue-green summer paddy, you vaguely think ahead to autumn: this green rice, drinking in the summer sun, will in time bow its head and ripen gold; this present blueness is a figure on the way to that autumn harvest. Of course, the labor of those who work the field is beyond my reckoning — the water management, the staring-match with the weather, all in their hands — and just gazing at the green in passing, calling it lovely, I feel a little apologetic. Still, that blue of a summer paddy is, after all, good.
