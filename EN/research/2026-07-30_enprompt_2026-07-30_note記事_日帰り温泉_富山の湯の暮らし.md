【English】Hot springs aren't a special occasion.

For many, "hot spring" means a special place you travel to. But live in Toyama and onsen are closer to hand — a land of mountains, starting with the Tateyama range, so day-use hot-spring baths are dotted all around. No trip needed; you can go for a soak on a whim. We go on a weekend evening or a tired day to a nearby day-onsen — not a grand reward, just a small breather in ordinary life.

The good thing is the ease: one towel, a short drive, and you're in a big bath. Many have open-air baths, and soaking in hot water with the outside air on you loosens you to the core. Winter, with snow beyond the open-air bath, is best — but even in summer, a clean soak after sweating and then cooling in the breeze feels good; each season has its own pleasure, and being day-use, a quick in-and-out is easy too.

And the pleasure isn't only the soak — it's the drink after. Our standard is bottled milk: hand on hip, a good gulp. Somehow onsen milk always tastes better; the cold seeps into a flushed body. The kids pick coffee milk or fruit milk. That after-bath drink completes the set — small, but it's why you want to go again.

While you're in the bath there's no phone, no errands — you just soak and space out. That "doing nothing" time turns out to matter in a busy life; you drift back over the day, your head quiets, and by the time you get out you're somehow lighter than when you came. An onsen loosens the mind as well as the body. Having one close by is a plain but real richness of rural life. Nothing special — soak, drink milk, space out — but I'd be glad if that ease came through. (Waters and facilities vary; mind overheating and dehydration, and don't bathe after drinking or when unwell.)
