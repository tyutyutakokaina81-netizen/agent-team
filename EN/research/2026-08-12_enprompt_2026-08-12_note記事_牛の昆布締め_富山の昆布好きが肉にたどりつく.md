【English】Beef kombu-jime — where Toyama's love of kelp finally reaches meat.

Lift the lid of the black tray and there, on dark green kombu (kelp), the marbled beef lies neatly in a row — red meat laced with fine white fat, each slice set flat against the kombu's moist surface. Beef and kelp. The first time I saw the pairing, honestly, it threw me a little. There's a thing called kombu-jime. In Toyama it's a fairly ordinary way to eat: fish you'd otherwise serve as sashimi is pressed between two sheets of kombu and left a while, so the kombu draws off some of the fish's moisture, firming the flesh, while the kombu's umami passes into the fish in return. This is the beef version of that.

Toyama, they often say, is a land that eats a lot of kombu. The kelp itself is caught in the northern seas, yet somehow it's consumed heavily here — a remnant, people say, of the old Kitamaebune ships that carried Hokkaido kombu to this region (the origins seem to be debated). So Toyama has many ways of using kombu, and kombu-jime is one, fish being the basic case. But in a place this fond of kombu, it's no surprise that someone, someday, thought "meat might work too" — and sure enough, beef kombu-jime is properly a thing.

You eat it raw, or nearly so. Pressed and rested between kombu, the meat's surface turns a little moist and carries a faint kelp scent; bite in and it's the sweetness of the beef fat first, then the kombu's slow umami coming up behind. A touch of soy, a little wasabi or ginger; the marbled fat melts on the tongue, and the kombu's savor layers over it — unlike fish kombu-jime, unlike ordinary grilled beef; the kombu doing quiet, good work behind the meat. Honestly, it's not an everyday thing: it uses good beef, and being eaten raw it wants care with freshness and handling, so even for us it's a small luxury of some occasion, not many times a year. Still, when it appears, I'm glad. So fond of kombu that fish alone wouldn't do, until at last they went and cured meat with it too — think of it that way and something of Toyama's sheer stubbornness about kombu seems packed into the plate, which makes me grin. The sea's gift of kombu, and wagyu — two utterly different things, meeting, coolly, through the single trick of kombu-jime. (Beef kombu-jime is eaten raw or nearly so, so freshness, hygiene and a trusted source matter; raw meat carries a risk of food poisoning, and young children, the elderly, pregnant people and anyone unwell should take special care. This is our one example and does not recommend any preparation. Kombu consumption and the Kitamaebune origin are debated, and this judges no region or brand as better.)
