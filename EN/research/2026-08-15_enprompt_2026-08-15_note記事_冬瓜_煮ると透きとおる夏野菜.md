【English】Tougan (winter melon) — a summer vegetable named for winter, that turns clear when you simmer it.

In the pot, the once-white melon had gone see-through without my noticing. I'd cut it into pieces, shaved the corners round, and let it simmer in a thin dashi. Look away for a while, lift the lid, and the slice that was as white as daikon now glows a pale jade, thin enough to let light through. Press it with your chopsticks and it sinks without any force at all.

You write "tougan" with the character for winter, yet it lines up in the shops through summer. Its season is said to be summer. When I first learned that, it threw me a little — I'd been sure it was a winter thing.

The name has several stories behind it, they say. The common one is that even harvested in summer, a whole melon keeps in a cool place all the way to winter. The skin is hard, the water stays sealed inside, and it holds across the seasons — so "the melon that keeps until winter" becomes tougan. Only one of several tellings, nothing I'd state as fact, but I like how a bit of old household wisdom seems to show through the gap between its name and its season.

Cut it open and the inside is pale as water. Almost no grassy smell, and honestly, almost no flavor to speak of. Bite it raw and it's just "a faint melon." That nothingness, I finally understood, is the melon's best quality. Being faint means it takes on whatever is around it. Tougan doesn't assert itself; instead it soaks the simmering dashi right into its flesh — bonito, chicken, or kombu and shiitake if you keep it meatless. Bite down and the broth comes flooding back. So eating simmered tougan, you're half eating the melon and half eating the dashi. At our place we simmer it in lightly seasoned dashi and spoon a ginger-thickened sauce over it — just our one way of doing it.

Being mostly water, cut pieces are said to spoil quickly, so I use them up soon — ordinary summer-kitchen caution. I couldn't tell you a thing about its health benefits. But when the heat kills your appetite, that faintness slips down easily, and the body is grateful. A single clear slice on the spoon, only the taste of dashi unraveling softly. Named for winter, in season in summer, with almost no flavor of its own — and still it has a place at the summer table. I'm rather fond of this quiet melon.
