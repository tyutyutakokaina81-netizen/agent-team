【English】In midsummer, everyone comes home.

In the middle of summer comes Obon. For us it means, above all, the time when everyone comes home — siblings who left for the city, relatives who live far away, all gathering back at the family house. A normally quiet home suddenly fills up. Obon is said to be the time you welcome the spirits of your ancestors home and then send them off again. Honestly, we're not experts in the finer rites; but the feeling — family gathering and remembering those who've died — is probably the same in any household.

There's a day to welcome and a day to send off. We tidy the household altar and offerings and go together to visit the family grave, flowers and incense in hand, and everyone puts their hands together, children included. The timing and customs vary a lot by region and family — some observe it in July, some in August; some light a "welcoming fire," some don't. There's no single "correct" way; Obon is each household's own way of doing it.

The best part is the faces you haven't seen in a while — nephews and nieces grown taller, siblings a little older. After the altar, everyone gathers around the table for rambling talk ("you've gotten big," "is that shop still there?") deep into the night. Family who usually live apart are under one roof for these few days; remembering the dead becomes a time that reconnects the living.

And there's bon-odori — the Obon dance. At night a tower goes up in a square, lanterns light, and to the drum everyone forms a circle and dances; skill doesn't matter, you just follow along. Small children and the elderly share the same loose, open circle — that's its charm. They say you're dancing together with the ancestors who've come home; the living and the dead in the same summer night. When Obon ends and everyone returns to their own lives, the house goes quiet again — a little lonely, but there's always next year. This is how a Japanese home meets its dead in summer, and I'd be glad if this "coming home, welcoming and sending off" came through. (Timing and customs vary greatly by region and household; this is only our way.)
