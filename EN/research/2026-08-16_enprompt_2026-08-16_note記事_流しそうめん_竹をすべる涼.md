【English】Nagashi-somen — a summer's day sliding down bamboo.

Today I want to write about nagashi-somen. In the back garden my father would lay out a length of bamboo, split in half. He'd gouge the joints out with a metal skewer, sand the inside smooth, and prop the chute at a gentle slope on a stand rigged from a stepladder and a laundry pole. When water poured down from the top, the noodles slid along the bottom of the trough and dropped like a tiny waterfall into the tub below. The moment I saw it, I thought: ah, it's summer. Chopsticks in hand, I fought hard for the best spot downstream.

Nagashi-somen is less a meal than a game. Served on a plate, it's gone in three minutes. Instead you send it down bamboo and catch it as it flows. Sometimes you miss — you just let out a small "ah" and watch it slip past. There's nothing less efficient, and yet that waste was what made it fun. I can't say our way was the correct one; how you build the stand and run the water varies by home and by area.

Whoever stands upstream holds a quiet power — the one who lifts the noodles from the pot and floats them down, mouthful by mouthful. Usually that was my father: the spacing, the amount, the timing, all up to his mood. Now and then he'd tease us, sending a huge clump at once, and downstream turned into a laughing scramble of clashing chopsticks. Then nothing would come for a while, and even that waiting had its own strange pleasure.

One serious note. Always use clean, drinkable water — outdoors it's easy to reach for whatever water is nearby, but untreated or unknown water shouldn't go down the chute. Fresh-split bamboo keeps splinters, so the inside, where food passes, should be sanded smooth. It's a food that touches water directly, and that's the one part I wouldn't cut corners on.

Honestly, the noodles don't taste special — probably no different from a plate. What you're really eating is the sound of the water, the green smell of bamboo, the waiting, the small triumph of catching one. When the water stops, the noisy chute goes quiet, and my father tips it to empty the last of the water. Nagashi-somen, I think now, wasn't a way of eating noodles. It was a way of letting one whole day of summer slide by, together.
