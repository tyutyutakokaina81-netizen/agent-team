【English】Zaru soba — just a little dip in the tsujiru.

With a good slurp — zzzt — you pull the soba in. Lift a mouthful of the cold noodles with your chopsticks, dip just the tips into the dipping sauce, and haul them briskly into your mouth, and they slide down your throat. On a hot day, few things go down this smoothly. You don't chew soba much — "taste it with your throat, not your teeth," they even say; whether that's true I don't know, but that slide down the throat really is one of soba's chief pleasures, and a cold zaru soba at a summer noon settles you a little just by arriving.

There's a sort of etiquette to the dip: not soaking the whole strand, but touching just the tip lightly into the sauce — "the stylish way is only a little sauce," you often hear. Though there are other views — some say plenty is better — so in the end it's taste. Me, it depends on the mood: with good soba I dip a little, to catch the aroma; hungry, I soak it deep, and never mind stylish. The garnish does fine work: chopped scallion and wasabi, stirred into the sauce — the scallion's crisp bite, the wasabi's sharp rush up the nose — and with just those two the sauce tightens right up. How much wasabi is the tricky part: overdo it and the back of the nose stings till your eyes water — and yet that near-painful jolt is habit-forming, so now and then I misjudge, writhe, and put it in again anyway. I don't learn.

Zaru soba's hidden pleasure is the last of it: soba-yu, the cloudy white water the noodles were boiled in. Pour it into the leftover sauce and drink — the sauce, steeped with soba's flavor, thinned by the warm water to just the right strength, is quietly good. Noodles finished, you pour the soba-yu, and with that closing cup the meal feels properly ended: a smooth cold start, a warm, settling finish. That flow, well — it's pretty good. (The dip, the garnish and how you take soba-yu vary by region, person and view; this is our one example. Those with a soba allergy, take care, and mind food safety.)
