【English】On hot days, we don't use the stove.

On a hot summer day even standing in the kitchen is rough — stir-frying at the burner, the sweat won't stop. So in the peak of summer our meals drift toward "no-fire cooking." Call it lazy if you like, but on a hot day it's the most sensible thing. Here are our no-cook summer meals; each is barely a recipe, yet plenty good when it's hot.

First, somen. Strictly there's a moment of heat to boil it, but no frying or simmering — boil, chill in cold water, eat with dipping sauce. That's it. Add chopped negi, ginger, myoga and shiso from the garden and it's much better; it slips down the throat, so you can eat it even with no appetite. The kids love it, and days of "somen again" aren't rare in summer.

Then cold tofu (hiyayakko): turn chilled tofu into a dish, pour soy sauce, top with bonito flakes, negi, ginger. That alone is a proper dish. Tofu has taste on its own, goes down smoothly when it's too hot to eat, and gives you protein for the heat — and you barely need a knife or board, so there's little washing up, a blessing in summer.

With somen or cold tofu, plus cold rice, natto, a raw egg and pickles, it's already a fine summer table. The only fire is cooking rice (and that's done in the morning). Just take things from the fridge and lay them out — little effort, but the nutrition is surprisingly fine, and cold, light food slips into a sweaty, tired body. "No fire" sounds lazy, but if the cook wilts making an elaborate meal in the heat, what's the point? Hot days have their own food — set it out, eat it, keep a cool face; that too is a way to get through summer. This is how a Japanese home rides out a hot summer — lazy, maybe, but everyday food like this is what a place's life actually is, and I'd be glad if a little came through. (Take care with raw egg, tofu and fresh garnishes; ways vary by household.)
