【English】The sound of Japan is cast in Takaoka.

On New Year's Eve in Japan, temple bells ring out 108 deep, slow tones — a sound many people carry in their memory as "the sound of Japan." What is less known is that a great many of those bells are said to be cast in Takaoka, a city in Toyama. For about four hundred years, since a feudal domain invited metal casters here in the early Edo period, Takaoka has melted, poured and hammered copper and brass into tools and Buddhist wares — a craft now recognized as a traditional Japanese handicraft. In the old Kanaya-machi quarter, stone-paved lanes and latticed houses still remember the casters who lived there.

What I find striking, as a resident, is that these artisans make not only shapes but sound. A temple bell's thickness and form decide how it rings and how long the note lingers, so bell-making is as much about designing a sound as a shape. The same is true of the orin, the small clear-toned bell struck at home altars. Some workshops here now cast orin and wind chimes from tin, with a startlingly pure tone — even the chime ringing under a summer eave is an extension of this town's craft.

The town's great bronze Buddha, its bells, its wind chimes — all are siblings born of the same casting craft. So the bell you once heard at some distant temple, or the orin you found in an inn, may well trace back here. Not every bell is from Takaoka, and there are other centers — but as someone who lives here, I feel a quiet pride in it.

When you visit, don't only look; listen. The dawn strike of a temple bell, the dry ring of metal from a workshop, the long thin fade of an orin at a shop counter. This town's finest sight may, in fact, be a sound. You can't photograph it or carry it home — but a sound you've heard tends to stay with you. I'd love for visitors from abroad to come and find where the "sound of Japan" is born.
