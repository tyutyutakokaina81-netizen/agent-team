【English】Lights drifting across the water.

At the end of Obon, some regions do toro-nagashi. Having welcomed the ancestors home, to send them off again you light a small lantern and set it gently on the water of a river or the sea. How it's done, what it's called, whether it's done at all — all vary by place, but ours is one of the regions with this way of sending off.

After dark, people gather at the water's edge. One lantern, then another, is set on the water, and the lights begin to drift slowly out. It's the opposite of loud fireworks — quiet, a little lonely, and somehow indescribably beautiful. The lanterns are small, a paper or wood float a bit bigger than your palm, with a light on it; you crouch at the water's edge, let go softly, and it sways, then slowly moves off. As it leaves your hands and travels over the water, everyone watches it go in silence. They say you're seeing off the ancestors' spirits, but what each person is really picturing is probably their own lost, dear one.

One alone is a small light. But as many lanterns are set adrift from all along the bank, the dark water fills with drifting lights, moving off in a slow line; watching that band of light from the shore, something in your chest goes quiet. It's utterly unlike a lively festival — the lights are bright, yet somehow lonely, and maybe that is what "sending off" is. When the lanterns dwindle and finally vanish, it's over, and people head home; the ancestors sent off, a quiet break falls on the summer. (In recent years, out of care for the environment, many places have changed how it's done — collecting the lanterns downstream, or only lighting them without setting them adrift; ours has slowly changed too.) Sending off is lonely, but having a place to do it and someone to remember is probably also a kind of happiness, and toro-nagashi is a time of feeling both at once. This is how a Japanese summer sees off its dead — with light on water — and I'd be glad if the quiet of those many small lights drifting over the dark water came through. (Timing and customs vary greatly by region and sect, and are changing for the environment; this is only our area's way.)
