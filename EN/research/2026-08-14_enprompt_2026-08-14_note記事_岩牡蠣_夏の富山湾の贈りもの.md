【English】Iwagaki — summer's rough-shelled gift from Toyama Bay.

It was a morning when the smell of the tide still hung in the alleys. On a beach around Himi, chunky iwagaki (rock oysters) lay heaped on ice in a foam box. The shells were grey, chipped, crusted with barnacles and bits of seaweed. Honestly, not pretty — more like plain stones. And yet, locals like to say, the whole of Toyama Bay's summer is packed inside those ugly shells.

Many people picture oysters in winter — the small ones for hotpot or frying, usually farmed, in season when it's cold. Iwagaki are a different kind. They are often said to be in season in summer, roughly from early to high summer. Mostly wild, larger in both shell and meat than the winter sort; some sit heavy in the palm. If the winter oyster tastes tight and firm, the summer iwagaki is said to be rounder, with a milky richness. Taste is personal, though, and it varies a lot by the individual oyster — take this as just one household's impression.

I'll confess I'm bad at shucking them. The shell is thick and stubborn; you slip a knife near the hinge and twist, and my hand keeps slipping. Even in work gloves the shell edges are sharp enough to cut. My first year I shattered the shell into gravel that got into the meat. Skilled people open one cleanly in seconds — that's a craft I still don't have.

Most people love them raw: a squeeze of lemon, the seawater in the shell, one mouthful, the scent of the tide rising through your nose. But let me be plain: raw shellfish always carries a risk of food poisoning — norovirus, shellfish toxins, bacteria. Choose fresh ones sold for raw eating from a source you trust, and if you have any doubt, grill or steam them instead; grilled oysters plump up and are good in their own way. People who are pregnant, young children, the elderly, anyone unwell, and those with weak immune systems are safer avoiding raw shellfish. The tastier the thing, the more careful the judgment just before it.

Pry the shell open and the inside gleams pearl-grey, a big piece of meat set in the hollow. Rough outside, smooth within — that gap surprises me a little every year. A beach auntie, watching me struggle, laughed and opened one for me. "You're clumsy," she said, and I had no reply. The oyster that hides summer inside a stone-like shell — I still can't open it well.
