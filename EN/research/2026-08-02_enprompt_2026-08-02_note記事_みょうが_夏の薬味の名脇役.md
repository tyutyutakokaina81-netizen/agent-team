【English】When it comes to summer yakumi (aromatic garnishes), shiso and green onion are the standards — but I'd really like myoga to be counted among them too.

With myoga you eat that plump, reddish-purple flower bud. Chop it and a distinctive, clean, sharp fragrance rises; bite it and there's a faint bitterness and a crisp, crunchy texture. That fragrance and light bitterness suit a hot-weather table surprisingly well. Its main role is as a garnish: chopped over somen noodles, over chilled tofu, scattered into miso soup — just that makes a dish feel much more like summer, and even on a day too hot for an appetite, the scent of myoga makes the somen slide right down. A garnish isn't the star, but you miss it when it's gone; myoga is exactly that kind of fine supporting player. Beyond a raw garnish, pickled in sweet vinegar it turns a pretty pink, just right as a palate cleanser; as tempura the bitterness softens into something mellow. The same myoga shows many faces depending on how you use it. What's nice is that it's very easy to grow — even in a poorly-lit corner of a field or garden it quietly multiplies, and in summer the flower buds pop up along the ground; finding and picking them is a little like a treasure hunt. It comes back every year without fuss — not a flashy vegetable, not so much that you're overwhelmed — but when you've run out of garnish in summer, having myoga in the garden is very reassuring. There's an old saying that eating myoga makes you forgetful; at our house, whenever it's served, someone always jokes "eat too much and you'll get absent-minded." Of course it's just an old wives' tale with no scientific basis, but the joke comes out together with the dish. Fragrance, light bitterness, and a little bit of folklore — maybe myoga is something you enjoy along with the whole air of a summer table, not just the taste. For visitors from abroad that distinctive scent might be puzzling at first, but put it on somen and take a bite, and I think you'll remember it as "the taste of a Japanese summer." (Just my own way; uses and tastes vary by home and area; "eating it makes you forgetful" is a groundless old superstition, not stated here as fact; scent preference varies by person.)
