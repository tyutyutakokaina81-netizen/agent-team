【English】Scooping a life with a paper net.

Of all the night-stalls at a summer festival, the one kids always want is kingyo-sukui — goldfish scooping. Red goldfish dart through a shallow tank, and you crouch in front with a small hoop covered in thin paper — a poi — and try to scoop one out. That's the whole game, yet somehow everyone gets serious. Ours stops first at the goldfish stall every festival, pays a few hundred yen, takes a poi, and crouches at the tank. Here's a note on it.

Because the poi is just thin paper on a hoop, it softens the moment it hits the water and tears. Chase a goldfish and jerk the net and the paper rips at once — game over. The trick, they say, is to move gently, lifting the fish at an angle where the paper meets little water resistance; but knowing it and doing it are different, and the instant a goldfish is right there you tense up and press too hard. "Ah, it tore!" — and that frustration is part of it too.

Scoop one before the paper goes and a child lights up. The stall keeper bags the goldfish with some water, and the red fish sways in the bag as you carry it home like treasure, a bag of goldfish in one hand on the way back from the festival — one of the set images of a Japanese summer. A few hundred yen of play, but the one you scooped becomes a special one.

Home, you scramble out a bowl or tank and fill it; from that day the fish is one of the family. Honestly, festival goldfish are sensitive to change and can weaken within days — or, well cared for, live for years and grow surprisingly big (it varies, and views differ). Either way, once you've scooped it and brought it home, you see it through: for a child that can be a first lesson in taking charge of a life. It's only a few hundred yen, but the breath-held few minutes of chasing a fish without tearing the paper aren't measured in money; caught or not, the time itself becomes a festival memory. I'd be glad if the thrill of scooping a life with a paper net came through. (Methods and a goldfish's care and lifespan vary greatly; if you bring a living thing home, see it through responsibly.)
