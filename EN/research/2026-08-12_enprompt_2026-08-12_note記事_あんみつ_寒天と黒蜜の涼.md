【English】Anmitsu — the cool of agar and black-sugar syrup.

Scoop with a spoon and the cubed agar (kanten) slips away, tsurun. In the glass dish, clear agar, white shiratama (rice-flour dumplings) and brown anko (sweet bean paste) sit neatly in a row, and over them you pour kuromitsu (black-sugar syrup) in a thick drizzle — chilled anmitsu. One spoonful and the agar's smooth cold and the syrup's rich sweetness come all at once; on a hot day, this is a treat. Anmitsu packs many tastes and textures into a single dish: smooth agar, chewy shiratama, sticky anko, plus fruit like mikan or canned peach, and sometimes a salty green pea or two — you scoop these scattered things together with a spoon and eat.

One pleasure is the moment you pour the syrup. We're the kuromitsu kind — that rich, deep sweetness of black sugar, which, clinging to the agar and anko, is irresistible; drizzled on, the agar glistens black and glossy. But some like the lighter shiromitsu (clear syrup); this, too, divides people, and kuromitsu's richness and shiromitsu's clarity are each good — which to pour, you dither a little before the dish, and that dithering, too, is part of the fun. The star, I think, is the agar: it has almost no taste of its own, just smooth and cool, and that tastelessness is the good of it — without getting in the way of the surrounding anko and syrup, it adds only a cold texture. That decisiveness of the agar keeps anmitsu a cool sweet that isn't too heavy; anko alone is a touch cloying, but with the tasteless agar in it, the whole pulls together clean, eaten smoothly even on a hot day — that coolness is thanks to the agar.

Anmitsu isn't something to down in one go like shaved ice; with a spoon you savor various combinations little by little — this time agar and anko, next shiratama and syrup, then with fruit — each scoop a little different, and you enjoy the shifts of taste within the one dish. Not a showy sweet, but many tastes and textures quietly in harmony in a single vessel; on a hot afternoon, savoring cold anmitsu by the spoonful, that careful sweet time makes a fine pause of summer. (Ingredients, syrup and arrangement vary by taste and view; this is our one example. Chew shiratama and mochi well — young children and the elderly should take care not to choke — and mind storage and food safety of cold sweets.)
