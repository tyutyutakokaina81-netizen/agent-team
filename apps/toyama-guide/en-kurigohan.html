Japan Doesn't Make Chestnuts Sweet

Ask someone outside Japan what a chestnut becomes and the answer is usually a dessert: marrons glacés, Mont Blanc, roasted and sugared. In Japan the most common autumn treatment is different — the chestnuts go into the rice. In Toyama kitchens from September, kurigohan gets cooked: rice, chestnuts, a little salt. That is essentially the whole recipe.

Choosing not to sweeten it

Some households add a little glutinous rice or a splash of sake, but no sugar goes in. What you taste instead is two different kinds of sweetness sitting side by side — the rice's and the chestnut's. The explanation you hear is that sugar would flatten the difference between them; whether that is really why, it's hard to say, though one bite makes it plausible. Chestnuts vary too: some crumble floury, some stay dense and moist, depending on variety and how they cook. The same kitchen gets a different result in a different year.

The hard part is the peeling

Honestly, kurigohan is a chore. A chestnut has a hard outer shell and a bitter inner skin, and both have to come off — the shell prised away with the heel of a knife, the inner skin pared off after. It takes time and leaves your fingers sore. Soaking them overnight first, to soften things, is a common trick. So nobody makes this daily. It happens a few times during the short weeks chestnuts are around, and the quiet hour of peeling at the counter has become part of the dish itself.

Food that costs you something

By any measure of convenience it doesn't add up: an hour of peeling for ten minutes of eating. People still do it, probably because the hour is the season. The thing worth passing on is simply this — in Japan the chestnut doesn't end up in a pastry case. It ends up in a bowl of white rice, with no sugar and no cream. If a bowl is ever set in front of you here, take the first mouthful plain, and find out how far rice, salt and chestnut can go on their own.

Fact-check note
  • ✅ Kurigohan = rice cooked with chestnuts; salt is the standard seasoning and sugar is not added.
  • ✅ Adding glutinous rice or sake varies by household and region.
  • ✅ Chestnuts have a hard outer shell and a bitter inner skin; peeling is laborious, and soaking is a known trick.
  • ⚠️ "No sugar, so the two sweetnesses stay distinct" is an explanation people give, not an established fact.
  • ⚠️ Texture (floury vs moist) varies by variety and cooking — a general tendency.
  • ⚠️ "An hour of peeling" is a rough figure depending on quantity and practice.
