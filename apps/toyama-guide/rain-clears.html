序Prologue

三月の光The March Light

◦

電車が海沿いのカーブに差しかかると、窓の外がふいに明るくなった。

富山湾だった。

遥は、膝の上で握っていたスマートフォンから顔を上げた。灰色をしていると思い込んでいた三月の海は、思いのほか青くて、いくつもの小さな光が波の上で跳ねていた。その光の奥、水平線のさらに向こうに、白いものが浮かんでいる。

最初、雲だと思った。

けれど雲にしては輪郭がはっきりしすぎていて、動かなかった。青い海の上に、まるで空へ切り出された城壁のように、雪をかぶった山並みが連なっている。海の向こうに、山がある。それも、こんなに高い山が。東京で暮らした七年のあいだに、遥はすっかり忘れていた。ここでは、海と山が同じ景色のなかに入ってしまうということを。

立山連峰だ、と胸の奥で誰かの声がした。祖母の声だった。

子どもの頃、この電車に祖母と乗ったことがある。同じ席に、同じように海を見ながら座っていた。あのとき祖母は、遥の小さな手を自分の膝の上に載せて、「あれが立山やちゃ」と言った。剱岳、大日、薬師──ひとつひとつの名前を、まるで親戚の名でも読み上げるように教えてくれた。遥はそのほとんどを覚えていない。覚えているのは、祖母の手の乾いた温かさと、窓ガラスに触れた指先が思いのほか冷たかったことだけだ。

その祖母が、先月、亡くなった。

享年八十三。眠るように、と親戚は言った。実際、祖母は自分の布団のなかで、朝、目を覚まさなかった。台所には前の晩に研いだ米が水に浸けてあって、鍋には昆布が一枚、出汁を取るために沈められたままだったという。祖母はその朝も、いつものように味噌汁をつくるつもりでいたのだ。

葬儀に、遥は帰らなかった。

正確に言えば、帰れなかった。ちょうどその週、遥が三か月かけて準備していた大きなプレゼンがあった。上司には「祖母が亡くなって」と伝えたが、上司はほんの一瞬だけ表情をゆるめ、それから当然のように、「それは……日程、なんとかならない?」と言った。遥は「大丈夫です」と答えた。自分でも、なぜそう答えたのか、いまだにわからない。

プレゼンは、通らなかった。

クライアントは席を立つ前に、遥のつくった三か月分の資料を一瞥もせず、「なんか、思ってたのと違うんだよね」とだけ言った。思ってたのと違う。その一言のために、遥は年末年始も土日も机に向かっていた。抗議する気力はなかった。ただ、会議室を出たあと、廊下の窓に映る自分の顔を見て、この顔は誰だろう、と思った。目の下に濃い隈があって、頬がこけていて、口の両端が、笑い方を忘れた人のように下がっていた。

その夜、遥は熱を出した。

三十九度の熱のなかで、遥は祖母の台所の夢を見た。昆布の匂いのする、朝の台所。窓から差し込む海の光。祖母の背中。「ごはんやよ」という声。目を覚ましたとき、枕がぐっしょり濡れていて、遥は自分が泣いていたことに気づいた。

会社には、辞表を出した。

引き止められることを、少しだけ期待していた。けれど上司は、辞表を受け取ると、「そっか。まあ、無理させちゃったもんな」と言って、それきりだった。七年間、遥という人間がその会社に残したものは、その一言ぶんの重さしかなかった。

こうして遥は、二十九歳の春、何も持たずに故郷へ帰ってきた。

祖母の遺した家を片づけるため、というのが表向きの理由だった。両親はとうに離れた土地で新しい暮らしを営んでいて、この家に関わりたがらなかった。「あんたが行くなら任せる」と母は電話口で言った。「どうせ、あんたが一番おばあちゃんっ子やったんやし」

一番おばあちゃんっ子。その言葉が、帰らなかった葬儀のことと重なって、遥の胸を細く刺した。

電車がトンネルに入り、海が消えた。

窓ガラスに、遥の顔が映った。三十九度の夜に見た、あの見知らぬ顔よりは、少しだけましになっている。それでも、目の奥にはまだ、東京の会議室の蛍光灯の白さが残っていた。

トンネルを抜けると、また海が現れた。

そして、駅が近づいてきた。

小さな、無人の駅だった。ホームのすぐ向こうが海で、ホームと海のあいだには、ただ低いコンクリートの柵と、松の木が数本あるきりだった。駅名標に、ひらがなで駅の名が書いてあった。

あまはらし。

雨が、晴れる。

遥はその名を、口のなかで小さく繰り返した。子どもの頃、この駅名を不思議な言葉だと思っていた。雨が晴れる、なんて、まるで願いごとみたいだ、と。

電車が停まった。

降りるのは、遥ひとりだった。

ドアが開くと、海の匂いが車内になだれ込んできた。潮の匂いと、少しの藻の匂いと、それから、どこか遠くから漂ってくる、コーヒーの匂い。

遥はスーツケースを引いて、ホームに降り立った。

三月の光が、海の上いっぱいに散らばっていた。その光の向こうに、さっき電車から見た白い山並みが、いまはもっと近く、もっとくっきりと見えた。海があって、その向こうに雪の山があって、そのあいだを、光が埋めていた。

遥は、しばらくそこに立っていた。

スーツケースの持ち手を握ったまま、動けなかった。

なぜ動けないのか、遥にはわからなかった。ただ、その景色を見ていると、胸の奥のほうで、長いあいだ固く結ばれていた何かが、ほんの少しだけ、ゆるむのを感じた。それは痛みに似ていた。凍えていた指先が温まるときのような、あの、痛みに似た感覚だった。

風が吹いて、松の枝が鳴った。

遥は目を細めて、海の向こうの山を見た。

「ただいま」

誰にともなく、遥はそう言った。

その声は、波の音にすぐにかき消された。それでも、言ってしまえば、少しだけ楽になった。

こうして、遥の帰郷の日々が始まった。

この町で、遥がひとりの男と出会うことを、この日の遥はまだ知らない。

海越しの立山を、いつか誰かと並んで見る朝が来ることも──まだ、知らない。

When the train swung into the curve along the coast, the world outside the window turned suddenly bright. It was Toyama Bay. Haruka lifted her eyes from the phone she had been gripping in her lap. The March sea, which she had assumed would be grey, was bluer than she expected, and small points of light leapt and scattered across the waves. Beyond that light, far past the horizon, something white was floating. At first she thought it was a cloud. But it was too sharply drawn to be a cloud, and it did not move. On top of the blue sea, like a rampart cut into the sky, a range of snow-covered mountains stood in a long line. Beyond the sea, there were mountains. And such high mountains, at that. In the seven years she had lived in Tokyo, Haruka had forgotten this completely — that here, the sea and the mountains could fit inside the same view. The Tateyama Range, said a voice somewhere deep in her chest. It was her grandmother's voice. When she was a child, she had ridden this train with her grandmother. She had sat in the same seat, looking at the same sea. Her grandmother had rested Haruka's small hand on her own lap and said, "That's Tateyama, that is." Tsurugi, Dainichi, Yakushi — she had read off the names one by one, the way you might recite the names of relatives. Haruka remembered almost none of them. What she remembered was the dry warmth of her grandmother's hand, and how the fingertips she pressed to the window glass had been surprisingly cold. That grandmother had died last month. She had been eighty-three. As if falling asleep, the relatives said. And in fact, her grandmother had simply not woken up one morning in her own bed. In the kitchen, rice she had rinsed the night before was soaking in water, and in the pot a single sheet of kombu had been left sunk to draw the stock. Her grandmother had meant to make miso soup that morning too, as she always did. Haruka had not gone home for the funeral. To be precise, she had not been able to. That very week, she had a major presentation she had spent three months preparing. She told her manager, "My grandmother has died," and for just an instant his face softened — and then, as though it were the most natural thing in the world, he said, "That's… can't the schedule be moved?" Haruka said, "It's fine." Even now she does not know why she answered that way. The presentation did not go through. Before the client stood up, he did not so much as glance at the three months of materials Haruka had made. "It's just — not quite what we had in mind," was all he said. Not quite what we had in mind. For that one sentence, Haruka had sat at her desk through the New Year holidays and every weekend. She had no strength left to protest. Only, walking out of the meeting room, she caught her own face in the corridor window and thought: whose face is that? There were dark hollows under the eyes, the cheeks were sunken, and the corners of the mouth turned down, like a person who had forgotten how to smile. That night, Haruka ran a fever. In the middle of a thirty-nine-degree fever, she dreamed of her grandmother's kitchen. A morning kitchen that smelled of kombu. The light of the sea through the window. Her grandmother's back. The voice that said, "It's ready." When she woke, her pillow was soaked through, and Haruka realized she had been crying. She handed in her resignation. A small part of her had hoped to be stopped. But her manager took the letter and said, "I see. Well — I did push you too hard," and that was all. Whatever Haruka the person had left behind in that company over seven years weighed exactly as much as that single sentence. And so, in the spring of her twenty-ninth year, Haruka came home with nothing. The official reason was to clear out the house her grandmother had left. Her parents had long since built a new life in a distant place and wanted nothing to do with this house. "If you'll go, then it's yours to handle," her mother said over the phone. "You were always the one closest to Grandma, anyway." The one closest to Grandma. That phrase, overlapping with the funeral she had not attended, pierced Haruka's chest in a thin, fine line. The train entered a tunnel, and the sea vanished. Haruka's face appeared in the window glass. It was a little better, at least, than that stranger's face she had seen on the night of the fever. And yet, deep in the eyes, the white of the fluorescent lights of that Tokyo meeting room still lingered. The train came out of the tunnel, and the sea appeared again. And then a station drew near. It was a small, unstaffed station. Just beyond the platform was the sea, and between the platform and the water there was only a low concrete rail and a few pines. On the station sign, the name was written in hiragana. Amaharashi. The rain clears. Haruka repeated the name quietly in her mouth. As a child she had thought this station name a strange phrase. The rain clears — as if it were a wish. The train stopped. She was the only one getting off. When the doors opened, the smell of the sea came pouring into the car. The smell of the tide, and a little of seaweed, and from somewhere far off, the smell of coffee. Haruka stepped down onto the platform, pulling her suitcase. The March light was scattered across the whole surface of the sea. Beyond that light, the white range she had seen from the train stood now closer, and far more clearly. There was the sea, and beyond it the snow mountains, and the light filled the space between them. For a while, Haruka simply stood there. She could not move, her hand still on the handle of the suitcase. She did not know why she could not move. Only that, looking at that view, she felt something that had been tied fast for a long time deep in her chest loosen, just a little. It resembled pain. Like the ache of frozen fingertips beginning to warm. The wind blew, and the pine branches rang. Haruka narrowed her eyes and looked at the mountains beyond the sea. "I'm home," she said, to no one. The words were swallowed at once by the sound of the waves. Even so, once she had said them, she felt a little lighter. And so the days of Haruka's homecoming began. That in this town she would meet a certain man — this, the Haruka of that day did not yet know. That a morning would come when she would stand beside someone and look at the Tateyama Range across the sea — that, too, she did not yet know.

第一章Chapter One

海の見える駅The Station by the Sea

◦

祖母の家は、駅から歩いて十分ほどの、坂の途中にあった。

古い木造の平屋で、瓦の色はすっかり褪せ、玄関の引き戸は建てつけが悪くなっていた。遥が力を込めて引くと、戸は最初、頑固に抵抗し、それから急に諦めたように、がたんと音を立てて開いた。

家のなかは、冷えていた。

冷えていたけれど、匂いは、遥の記憶のなかの匂いのままだった。畳の匂いと、古い木の匂いと、そのぜんぶの底のほうに沈んでいる、かすかな昆布の匂い。台所からだ、と遥は思った。

靴を脱いで上がると、廊下の板が足の裏で冷たく鳴った。

居間には、祖母の暮らしがそのまま残っていた。座卓の上に、飲みかけの湯呑みが伏せてあった。壁には手書きのカレンダーが掛かっていて、二月のところで日付が止まっていた。祖母が亡くなった日から、時間はここで動くのをやめていた。

遥は台所に立った。

流し台の隅に、小さな鍋があった。なかを覗くと、水と、昆布が一枚。祖母が最後に沈めた昆布だった。誰かが片づけてくれてもよさそうなものなのに、親戚たちは、この台所には手をつけずにいたらしい。あるいは、手をつける気になれなかったのかもしれない。

遥は、その昆布を捨てられなかった。

鍋を火にかけようとして、けれどガスの元栓が閉められていることに気づいて、やめた。かわりに、遥は鍋のなかの昆布を、そっと指先で撫でた。ふやけた昆布は、思いのほか柔らかかった。

その夜、遥は祖母の布団で眠った。

自分の布団を出すのが面倒だったから、というのが理由だった。けれど本当は、祖母の匂いのする布団で眠りたかったのだと、あとになって遥は思った。天井を見上げながら、遥は東京のマンションの天井を思い出した。あそこでは、隣の部屋の物音や、上の階の足音や、外を走る車の音が、絶え間なく聞こえていた。ここでは、聞こえるのは波の音だけだった。それも、耳を澄まさなければ聞こえないほどの、遠い波の音。

遥は、久しぶりに、夢を見ずに眠った。

翌朝、遥は早くに目を覚ました。

東京にいた頃なら、休みの日は昼まで泥のように眠っていた。けれどここでは、障子越しの光が、いやでも人を起こした。障子の桟の影が、畳の上にくっきりと落ちていた。遥は布団のなかで、その影が少しずつ動いていくのを、しばらく眺めていた。

起き上がって、遥は雨戸を開けた。

海が、そこにあった。

昨日と同じ海。けれど昨日よりも、光が澄んでいた。朝の海は、昼の海とは違う青をしていた。もっと深くて、もっと静かな青。その向こうに、雪の山並みが、朝の光を受けて、ほんのりと薔薇色に染まっていた。

遥は、思わず息を止めた。

こんな景色を、自分は毎朝見られる場所で育ったのだ。それなのに、忘れていた。忘れて、東京の灰色の窓の景色を、七年間、当たり前のものとして見ていた。

遥は、無性にコーヒーが飲みたくなった。

けれど台所には、コーヒーどころか、飲めるものが何もなかった。冷蔵庫は空だった。祖母は最後まで、家で誰かの帰りを待つように、けれど誰も帰らない冷蔵庫を、几帳面に空にして逝ったのだった。

遥は、上着を羽織って外に出た。

昨日、駅に降り立ったとき、どこからかコーヒーの匂いが漂ってきたのを思い出したのだ。この時間に開いている店があるとは思えなかったが、歩いてみる気になった。とにかく、この家のなかにじっとしていると、祖母の不在ばかりが目についてしまう。

坂を下り、駅のほうへ歩いた。

早朝の町は、まだ眠っていた。人影はなく、聞こえるのは波の音と、遠くで鳴くカモメの声だけだった。駅の前を通り過ぎ、海沿いの道を歩いていくと、松林の切れ間に、一軒の小さな建物が見えた。

木造の、二階建て。

一階の窓が、大きく海に向かって開いていて、そのなかに、灯りがともっていた。

入り口の上に、木の板の看板が下がっていた。墨で、一文字。

〈汀〉

みぎわ、と読むのだろう、と遥は思った。水際、という意味の。

窓の向こうで、誰かが動いていた。カウンターの内側に、男が立っていた。背が高くて、痩せていて、髪が少し長い。男は、コーヒーを淹れていた。細い注ぎ口のポットから、湯を、まるで糸を引くように、ゆっくりと落としている。その手つきが、あまりに真剣で、あまりに静かで、遥はしばらく、窓の外からその様子に見入ってしまった。

男が、ふと顔を上げた。

目が合った。

遥は、盗み見していたことを見咎められた子どものように、あわてて視線をそらした。けれど、逃げるのも不自然だと思い直して、遥は入り口の戸に手をかけた。

からん、と、小さなベルが鳴った。

「いらっしゃいませ」

男の声は、低くて、少しかすれていた。「まだ、準備中なんですけど」

「あ……すみません」遥は口ごもった。「開いてるのかと思って」

「いや」男は、湯を落とす手を止めずに言った。「開いてます。準備中っていうのは、僕がまだ、目が覚めてないっていうだけの意味なんで」

遥は、その言い方に、少しだけ笑ってしまった。

「座ってください」男は、カウンターの椅子を、目で示した。「コーヒー、飲みますか。いま淹れてるやつ、もうすぐ落ちるんで」

遥は、カウンターの端の椅子に腰かけた。

男の背後の窓から、海が見えた。海と、その向こうの山と、朝の光。この店は、海に向かって建っていた。窓のすぐ下が海で、遠くに、ぽつんと、大きな岩が見えた。海のなかに、まるで置き忘れられたように立っている、ひとつの岩。

「あの岩、なんていうんですか」遥は訊いた。

男は、遥の視線の先を追って、「女岩ですよ」と言った。「めいわ、って読みます。あの岩の向こうに、立山連峰が見えるでしょう。写真を撮る人が、よくあの構図を狙って来ます」

「写真」

「ええ」男は、コーヒーを、遥の前のカウンターに置いた。「僕も、撮ります。毎朝」

湯気の立つカップから、深い香りが立ちのぼった。遥は両手でカップを包んだ。あたたかかった。指先から、そのあたたかさが、ゆっくりと体の芯へ伝わっていった。

一口、飲んだ。

苦くて、けれど、その苦さの奥に、どこか甘いものが隠れていた。ずっと昔に飲んだことのある味のような気がして、けれど、どこで飲んだのかは思い出せなかった。

「おいしい」遥は、思わず言った。

男は、少しだけ意外そうな顔をした。それから、ほんの少し、口の端をゆるめた。笑った、というほどでもない、けれど確かに、その顔からさっきまでの眠たげな硬さが、ふっと消えた。

「柚木です」男は言った。「柚木湊。ここの、店主です」

「紺野です」遥も名乗った。「紺野遥。……この先の坂の上の家に、越してきたんです。というか、戻ってきた、というか」

「紺野さん」男は、その名を確かめるように繰り返した。それから、何かに気づいたように、動きを止めた。「坂の上の、紺野……もしかして、佐和子さんの?」

遥は、驚いて顔を上げた。

「祖母を、ご存じなんですか」

「ええ」湊は、静かに頷いた。「よく、来てくれてました。うちの、常連さんで」

遥は、言葉を失った。

祖母が、この店に。海の見えるこの店のカウンターに、遥がいまこうして座っているように、座っていた。その景色を思い浮かべると、胸の奥が、ぎゅっと縮んだ。遥は、祖母の暮らしのことを、何も知らなかった。この町でどんなふうに一日を過ごし、どんな店で、どんなコーヒーを飲んでいたのか。孫のくせに、何も。

「佐和子さん、いつも、あの席に座ってました」湊は、カウンターのいちばん奥、窓に近い席を、目で示した。「あそこから、海を見て。コーヒーを一杯だけ、時間をかけて飲んで。それで、帰っていかれました」

「……そうですか」

「先月、亡くなったって、聞きました」湊の声が、少し低くなった。「お悔やみ申し上げます。……ここのところ、姿を見ないなと思ってたんですけど」

遥は、コーヒーのカップに目を落とした。カップのなかに、朝の光が、小さな円になって映っていた。

「わたし、」遥は、言いかけて、口をつぐんだ。何を言おうとしたのか、自分でもわからなかった。わたし、葬儀に帰らなかったんです。わたし、祖母のことを何も知らないんです。わたし──言葉は、どれも喉の途中で、つかえて出てこなかった。

湊は、それ以上、何も訊かなかった。

ただ、遥のカップが空になると、「もう一杯、淹れましょうか」と言った。「今日のは、深煎りなんで。二杯目のほうが、味がわかると思います」

遥は、頷いた。

湊が、また、湯を糸のように落としはじめた。その静かな手つきを見ているうちに、遥は、なぜか涙が出そうになった。理由はわからなかった。ただ、この店の、この光の、この匂いのなかにいると、東京でずっと張り詰めていた何かが、ゆっくりと、ほどけていくのがわかった。

窓の外で、女岩の向こうの山が、朝の光を浴びて、白く輝いていた。

「あの山、」遥は言った。「祖母が、子どもの頃、名前を教えてくれたんです。剱岳とか、大日とか。でも、覚えてなくて」

「よかったら」湊は、二杯目を遥の前に置きながら言った。「また、教えますよ。晴れた朝なら、ぜんぶ見えるんで」

遥は、その言葉に、小さく頷いた。

その朝、遥は二杯のコーヒーを飲んで、店を出た。

店を出るとき、湊が「また、どうぞ」と言った。社交辞令かもしれなかった。けれど遥は、その一言を、坂を上って家に帰るあいだも、ずっと手のなかで温めていた。

家に戻ると、台所の鍋のなかの昆布が、朝の光を受けて、静かに水に沈んでいた。

遥は、その鍋を火にかけた。ガスの元栓は、さっき外へ出る前に、開けておいたのだった。

鍋のなかの水が、やがて、かすかに湯気を立てはじめた。

昆布出汁の匂いが、台所いっぱいに広がった。

それは、祖母の匂いだった。

Her grandmother's house stood halfway up a hill, about ten minutes' walk from the station. It was an old single-story wooden house; the color of the roof tiles had faded entirely, and the front sliding door had warped in its frame. When Haruka pulled it with all her strength, the door resisted stubbornly at first, and then, as though suddenly giving up, opened with a rattling bang. Inside, the house was cold. Cold — but the smell was exactly the smell in her memory. The smell of tatami, the smell of old wood, and beneath all of it, sunk to the bottom, a faint smell of kombu. From the kitchen, Haruka thought. She took off her shoes and stepped up, and the floorboards of the hallway rang cold against the soles of her feet. In the living room, her grandmother's life remained just as it had been. On the low table, a half-drunk teacup sat overturned. A handwritten calendar hung on the wall, its dates stopped at February. From the day her grandmother died, time here had stopped moving. Haruka stood in the kitchen. Her grandmother's kitchen was small. But into it the whole of her grandmother's living had been pressed, tight. A well-used wooden cutting board. A knife whose handle was coming loose. A pot, blackened with soot, that must have been used for decades. On the shelf, bowls and plates of every size were stacked neatly. When she opened a drawer, dried goods were sorted inside with meticulous care. Kombu. Dried sardines. Dried shiitake. Strips of dried radish. Her grandmother had been a person of stock. Whatever she made, she began by drawing the stock. Haruka picked up a single sheet of the kombu. Rishiri kombu, the bag said. Her grandmother had used good kombu. She had never been well off, and yet the one thing she used well was the kombu that formed the base of the stock. Perhaps it had been her grandmother's single luxury. In the corner of the sink was a small pot. When Haruka looked inside, there was water, and a single sheet of kombu. The last kombu her grandmother had sunk. Someone might have cleared it away, and yet the relatives had left this kitchen untouched. Or perhaps they simply could not bring themselves to touch it. Haruka could not throw that kombu away. She started to set the pot on the stove, but noticing that the gas valve was closed, she stopped. Instead, Haruka gently stroked the kombu in the pot with her fingertip. The softened kombu was more tender than she expected. That night, Haruka slept in her grandmother's futon. The reason was that laying out her own futon felt like too much trouble. But the truth, she thought later, was that she had wanted to sleep in the futon that smelled of her grandmother. Looking up at the ceiling, Haruka remembered the ceiling of her Tokyo apartment. There, the sounds of the next room, the footsteps of the floor above, the cars running outside, had all been endless. Here, all she could hear was the sound of the waves. And even that was so distant she had to strain to hear it. For the first time in a long while, Haruka slept without dreaming.

The next morning, she woke early. In Tokyo, on her days off, she had slept like mud until noon. But here, the light through the paper screens got a person up whether she liked it or not. The shadows of the screen's frame fell sharply across the tatami. From inside her futon, Haruka watched for a while as those shadows moved, little by little. She got up and opened the storm shutters. The sea was there. The same sea as yesterday. But the light was clearer than yesterday. The morning sea was a different blue from the daytime sea. Deeper, quieter. Beyond it, the snow-covered range, catching the morning light, was faintly tinged with rose. Haruka held her breath. She had grown up in a place where she could see a view like this every morning. And yet she had forgotten it. Forgotten it, and for seven years looked at the grey view of a Tokyo window as though it were the natural order of things. She was seized with an overwhelming urge for coffee. But in the kitchen there was nothing to drink, let alone coffee. The refrigerator was empty. Her grandmother had, to the very end, kept the refrigerator meticulously empty — as if waiting at home for someone to come back, though no one ever would. Haruka threw on a jacket and went outside. Yesterday, when she had stepped down at the station, she remembered a smell of coffee drifting from somewhere. She did not think there would be a shop open at this hour, but she felt like walking. In any case, sitting still inside this house, she noticed nothing but her grandmother's absence. She went down the hill, toward the station. The early-morning town was still asleep. There were no people; all she could hear was the sound of the waves and the cries of gulls somewhere far off. She passed in front of the station, and walking along the coast road, she saw, in a gap between the pines, a single small building. Two stories, wooden. The window of the first floor opened wide toward the sea, and inside it a light was lit. Above the entrance hung a wooden sign. A single character in ink. Migiwa. The water's edge, Haruka thought it must mean. Beyond the window, someone was moving. Inside the counter stood a man. Tall, thin, his hair a little long. The man was making coffee. From a pot with a slender spout he was pouring hot water, letting it fall slowly, as though drawing a thread. His movements were so serious, so quiet, that for a while Haruka stood there outside the window, absorbed in watching him. The man looked up suddenly. Their eyes met. Like a child caught peeping, Haruka hurriedly looked away. But it seemed unnatural to flee, and, thinking better of it, she put her hand on the entrance door. A small bell rang. "Welcome," the man said. His voice was low and a little hoarse. "We're not quite open yet, though." "Ah — I'm sorry," Haruka faltered. "I thought you were open." "No," the man said, not stopping the fall of the water. "We're open. When I say 'not quite,' I only mean that I'm not quite awake yet." Haruka laughed a little at the way he said it. "Sit down," the man said, indicating a counter stool with his eyes. "Do you drink coffee? The one I'm brewing now will be through in a moment." Haruka took the stool at the end of the counter. Through the window behind the man, she could see the sea. The sea, and the mountains beyond it, and the morning light. This shop was built facing the sea. Just below the window was the water, and far out, standing alone, was a single large rock — as if left behind and forgotten in the sea. "That rock — what's it called?" Haruka asked. Following the line of her gaze, the man said, "That's Meiwa." "It reads meiwa. Beyond that rock you can see the Tateyama Range, can't you. People who take photographs often come for that composition." "Photographs." "Yes." The man set the coffee on the counter in front of Haruka. "I take them too. Every morning." From the steaming cup, a deep fragrance rose. Haruka wrapped both hands around it. It was warm. From her fingertips, that warmth traveled slowly to the core of her body. She took a sip. It was bitter, and yet, behind the bitterness, something sweet was hidden. It felt like a taste she had drunk long, long ago, though she could not remember where. "It's good," Haruka said, without thinking. The man looked a little surprised. Then, just slightly, the corner of his mouth eased. Not quite a smile — but the sleepy hardness of a moment before vanished, softly, from his face. "Yuzuki," the man said. "Yuzuki Minato. I'm the owner here." "Konno," Haruka gave her own name. "Konno Haruka. I've — moved into the house up the hill past here. Or rather, come back to it." "Konno-san," the man repeated, as if confirming the name. Then, as though noticing something, he went still. "The Konno up the hill… you don't mean Sawako-san's?" Haruka looked up, startled. "You knew my grandmother?" "Yes," Minato said quietly. "She came often. A regular of ours." Haruka lost her words. Her grandmother, at this shop. At the counter of this shop by the sea, she had sat, as Haruka was sitting now. When she pictured it, her chest tightened. Haruka had known nothing of her grandmother's life. How she had spent her days in this town, at what shop, drinking what coffee. Her own grandchild, and she had known nothing. "Sawako-san always sat at that seat," Minato said, indicating with his eyes the seat at the very back of the counter, near the window. "From there, she'd look at the sea. She'd take her time over a single cup of coffee, and then she'd go home." "…I see." "I heard she passed last month," Minato said, his voice lowering a little. "My condolences. I'd been thinking she hadn't come by, lately." Haruka dropped her eyes to the cup. Inside it, the morning light was reflected in a small circle. "I—" Haruka began, and stopped. She did not know what she had meant to say. I didn't go home for the funeral. I know nothing about my grandmother. I — every word caught in her throat and would not come. Minato asked nothing more. He only said, when Haruka's cup was empty, "Shall I brew you another? Today's is a dark roast. I think the second cup is when the flavor comes through." Haruka nodded. Minato began again to let the water fall like a thread. Watching those quiet movements, Haruka felt, for no reason she could name, tears rising. She did not understand it. Only that, inside this shop, inside this light, this smell, she could feel something she had held taut in Tokyo unravel, slowly. Beyond the window, the mountains past Meiwa Rock shone white in the morning light. "Those mountains," Haruka said. "My grandmother taught me their names when I was small. Tsurugi, Dainichi. But I don't remember." "If you like," Minato said, setting the second cup before her, "I'll teach you again. On a clear morning, you can see all of them." Haruka gave a small nod. That morning, she drank two cups of coffee and left the shop. As she left, Minato said, "Come again." It might have been a courtesy. But all the way up the hill and home, Haruka held that one phrase, warm, in her hands. Back at the house, the kombu in the pot in the kitchen sat quietly in the water, catching the morning light. Haruka set the pot on the stove. The gas valve she had opened before going out. The water in the pot, before long, began faintly to steam. The smell of kombu stock spread through the whole kitchen. It was the smell of her grandmother.

第二章Chapter Two

汀という喫茶店The Coffee Shop Called Migiwa

それから遥は、毎朝、汀へ通うようになった。

通う、というほど大げさなものではない。ただ、目が覚めて、雨戸を開けて、海を見ると、自然と足がそちらへ向くのだった。祖母の家には、まだコーヒーを淹れる道具がなかった。それを口実にしていることを、遥は自分でも知っていた。

湊は、いつも先に店にいた。

遥が着く頃には、店の窓はもう海に向かって開かれていて、カウンターの奥では、コーヒーの豆を挽く音がしていた。手回しのミルだった。ごりごりという低い音が、朝の静けさのなかに、規則正しく響いた。

「おはようございます」

遥がそう言うと、湊は挽く手を止めずに、「おはよう」とだけ返した。敬語が、いつのまにか取れていた。それが、遥には少しくすぐったかった。

汀は、変わった店だった。

メニューがなかった。壁にも、テーブルにも、値段の書かれたものは何ひとつ貼られていなかった。客が来ると、湊は「今日は何にします」とは訊かず、ただ、その日いちばん状態のいい豆で、コーヒーを淹れた。それが気に入らない客もいるだろう、と遥は思った。けれど、この店に来る客は、どうやらそういう客ではなかった。

常連は、みな年配だった。

朝いちばんに来るのは、松蔵という老人だった。もう八十を過ぎているという。若い頃は定置網の漁師だったそうで、顔じゅうが日と潮に焼かれて、深い皺が刻まれていた。松蔵は、いつもカウンターのいちばん手前に座り、コーヒーには手をつけず、まず窓の外の海をじっと見た。それから、ぽつりと、その日の海のことを言った。

「今日は、凪いどるな」とか、「西風が入っとる。午後から荒れるちゃ」とか。

湊は、それに、「そうですね」とだけ答えた。

遥は、そのやりとりが好きだった。松蔵の言葉には、七十年ぶんの海が入っていた。天気予報よりも確かに、松蔵は海の翌日を言い当てた。

「あんた、佐和子さんの孫やろ」

通いはじめて三日目に、松蔵が遥に言った。

「はい」遥は頷いた。

「似とるわ」松蔵は、遥の顔をしげしげと見た。「目のあたりが。佐和子さんも、若い頃は、そんな目をしとった」

「祖母の、若い頃を、ご存じなんですか」

「同い年やちゃ」松蔵は、皺だらけの顔で、少しだけ笑った。「同じ小学校に通うとった。……あの人はな、東京へ嫁に行った娘がおって、その娘が孫を連れて夏に帰ってくるのを、毎年、それはそれは楽しみにしとった」

遥は、その孫が自分のことだと気づいて、言葉に詰まった。

「でも、その孫も、大きゅうなってからは、帰ってこんようになった」松蔵は、海を見たまま、独り言のように続けた。「佐和子さんは、それでも、毎年、夏になると、白えびを買うとった。孫が好きやから、って。孫は、来んのにな」

遥の胸を、鋭いものが貫いた。

白えび。遥は、覚えていた。子どもの頃、祖母の家で食べた白えびのかき揚げ。甘くて、さくさくして、口のなかで溶けるようだった。あれを、祖母は毎年、来ない孫のために買い続けていたのだ。

「松蔵さん」湊が、静かに口を挟んだ。「それくらいで」

松蔵は、はっとしたように口をつぐんだ。それから、「すまん」と、遥のほうを見ずに言った。「年寄りの、悪い癖や。昔のことばっかり、しゃべってしまう」

「いえ」遥は、首を振った。声が、うまく出なかった。「……ありがとうございます。祖母のこと、教えてくれて」

その日、遥はコーヒーを飲み終えても、しばらく席を立てなかった。

松蔵が帰り、ほかの常連も帰って、店には遥と湊のふたりだけになった。湊は、カウンターの向こうで、洗い物をしていた。水の音が、静かに響いた。

「松蔵さん、悪気はないんです」湊が、水の音のあいだに言った。「ただ、思ったことを、そのまま口に出す人なんで」

「わかってます」遥は言った。「本当のことだから、余計に、こたえただけで」

湊は、洗い物の手を止めた。

遥は、窓の外の海を見た。女岩が、午前の光のなかに、静かに立っていた。

「わたし、祖母の葬儀に、帰らなかったんです」

言うつもりのなかった言葉が、口をついて出た。「仕事があって。大事なプレゼンで。……でも、そのプレゼンは、通らなくて。わたしがつくったものは、一目も見てもらえなくて。それで、会社も辞めて。祖母のお葬式も、大事な仕事も、両方、なくして、ここへ来たんです」

湊は、何も言わなかった。

ただ、遥の前に、新しいコーヒーを置いた。頼んでいないコーヒーだった。

「これ、」湊は言った。「佐和子さんが、好きだった豆です」

遥は、そのカップを見た。

「毎年、夏になると、佐和子さんは、ちょっといい豆を注文してました」湊は言った。「孫が帰ってきたときに、飲ませたいから、って。うちのコーヒーが、東京のカフェのやつより、うまいって言えるように、いい豆で練習しとくんや、って」

遥の目から、涙が、こぼれた。

止めようとしても、止まらなかった。カウンターに、ぽたぽたと、涙が落ちた。遥は、それを手の甲で拭った。拭っても拭っても、あとからあとから、涙が出てきた。七年間、東京の会議室でも、満員電車でも、ひとりのマンションでも、一度も泣かなかった。泣けなかった。それが、この海の見える店で、頼んでもいないコーヒーを前にして、堰を切ったように、あふれ出した。

湊は、それを見ても、慌てなかった。

ただ、遥が泣きやむのを、静かに待っていた。窓の外で、波が寄せては返した。カモメが鳴いた。遠くを、漁船が一艘、白い航跡を引いて進んでいった。

「ゆっくり、飲んでください」

遥が少し落ち着くと、湊はそう言って、店の奥へ引っ込んだ。ひとりにしてくれたのだ、と遥は思った。それが、ありがたかった。

遥は、祖母の好きだった豆のコーヒーを、ゆっくりと飲んだ。

苦くて、けれど、その苦さの奥に、ずっと昔に飲んだ味が、確かにあった。それが何の味なのか、遥はもう、思い出せた。祖母の台所の味だ。あの、昆布出汁の匂いのする朝の台所で、祖母が遥のために、特別な日にだけ淹れてくれた、インスタントではない、本物のコーヒーの味。

遥は、祖母が、この味を守ってくれていたことを知った。

来ない孫のために。

その日、遥は初めて、この店に長くいた。昼近くまで、窓の外の海を見て過ごした。湊は、時々、店の奥から出てきて、豆を挽いたり、常連の相手をしたりした。遥のことは、放っておいてくれた。けれど、放っておかれている、という感じはしなかった。同じ空間に、静かに一緒にいる、という感じだった。

帰り際、遥は訊いた。

「柚木さんは、どうして、この店を?」

湊は、少し間を置いて、答えた。

「祖父の店だったんです」湊は言った。「僕は、東京で、写真の勉強をしてました。報道カメラマンになりたくて。でも……いろいろあって、帰ってきて、この店を継ぎました」

「いろいろ」

「ええ」湊は、それ以上、言わなかった。

遥は、それ以上、訊かなかった。

自分が、祖母の葬儀のことを、まだ誰にも詳しく話せないように、湊にも、話せない「いろいろ」があるのだろう、と思った。人はみな、口に出せない何かを抱えて、それでも毎朝、目を覚まし、コーヒーを淹れ、海を見て、生きている。

店を出ると、空が曇りはじめていた。

「午後から、荒れるって」遥は、松蔵の言葉を思い出して言った。

「ええ」湊は、空を見上げた。「松蔵さんが言うなら、荒れます。あの人の予報は、当たるんで」

その午後、本当に、海は荒れた。

遥は、祖母の家の窓から、白く波立つ海を見ていた。さっきまで穏やかだった海が、まるで別の生きもののように、うねっていた。女岩に、白い波しぶきが、何度も打ちつけていた。

海は、こんなにも、表情を変える。

遥は、東京の景色を思い出した。あの町の景色は、いつも同じだった。晴れても曇っても、ビルはビルで、道路は道路で、人は人だった。景色が変わるということが、なかった。

ここでは、海が、毎日、違う顔をしている。

その日、遥は初めて、この町で暮らしていくことを、少しだけ、悪くないと思った。

After that, Haruka began going to Migiwa every morning. "Going," though, was too grand a word. She would simply wake, open the storm shutters, look at the sea — and her feet would turn that way of their own accord. There was still no equipment in her grandmother's house for making coffee. She knew, herself, that she was using this as an excuse. Minato was always there before her. By the time Haruka arrived, the shop's window would already be open toward the sea, and behind the counter came the sound of coffee beans being ground. It was a hand mill. The low, grinding rhythm of it rang out steadily in the morning quiet. "Good morning," Haruka would say. And Minato, without stopping his grinding, would answer only, "Morning." Somewhere along the way, the politeness had dropped out of his speech. It tickled her, a little. Migiwa was a strange shop. There was no menu. Nothing with a price on it was posted, on the walls or the tables. When a customer came, Minato did not ask, "What will you have today?" He simply brewed coffee with the best bean he had that day. Some customers, Haruka thought, would find that irritating. But the customers who came to this shop, it seemed, were not that kind of customer. The regulars were all elderly. The first to arrive each morning was an old man named Matsuzo. Over eighty now, he said. In his youth he had been a fixed-net fisherman, and his whole face was burned by sun and salt, cut with deep wrinkles. Matsuzo always sat at the very front of the counter, and before touching his coffee he would gaze at the sea outside the window. Then, quietly, he would say something about the sea that day. "Calm today," he might say. Or, "West wind's come in. It'll turn rough by afternoon." Minato would answer only, "That so." Haruka loved that exchange. In Matsuzo's words were seventy years of the sea. More surely than any forecast, Matsuzo named the sea of the next day. "You're Sawako-san's granddaughter, aren't you," Matsuzo said to her on the third day. "Yes," Haruka nodded. "You resemble her," Matsuzo said, looking her over. "Around the eyes. Sawako had eyes like that too, when she was young." "You knew my grandmother? When she was young?" "Same age, we were," Matsuzo said, and his wrinkled face smiled a little. "Went to the same primary school. That woman — she had a daughter who married off to Tokyo, and every year she looked forward, so much, to that daughter coming home in summer with a grandchild." Realizing that the grandchild was herself, Haruka's words caught. "But that grandchild, too, once she grew up, stopped coming home," Matsuzo went on, as if to himself, still looking at the sea. "And even so, every year when summer came, Sawako bought shiraebi. Because the grandchild liked them, she said. Even though the grandchild never came." Something sharp ran through Haruka's chest. Shiraebi. The white shrimp. She remembered them — the shiraebi fritters she had eaten at her grandmother's house as a child, sweet and crisp, dissolving in the mouth. Every year, her grandmother had gone on buying them for a grandchild who never came. "Matsuzo-san," Minato cut in, quietly. "That's enough of that." Matsuzo shut his mouth as if startled. "Sorry," he said, not looking at Haruka. "Bad habit of an old man. Nothing but old stories." "No," Haruka said, shaking her head. Her voice would not come out right. "…Thank you. Matsuzo left, and the other regulars, and the shop was left with only Haruka and Minato. Behind the counter, Minato was washing up. The sound of the water rang quietly. "Matsuzo-san means no harm," Minato said, in a gap between the water. "He's just a man who says what he thinks, as he thinks it." "I know," Haruka said. "It only hurt more because it's true." Minato stopped washing. Haruka looked at the sea outside the window. Meiwa Rock stood quiet in the morning light. "I didn't go home for my grandmother's funeral," she said. The words she had not meant to speak came out. "There was work. An important presentation. But the presentation didn't go through. What I'd made — no one even glanced at it. So I quit the company too. My grandmother's funeral, and the work that mattered — I lost both, and came here." Minato said nothing. He only set a new cup of coffee in front of her. A coffee she had not ordered. "This," Minato said, "is the bean Sawako-san liked." Haruka looked at the cup. "Every year, when summer came, Sawako-san ordered a slightly finer bean," Minato said. "Because she wanted to serve it to her grandchild, when the grandchild came. So she could say her coffee was better than the cafés in Tokyo — she said she practiced with a good bean, to be ready." Tears spilled from Haruka's eyes. However she tried to stop them, they would not stop. They fell onto the counter, drop by drop. Haruka wiped them with the back of her hand. Wipe as she might, more came, and more. In seven years — in Tokyo meeting rooms, on packed trains, in her lonely apartment — she had never once cried. Could not. And now, in this shop by the sea, before a coffee she had not even ordered, it came out as though a dam had broken. Minato, seeing it, did not panic. He only waited, quietly, for Haruka to cry herself out. Beyond the window the waves came and went. A gull cried. Far off, a fishing boat passed, trailing a white wake. "Drink it slowly," Minato said, when she had calmed a little, and withdrew to the back of the shop. He was leaving her alone, Haruka understood. And she was grateful. She drank the coffee her grandmother had loved, slowly. It was bitter, and yet, behind the bitterness, the taste she had drunk long ago was certainly there. And now she could remember what taste it was. It was the taste of her grandmother's kitchen. In that morning kitchen that smelled of kombu stock, on special days, her grandmother had brewed her real coffee — not instant — just for her. Her grandmother, Haruka understood, had kept this taste alive. For a grandchild who never came. That day, for the first time, Haruka stayed a long time in the shop. Until nearly noon, she passed the time watching the sea outside the window. Minato came out now and then, ground beans, tended to the regulars. He left her be. And yet she did not feel left alone. It felt more like being, quietly, together in the same space. On her way out, Haruka asked, "Why did you take on this shop?" Minato paused before answering. "It was my grandfather's shop," he said. "I was in Tokyo, studying photography. I wanted to be a news photographer. But — a number of things happened, and I came back, and took over the shop." "A number of things." "Yes." Minato said no more. And Haruka asked no more. Just as she could not yet tell anyone, in detail, about the funeral she had not attended, so Minato must have "a number of things" he could not speak of. Everyone carries something they cannot say aloud, and still, every morning, wakes, brews coffee, looks at the sea, and lives. When she stepped outside, the sky had begun to cloud. "It'll turn rough in the afternoon," Haruka said, remembering Matsuzo's words. "Yes," Minato said, looking up at the sky. "If Matsuzo-san says so, it will. His forecasts come true." That afternoon, the sea did turn rough. From the window of her grandmother's house, Haruka watched the sea whiten with waves. The sea that had been calm only that morning now heaved like a different living thing. White spray struck Meiwa Rock again and again. The sea changes its face so much, she thought. She remembered the Tokyo scenery. That town's scenery had always been the same. Rain or shine, buildings were buildings, roads were roads, people were people. The scenery did not change. Here, the sea wore a different face every day. And that day, for the first time, Haruka thought that living in this town, a little, might not be so bad.

「ええ」湊は言った。「毎朝、女岩の向こうの立山を。誰も来ない時間に、ひとりで」

「毎朝?」

「二年、続けてます」湊は言った。「まだ、一枚も、これだ、っていう写真が撮れてないんですけど」

「二年も」遥は、驚いた。「同じ場所を、二年も」

「同じ場所でも、同じ朝は、二度とないんです」湊は言った。「光も、雲も、波も、山の見え方も、毎朝違う。……いつか、僕が撮りたい、と思ってる朝が、来るはずなんです。それを、待ってます」

「どんな朝を、撮りたいんですか」

湊は、しばらく黙った。

それから、「たぶん、」と、ゆっくり言った。「一年でいちばん寒い朝です。真冬の、風のない、よく晴れた朝。空気が凍りついて、海がぴたりと凪いで、女岩の向こうに、立山連峰がぜんぶ、くっきりと見える。……そういう朝が、一年に、何日かだけあるんです。その朝の光を、撮りたい」

遥は、湊の横顔を、闇のなかで見つめた。

「あ」

湊が、小さく声を上げた。

遥は、湊の視線の先を追った。

海が、光っていた。

波打ち際に、青い光が、点々と現れていた。最初は、ほんの数えるほどだった。けれど、次の波が寄せると、その光は、もっと増えた。青白い、幻のような光。それが、波に運ばれて、浜へ、浜へと、打ち寄せてきた。

「身投げだ」湊が、囁いた。

遥は、立ち上がった。

波打ち際へ、駆け寄った。

そこには、無数のほたるいかが、打ち上げられていた。一匹一匹が、青い光を放っていた。まるで、夜空の星が、そのまま浜辺に降ってきたようだった。遥は、しゃがみこんで、一匹を、そっと手のひらに載せた。

冷たくて、小さくて、けれど、確かに、光っていた。

その光は、いのちの光だった。産卵のために、命がけで浜へやってきた、小さな生きものたちの、最後の光。遥は、その光を、手のひらのなかで見つめた。

なぜか、涙が出そうになった。

これは、死のための光でもあった。けれど、同時に、新しい命を生むための光でもあった。終わりと、始まりが、同じひとつの光のなかにあった。

「きれい」遥は、囁いた。

その言葉では、足りなかった。けれど、ほかに言葉がなかった。

湊は、少し離れたところで、三脚を立てていた。

シャッターの音が、闇のなかに、静かに響いた。

遥は、手のひらのほたるいかを、そっと海へ返した。青い光が、波に運ばれて、闇のなかへ消えていった。

「柚木さん」遥は、湊のほうを振り返った。「撮れましたか。これだ、っていう写真」

湊は、カメラのモニターを覗きこんでいた。

「いや」湊は、少し笑った。「これも、違います。……でも、悪くない」

ふたりは、しばらく、光る海を見ていた。

やがて、身投げは、静まった。青い光は、少しずつ数を減らし、最後の一匹が波に返されると、海はまた、ただの黒い海に戻った。まるで、いまのが夢だったかのように。

「教えてくれて、ありがとうございました」遥は言った。「わたし、こんなの、初めて見ました」

「僕も、毎年見てますけど」湊は言った。「毎年、初めて見るみたいに、驚きます。……いいものを、見せられてよかった」

汀の前で、ふたりは別れた。

「おやすみなさい」遥は言った。

「おやすみ」湊は言った。それから、少し迷うように、付け加えた。「明日の朝も、コーヒー、あります」

遥は、微笑んだ。

「行きます」

"The firefly squid come up onto the shore at night, to spawn," Minato explained from beside them. "On a calm night around the new moon. The whole beach glows blue. Even the locals don't get to see it often." Haruka tried to picture it, and could not quite. The whole beach, glowing blue. It sounded like something not of this world. "Tonight's the new moon," Minato said. "No wind. If Matsuzo-san says there'll be a beaching, there probably will be. I want to take photographs, too." The night beach was an entirely different place from the day one. There were no streetlights and no moon; the whole world was dark, and only the sound of the waves came out of the black. "Watch your feet," Minato said, lighting the ground at her feet with a flashlight. "There are a lot of rocks along here." The two of them walked the beach. For a while, nothing happened. The sea was simply black, the waves lifting only their white edges into the dark before they vanished. Little by little, Haruka grew cold. She began to think the firefly squid would not come. "They'll come," Minato said, as if reading her mind. "Let's wait just a little longer." The two of them sat on a piece of driftwood and looked at the sea. In the dark, Minato's profile was almost invisible. Only the fact of his presence, close beside her, was there. Had she ever, Haruka wondered, sat beside someone like this, silently gazing into the dark? In Tokyo, the gaps had always been filled — with words, with sound, with light. Silence was an awkward thing. But now, the silence with Minato was not awkward. If anything, it was comfortable. "Do you always shoot alone?" Haruka asked. "Yes," Minato said. "Every morning. The Tateyama across the sea, beyond Meiwa Rock. Alone, at an hour when no one comes." "Every morning?" "For two years now," Minato said. "And I still haven't taken one that I can call the one." "Two years. The same place, for two years." "Even the same place is never the same morning twice," Minato said. "The light, the clouds, the waves, how the mountains show — every morning is different. Someday the morning I want to photograph will come. I'm waiting for it." "What kind of morning do you want to photograph?" Minato was quiet for a while. Then, slowly, he said, "Probably — the coldest morning of the year. A deep-winter morning, windless, clear. The air frozen, the sea gone perfectly still, and beyond Meiwa Rock, the whole Tateyama Range, sharp and complete. There are only a few such mornings in a year. I want to photograph the light of one of them." Haruka looked at his profile in the dark. "Ah," Minato said softly. Haruka followed his gaze. The sea was glowing. At the water's edge, points of blue light had appeared. At first only a few. But when the next wave came in, the light increased. A pale, phantom-blue light, carried by the waves, washing in toward the shore, and in, and in. "The beaching," Minato whispered. Haruka stood. She ran to the water's edge. There, countless firefly squid had been cast ashore. Each one gave off a blue light. It was as if the stars of the night sky had come down whole to the beach. Haruka crouched and lifted one gently onto her palm. Cold, tiny — and yet, certainly, glowing. It was the light of a life. A light of small creatures that had come to the shore at the cost of their lives, to spawn — their last light. Haruka gazed at it in her palm. For some reason, tears rose. This was a light for death. And yet, at the same time, it was a light to bring forth new life. Ending and beginning were in the same single light. "It's beautiful," Haruka whispered. The word was not enough. But there was no other word. A little away, Minato had set up his tripod. The sound of the shutter rang quietly in the dark. Haruka returned the firefly squid on her palm gently to the sea. The blue light was carried away by the waves and vanished into the dark. "Yuzuki-san," she said, turning to him. "Did you get it? The photograph — the one?" Minato was peering into the camera's monitor. "No," he said, with a small laugh. "This isn't it either. But — it's not bad." For a while, the two of them looked at the glowing sea. Before long, the beaching subsided. The blue lights thinned, and when the last one was returned to the waves, the sea went back to being an ordinary black sea. As if what they had seen had been a dream. On the way back, Haruka and Minato walked the dark beach side by side. "Thank you for showing me," Haruka said. "I'd never seen anything like it." "I see it every year," Minato said. "And every year I'm astonished, as if for the first time. I'm glad I could show you something good." In front of Migiwa, they parted. "Good night," Haruka said. "Good night," Minato said. And then, as though hesitating, he added, "There'll be coffee in the morning, too." Haruka smiled. "I'll come." Climbing the hill home, Haruka could not sleep for a while. When she closed her eyes, that blue light floated up behind them. The single light of ending and beginning. And, beside that light, Minato's profile. Deep in her chest, Haruka felt something begin to glow, small, like a firefly squid. She did not yet know the name of that light. Or perhaps she knew it, and was pretending not to.

第四章Chapter Four

女岩の約束The Promise at Meiwa Rock

ほたるいかの夜から、遥と湊のあいだの何かが、少しだけ変わった。

遥は、息をのんだ。

空の色が、刻一刻と、変わっていった。同じ景色が、一分ごとに、まったく違う景色になった。湊が言っていた言葉を、遥は思い出した。同じ場所でも、同じ朝は、二度とない。本当に、そうだった。

太陽は、山のさらに向こうから昇ってくるのではなく、海のこちら側の、遥の背後の山から昇っていた。そして、その光が、海の向こうの立山連峰を、正面から照らした。雪をかぶった山々が、朝日を浴びて、黄金色に輝いた。海は、その光を映して、無数のさざなみを、金色に光らせた。

その瞬間、湊のシャッターが、続けざまに切られた。

かしゃ、かしゃ、かしゃ、と。

それから、湊は、カメラから顔を上げた。そして、大きく、息を吐いた。長いあいだ、息を止めていたかのように。

「柚木さん」

遥は、声をかけた。

湊が、振り返った。遥がそこにいることに、驚いたようだった。

「紺野さん」湊は言った。「いつから、そこに」

「少し前から」遥は言った。「邪魔しちゃ、悪いと思って」

湊は、少し困ったような顔をした。それから、「見てましたか」と訊いた。

「見てました」遥は言った。「きれいでした。……こんなにきれいなものを、毎朝、ひとりで見てたんですね」

湊は、何も言わなかった。ただ、カメラのモニターに目を落とした。

「撮れましたか」遥は訊いた。「これだ、っていう写真」

湊は、モニターを見つめたまま、首を振った。

「今日も、違います」湊は言った。「今日は、雲がなさすぎた。少しだけ、雲がほしかった。山にかかる雲が、光を、複雑にするんです。……でも、これは、これで、悪くない」

遥は、湊の隣に立って、モニターを覗きこんだ。

小さな画面のなかに、金色に輝く立山連峰が写っていた。女岩の黒いシルエットが、その手前に、静かに立っていた。海が、金色に光っていた。それは、遥がいま、目の前で見た景色そのものだった。けれど、写真のなかのそれは、目の前のそれよりも、なぜか、もっと切実に見えた。まるで、湊が、この景色に、何か祈りのようなものを込めているように。

Haruka too had seen it once as a child, taken by her grandmother. That day, the two of them went to Kaiwo Maru Park. When they reached the harbor, a white sailing ship floated there. Four masts rose straight into the blue summer sky. The sails were still furled. But even the bare frame of it had an overwhelming presence. Before long, the unfurling began. Sailors climbed the masts and spread the sails, one after another. White sails opened, one, and another, into the summer sky. When every sail was open, it was like twenty-nine white wings. The quiet heat of trying to capture the white sails of the Kaiwo Maru. Haruka wanted to keep looking at that profile. More than the ship, more than the sails — at that profile. Her own feeling, she knew, was already clear. That thing that had begun to glow deep in her chest in spring, on the night of the firefly squid. She already knew the name of that light. I love him, Haruka thought. I love this man. But she could not say it aloud. Minato and his memory of his brother. Those two years of prayer toward the sea beyond Meiwa Rock. Minato was still in the middle of that prayer. He had not yet taken the most beautiful morning. His heart might still be full with waiting for it. Was there room, there, for Haruka's feeling to enter? Haruka did not know. That night, the Kaiwo Maru was lit up. The white sails, lit, rose out of the dark like an apparition. On the water's surface the light was reflected, and swayed. As if two ships of light floated upon the sea. Haruka and Minato looked at those ships of light, side by side. "It's beautiful," Haruka said. "Yes," Minato said. For a while, they watched the light in silence. "Konno-san," Minato said, suddenly. "That is—" He faltered. Haruka looked at him. His face was faintly lit by the light of the ships. "Since you came to this town," Minato said, choosing his words, "my mornings have changed. Before, I only shot as if it were a duty. Like a kind of atonement to my brother. But now—" He stopped. Haruka waited for the rest. Her heart pounded. "Now," Minato said quietly, "every morning, I look forward to you coming to the shop. It's a strange way to put it. I take the photographs of the sea, go back to the shop, and wait for you to come. That's my morning, now." Haruka's chest filled. "So do I," Haruka said. Her voice shook. "Every morning, I look forward to going to Migiwa. I wake, look at the sea, go down the hill. And you're there, brewing coffee, waiting. Haruka and Minato looked at each other, and laughed, just a little. It had been so close. So close, and the two of them would have confirmed their feelings with something other than words. But that "so close" stayed in Haruka as a sweet ache. There's no need to hurry, she thought. There is still time until winter comes. While they waited, together, for the coldest, most beautiful morning of the year, surely they would cross that "so close." So Haruka believed. But that at the end of summer a single phone call from Tokyo would come to her — this, the Haruka of that night did not yet know.

That morning, Haruka woke before the day had broken. She needed no alarm. Her body woke of its own accord. As if her whole body had known this morning was coming. She opened the shutters, just a little. Outside, it was still dark. But the air was different. Frozen air poured in through the gap. A sharpness she had never known. A cold that seemed to freeze the depths of her lungs. And that cold was, endlessly, clear. A dry cold, with not a single cloud. She looked up at the sky. The stars were out. It was the first full sky of stars she had seen that winter. All winter the sky had been covered with cloud. This morning, that cloud was gone without a trace. The whole sky was scattered with stars, twinkling as if frozen in place. Matsuzo was right. This morning, it would clear. Haruka put on her warmest clothes. She threw on her grandmother's old shawl, too. And she left the house. Outside, snow had piled. But it was not the damp snow of the days before. Frozen, it glittered, reflecting starlight. With each step Haruka took, the snow gave a dry sound — kyu, kyu. She went down the hill. On the beach, Minato was there. He had already set his tripod, camera raised. Beside him, Matsuzo stood too, bundled against the cold, gazing out beyond the sea. "Haruka," Minato said, noticing her. His voice shook. Not from the cold alone. "I've come," Haruka said, standing beside him. The sky was still dark. But the eastern sky, near the horizon, was beginning, faintly, to brighten. The indigo sky shifted, little by little — indigo to navy, navy to ultramarine. And beyond the sea— The Tateyama Range showed. Still dark. But certainly there. Beyond the black silhouette of Meiwa Rock. Against the indigo sky, the snow-covered range stood, still. Haruka caught her breath. For the first time since winter came, she saw the Tateyama Range. Two weeks of waiting. Every morning hidden by cloud. This morning, sharp and clear, it was there. "You can see it," Haruka whispered. "Minato, you can see it. Tateyama." "Yes," Minato said. "You can see it. So clearly." The sky brightened, little by little. Ultramarine to indigo to blue. Blue to pale blue. And near the horizon, it began to flush rose. The light began to touch the Tateyama Range. First the highest point caught it. The sharp peak of Tsurugi, taking the morning sun, flushed faintly rose. Then the light came, little by little, down the mountains. Dainichi. Onanji. Yakushi. One peak and then another, the snow flushed rose. The sea was still dark. Still. The wind had dropped, dead still. The surface of the sea, like a mirror, lay quiet. And on that mirror, the rose-flushed Tateyama Range was reflected, upside down. In the sky, the true Tateyama. In the sea, the inverted Tateyama. Two ranges, facing each other across Meiwa Rock. "Minato," Haruka whispered. "The mountains — reflected in the sea." Minato did not answer. Peering into the viewfinder, he did not stir. Before long, the sun rose. From the hills at Haruka's back, the sun showed its face. And its light struck the Tateyama Range head-on. The rose-flushed peaks blazed, all at once, gold. The snow-covered mountains, taking the morning sun, shone blinding. And that light reflected on the mirror of the sea. The sea set countless golden ripples glittering. Meiwa Rock stood quiet on that golden sea. It was a scene not of this world. Haruka's tears would not stop. Never in her life had she seen anything so beautiful. Sea, and mountains, and light. All of it become one — a morning. A morning that would never come again, and yet, now, was certainly there, like a miracle. Minato's words came back to her. A morning so beautiful it hurts. This was it, exactly. Minato's shutter fired. Kasha. Once. And that was all. He did not fire it again. Minato lifted his face from the camera. And with his own two eyes, not the viewfinder, he looked at the scene. Tears were spilling from Minato's eyes. "Brother," he whispered, toward the Tateyama Range across the sea. "Are you watching? This is the morning you wanted, to the last, to see. I finally took it. It took two years. Sorry — for that time. When you said I was running, and I threw it back — 'all you know is the sea.' You were seeing something this beautiful, every day. The one who was running was me." Haruka took his hand. Minato closed his around hers. "Brother," he went on. "Let me introduce her. This is Haruka. Sawako-san's granddaughter. The person I care about. She's the one who let me live again. I took this photograph with her." Beyond the sea, the Tateyama Range shone gold. The light seemed to answer his words. Matsuzo, quietly, slipped away. He was leaving the two of them alone. Haruka and Minato stood side by side, looking at the sea and the mountains shining gold. "Minato," Haruka said. "My grandmother saw this morning too. Sixty years ago. With the one she loved." "Yes," Minato said. "The same morning, surely. And now, we're seeing it." Haruka took her grandmother's photograph from her bag. The young Sawako and the fisherman, laughing with the Tateyama beyond Meiwa Rock at their backs. She held the photograph up against the scene before her. The Tateyama beyond Meiwa Rock, sixty years ago. The Tateyama beyond Meiwa Rock, now. Across time, the same view was there. "Grandma," she said to the photograph. "Look. It's the same morning. The morning you saw with him. I'm seeing it now, with someone I care about." The wind blew. Frozen, clear wind. It dried the tears on Haruka's cheeks. Minato turned to her. "Haruka," he said. "Yes," Haruka said. "I love you," Minato said. Straight. "Two years ago, when I lost my brother, my time stopped. Every morning, shooting this sea, I lived like a dead man. But you came. You set my stopped time moving again. I want to live with you. In this town by the sea. Always." Tears welled again in Haruka's eyes. "So do I," she said. "I love you. I came back to this town worn down in Tokyo, a hollow shell. I expected nothing. Only to clear out my grandmother's house. But I met you. Drank Migiwa's coffee. Saw the firefly squid. And I took back the warmth of living, once more. I want to live with you. Here. Always." Minato drew her close. The two of them, with the sea and mountains shining gold at their backs, held each other. And kissed. It was a frozen, cold morning. But their lips were warm. Beyond Meiwa Rock, the Tateyama Range shone in the morning sun, on and on. It was a morning when the rain had cleared. A morning when the rain, waited for two years — no, sixty years — had, at last, cleared. The single photograph Minato took that morning— The Tateyama Range shining gold beyond Meiwa Rock. The mirror sea. And, gazing at that view, Haruka's tear-wet profile. Minato gave the photograph a title. "Amaharashi." The rain clears. That was the title of the photograph.

A year passed after that. Migiwa changed, a little. On the wall by the seat at the very back of the counter, near the window — the seat where her grandmother had once sat — a single photograph came to be hung. "Amaharashi." It is something better. This is love." Minato showed the letter to Haruka, and smiled, a little shyly.

After that winter morning, Haruka and Minato visited the grave of Minato's brother. Haruka met him for the first time. The young fisherman in the photograph, sun-browned, smiling. He resembled Minato, a little. But his face was far bolder, far more open, than Minato's. "How do you do," Haruka said before the grave, bowing. "I'm Konno Haruka. I'll live my life with Minato." Minato laid the "Amaharashi" photograph before the grave. "Brother," he said. "The photograph I promised. The morning you wanted, to the last, to see. I finally took it. I'm not running anymore. I'll live by this sea. Watch over us." The wind blew. The branches of the trees in the graveyard rang. It was as if his brother had answered.

Haruka lives on in her grandmother's house. The photograph and letters that came from the locked drawer, she keeps with care. And she has decided: someday she will find the grave of the man who sent those letters, and lay her grandmother's photograph before it. The man who promised, "When the coldest winter clears, I will come for you." The man who could not keep it. She wants to tell him. Sawako-san never forgot you. For more than sixty years she waited for you. And she never forgot that morning, the one she saw with you, the Tateyama beyond Meiwa Rock. So there is nothing left to regret. Sawako-san lived fully, for your share as well. And her granddaughter has seen your morning, again.

Haruka came to work at Migiwa. Though brewing the coffee remained, as ever, Minato's task. Haruka helped run the shop. And she added, to it, one small new thing. Shiraebi fritters. Miso soup drawn from kombu stock. The tastes of Toyama she had inherited from her grandmother, Haruka added to Migiwa's small menu. When a customer who came in the morning for coffee said, "I'm hungry," Haruka would fry shiraebi fritters. Crisp, sweet jewels of the sea. She would dip them in the kombu-stock broth and set them out. "Good," the customer would say. "These fritters — they taste like Sawako-san's." That was the finest praise Haruka could receive.

Matsuzo came to Migiwa every morning, as ever. He would sit at the very front, look first at the sea, and say something about the sea that day. "Calm today." "West wind's in. It'll turn rough by afternoon." And now and then, looking at the "Amaharashi" photograph, he would say: "A fine photograph. Sawako-san saw that morning too. Sixty years ago. She too looked at those mountains from this shop. And round and round it goes — comes to this." Haruka nodded at his words. Round and round it goes. Sixty years ago, her grandmother had seen the Tateyama beyond Meiwa Rock with the one she loved. That view her grandmother had kept looking at for sixty years, for the one she lost. And her granddaughter had come back to this town, and seen the same view, with the one she loved. Round and round it goes. Across time, longing carries on. In this small town by the sea.

That winter, the coldest morning of the year came again. Matsuzo said, "Tomorrow morning, it'll clear." And the next morning, Haruka and Minato stood on the beach again. Beyond Meiwa Rock, the Tateyama Range showed, sharp and clear. The sun rose, and the snow peaks shone gold. On the mirror sea, the view was reflected, upside down. It was the same morning as the year before. And yet, a different morning too. This year, Minato had not brought his camera. "You're not shooting?" Haruka asked. "This year, I'll just look," Minato said. "With you. Not through a lens — with these eyes. To see this morning that will never come again, with you — that matters more." Haruka smiled. And she took Minato's hand. On the ring finger of Minato's left hand, a ring gleamed. On the ring finger of Haruka's left hand, the same ring gleamed. In spring, the two of them had become husband and wife. "Minato," Haruka said. "Yes," Minato said. "I'm happy," Haruka said. "So am I," Minato said. Beyond Meiwa Rock, the Tateyama Range shone gold. The sea was still. The wind had dropped. It was a frozen, clear morning. But their joined hands were warm. It was a morning when the rain had cleared. And, from here on, however many times, the rain will clear again. In this town by the sea. As long as the two of them stand side by side, and wait for the morning.
