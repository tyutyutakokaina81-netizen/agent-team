Why Toyama Never Let Go of Its Trains

Across much of rural Japan, local railways have been quietly disappearing — fewer riders, thinner timetables, then whole lines gone. Toyama is a striking exception. In Takaoka, a street tram still runs past people's front doors; a coastal local line reaches a seaside town on Toyama Bay; and in Toyama City, a line that had nearly outlived its purpose came back — rebuilt and strengthened — as a modern light-rail (LRT). In a region where people are said to lean heavily on cars, why did the trains survive here? The short answer isn't luck.

Disappearing was the "normal"

Japanese rural rail is caught in a downward spiral. Population falls; a car-per-person society takes hold; ridership drops; deficits force service cuts; the inconvenience pushes still more people away. Many lines have been abandoned at the end of that loop. So a regional area where trains are still alive is, in fact, not the default. Toyama is no more populous than its neighbors, and by reputation quite car-dependent — which is exactly what makes it interesting.

Takaoka to the sea; a city centre on rails

Takaoka has a street tram that runs from town toward a seaside district, now using low-floor cars as an everyday form of transport; its name is tied to a classical poet who left many verses in the Man'yōshū here. A coastal local line also links Takaoka to a seaside town, running along the bay — on a clear day, snow-capped mountains are said to appear across the water. And in Toyama City, a former JR line was reborn as a next-generation LRT: low-floor, easy to board, with the north and south sides of the station joined so you can loop through the city centre. In an era when the trend was to cut rail, Toyama bet on making it easier to ride instead.

A bet on a "walkable" city

Toyama City is often described as pursuing a "compact city" policy — clustering homes and daily services along rail and LRT lines so that people who can no longer drive can still get around, rather than sprawling thinly into the suburbs. It's an approach reported to draw study visits from cities abroad wrestling with the same aging-and-shrinking problem. None of this is a simple success story: costs and demographics remain hard, and assessments differ by viewpoint. But to eyes used to watching rural trains vanish, a place that chose to invest so the trains wouldn't become inconvenient — rather than folding them because they were — feels quietly remarkable.

Car country, and yet the trains live

Live in Takaoka and the railway is still part of daily life: the sound of a train, a tram pausing at a light, a single line out to a town by the sea. In car-first countryside, that is its own small luxury. If you visit Toyama, take the tram or the LRT rather than a car at least once — not for speed, but to feel the air of a town that held onto its trains when so many others let go.
