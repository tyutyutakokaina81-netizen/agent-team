Clothes Change on a Date, Not a Temperature

The first of June and the first of October. On those two dates school uniforms switch over — by the calendar, not the thermometer.

In the original form you went into long sleeves on 1 October however warm it was, and into short sleeves on 1 June however cold.

It has loosened

Many schools and workplaces now allow a transition period of some weeks in which either is acceptable.

At the end of September you see children still in short sleeves walking beside others already in long ones. The old sight of everyone changing at once has, as far as I can see, mostly gone.

The dates themselves remain, though. Loosened, but still the reference point.

The household version has no deadline

Which is why it never finishes. The summer things do not get put away, November arrives, and the winter clothes come out on top of them. Some years the cupboard holds both seasons at once.

It is the kind of job that only happens if you decide today is the day — which makes the uniform system, with its fixed date, the more sensible arrangement of the two.

The clothes you never wore

Folding away a summer shirt that never got used, I think I probably will not wear it next year either.

And I keep it anyway, and have the same thought at the next changeover. That round trip happens twice a year, reliably.

In Toyama

Some Octobers stay hot and some Novembers want the heating on, so the timing is hard to read here.

And because this is snow country the winter half is bulky: coats, thick socks, boots. It feels less like an exchange than like the room getting smaller once the winter things are out.

Fact-check note
  • ✅ In Japan, 1 June and 1 October are the customary dates for koromogae, used for school and company uniforms.
  • ✅ Transition periods keyed to actual temperature are now common.
  • ⚠️ "Many schools" and "as far as I can see" are the writer's impressions — no proportions or statistics are given.
  • ⚠️ "Everyone used to change at once" rests on the writer's memory; practice differs by region and generation.
  • ⚠️ The household passages describe one household, not a norm.
  • ⚠️ No temperatures or dates are cited for Toyama's autumn, which varies a great deal year to year.
  • ⚠️ "Snow country means more winter clothing" is an impression, not a measurement.
  • ⚠️ No school, district or personal name appears (prefecture level only).
