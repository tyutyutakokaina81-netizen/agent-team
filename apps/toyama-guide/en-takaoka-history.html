Takaoka: Walking From the Edo Period to the 1970s in One Afternoon

Takaoka is a city where the timeline blurs. In one afternoon you can pass Edo-period streets, a district of 400-year-old metal craft, a giant Buddha standing on an ordinary corner, and food halls unchanged since the 1970s—then a modern café in a converted townhouse. Takaoka never curated its past into a museum; it simply kept living through its eras, and they all remain, side by side.

Why so many eras coexist

Takaoka was founded in the early 1600s as a planned craftsman's town, and metal casting—especially copper—became its backbone. When industrialization arrived in the twentieth century, it built on that base rather than erasing it. So the Edo-era "craft town" and the Showa-era "industrial town" stacked into layers in the same place: 300-year-old techniques and mass-production values coexisting under one identity as a city of handwork.

Preserved, not restored

Around the castle park you'll find Edo-period merchant houses—many still lived in, not turned into shops. Where Kyoto often converts old buildings into cafés and galleries, Takaoka leaves a lot of them as homes. The effect is a living town: you'll see someone sweeping a doorway or hanging laundry between old timber facades. It reads less like a sightseeing set and more like a place people actually inhabit.

The copper workshops

Takaoka is known nationally for copperware—Buddhist altar fittings, architectural ornament, tea utensils. Rather than viewing it only in a museum, you can visit workshops (often by appointment) and watch what a craftsperson is making right now. In that conversation you hear the whole timeline: Edo-era technique, the changes industrialization brought, and the pressures of the present.

A Buddha on the street, and an old tram

The Takaoka Great Buddha—about 16 meters, cast in metal—stands in the center of town, not tucked inside a shrine complex. It functions as part of the streetscape: locals pass and pay respects daily, sharing the space with visitors. And the local Manyosen tram traces a route along an old road toward Kyoto, so the way you ride today follows a path merchants once walked.

How to walk it

Arrive early at the castle park while it's quiet and the Edo streets read clearly. Midday, eat at an old food hall (Takaoka's croquette set meals are their own story) or hear a craftsperson's story at a workshop. Late afternoon, visit the Buddha and feel two eras share one space. It's a small city, but few places let you feel time as layers this plainly.
