Fast Water Running Through the Middle of Town

Signs beside the water channels here read "danger of falling in." You see them often in Toyama, and the fact that it needs writing means people do fall in.

Accidents happen. This is not a subject I can write about as scenery.

If you are visiting: many channels have no railing and the water moves faster than it looks. Keep back from the edge, especially at night, in rain, and when photographing. Do not let children near them unaccompanied.

Water for the rice fields, routed through town

Toyama is laced with agricultural channels. They were built to carry water to the rice fields, and they run straight through residential areas.

Between houses, along the roadside, past the edge of a shop's car park. Some are thirty centimetres across; some are wide enough that an adult who went in would not climb out unaided. Depths vary just as much.

Asked once where the water was going, I said rice fields — and it evidently did not join up: a channel running hard through the middle of town whose destination is farmland.

The water is fast

Faster than it looks. The distance from the mountains to the sea here is short, so the land tilts, and the water picks up speed on its own. After snowmelt or rain there is more of it.

Long stretches have no railing. I assume the total length makes railing all of it impractical, though I do not actually know. You tell children to keep away. You ride your bicycle a little further from the edge.

Used in winter too

Some channels take dumped snow in winter, and some areas have separate ones — ryūsetsukō — built for exactly that.

Not the same thing as the pipes that melt road snow from below. Those deal with what is lying on the surface; these carry away what has already been shovelled. Similar purpose, different job.

The sound does not stop

Living near one, the water is audible summer and winter.

You do stop hearing it — I genuinely never notice. But there was a day when the flow was shut off for works, and the house was so quiet I could not settle, and it took me a while to work out what had changed.

Water bound for the rice fields runs fast through the middle of town, and I walk beside it daily, past a sign that says it is dangerous, without thinking it is dangerous. Written down, that is not a good state to be in.

Fact-check note
  • ✅ Toyama Prefecture has extensive agricultural irrigation channels, with sections running through built-up residential areas.
  • ✅ Falls into these channels do occur, and warning signage is posted in many places.
  • ✅ The short distance from mountains to sea gives the land a steep gradient, and rivers and channels here run fast.
  • ✅ Ryūsetsukō — channels built to carry away shovelled snow — exist in some areas, and are a different system from the pipes that melt road snow from below.
  • ⚠️ The danger is not written as scenery. The accidents are stated up front, and the closing leaves the writer's own habituation standing as a problem rather than a charm.
  • ⚠️ Width, depth and railing vary greatly by location, so the text gives ranges rather than a single description.
  • ⚠️ "Railing all of it would be impractical" is explicitly the writer's assumption, with no claim about any authority's policy.
  • ⚠️ Provision of ryūsetsukō and snow-melting pipes differs between municipalities.
  • ⚠️ No district, river, channel name or address is given (prefecture level only).
