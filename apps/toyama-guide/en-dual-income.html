Why So Many Couples in Toyama Both Work

In Toyama, it's completely ordinary to meet households where both parents work — and have children too. Visitors from big cities sometimes ask, "How do you manage it?" Toyama is, in fact, said to be one of the regions with the highest share of dual-income households in Japan, and women staying in the workforce is nothing unusual here. The reason isn't really about willpower. It lies in a particular local shape of "home" and "family."

A big house, three generations under one roof

Toyama has a high rate of home ownership, houses tend to be large, and three generations — grandparents, parents, children — often live together, a share that is high by national standards. That arrangement quietly powers dual-income life. While both parents are out working, grandparents are home: they handle the school run, the sudden fever, the gap after daycare and on days off. The very thing that stumps city couples — "who watches the kids?" — is often solved within the family itself. Not every household is like this, and living together brings its own small considerations; but this way of living has long been credited with supporting dual incomes.

Working women as simply normal

Women keeping a job here isn't treated as anything special. Many keep working after marriage and after having children, reinforced by seeing their own mothers and older peers do the same — everyone around you does it, so you do too. A history of manufacturing and local industry needing women's labor is one cited reason, though no single cause is definitive, and exact rankings shift by survey and year. The broad tendency — "lots of dual-income households in Toyama" — is what gets repeated.

The shape of the family sets the shape of work

Seen from abroad, both parents working while raising children is hard everywhere — and the answer depends on a region's family shape. Toyama's answer has long been: a big house, three generations, grandparents nearby. The grandparents watch over the children; the parents go to work with less worry. It's less a policy than a long-inherited habit of daily life. It is changing slowly — more young couples live separately now — but the sense of "raising kids as a whole family" still runs deep. Toyama's high rate of dual-income households isn't just a statistic; it's an expression of how people live here.
