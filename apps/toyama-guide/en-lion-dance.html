Follow the Flutes: Toyama's Autumn Belongs to the Lion Dance

Come to Toyama between September and October and you will start hearing flutes and drums drifting through the air. Autumn festival season has begun, and here that means shishimai — the lion dance. What tends to surprise visitors is not the dance itself but the sheer number of them: one city can hold many, the next hamlet over has its own, and so does the one after that. Toyama is said to have one of the highest concentrations of lion-dance troupes in Japan.

Why so many?

The reasons are debated and no single one settles it: gratitude for the rice harvest taking root in rice country; hamlets that long kept their independence and their own festivals; the movement of people and culture along the old Kitamae shipping route. What is clear is that this was never staged for tourists — it is a village observance. That is exactly why neighbouring troupes dance differently, with their own music and their own movements. "Our lion does it this way" is a real local feeling.

A long-bodied lion, and a tengu

Some Toyama lions have a very long cloth body with several people inside, so the animal ripples like one long creature — sometimes called a "centipede lion" (mukade-jishi). Forms vary by area, so this is not true everywhere in the prefecture. The other striking role is the one who faces the lion: a performer in a tengu mask, or a child with a staff, squaring off against the beast, leaping and striking. The lion rages, the other calms it — that is often the shape of the story, and it is a lively thing to watch.

The lion comes to your door

What stays with you is that this happens in the lanes, not on a stage. On festival day the lion moves through the hamlet and dances in front of each house — in doorways, in gardens — while flutes and drums play, children cry, and adults laugh. In many places there is a saying that if the lion bites your head, you'll stay healthy that year.

It isn't all rosy. Performers grow fewer each year; young people leave, and some troupes have paused for want of dancers. Neighbouring groups now sometimes dance together to keep going. Whether this survives in its present form is honestly not certain. So if you hear flutes in the distance on an autumn afternoon here, walk toward the sound. What you find won't be in a guidebook — it will be a village doing what it does every year.

Fact-check note
  • ✅ Toyama is described as having one of the highest numbers of lion-dance troupes in Japan (exact counts vary by survey and year, so no figure is given).
  • ✅ Autumn festivals (roughly September–October) commonly feature troupes dancing house to house.
  • ✅ Long-bodied "centipede lion" forms exist, and tengu-masked or child performers face the lion.
  • ⚠️ Why there are so many is debated (rice-harvest gratitude / hamlet independence / Kitamae route) — not settled.
  • ⚠️ Form, music and repertoire vary greatly by area; this is not uniform across the prefecture.
  • ⚠️ The "lion bites your head for health" saying differs by locality.
  • ⚠️ Declining numbers of performers and merged troupes are a general tendency; no specific group is identified.
