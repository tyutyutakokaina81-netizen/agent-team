One Food, Two Names, Depending on the Season

Some things eaten here carry a different name depending on the time of year, and this is the clearest case of it. Cooked rice, pounded only part of the way, rolled into a lump and wrapped in sweet red bean paste. In autumn it is called ohagi. In spring, the same thing is botamochi.

Named after flowers, supposedly

The explanation you usually hear is that each name comes from a seasonal flower — bush clover for the autumn one, peony for the spring one. It sounds plausible. There are competing accounts, and I could not tell you which is right.

Hardly anybody polices the distinction, either. Say botamochi in October and you will be understood, and some shops sell it as ohagi all year round. What everyone does know is that it has two names.

There is also a word for how far you crush the rice. Leaving the grains half intact is called hangoroshi in some regions, which translates uncomfortably as "half-killed" and refers to nothing more sinister than texture.

Made for the autumn equinox

Ohagi belongs above all to the equinox period in late September — a week or so around the day when daylight and darkness come out near equal. A great many households visit family graves in that week.

It is made to be set on the household Buddhist altar. I have been told the red of the beans carries a sense of warding off misfortune; that too is one account among several.

What is difficult in Toyama at this time of year is the volume. Around the equinox, ohagi made in various houses starts circulating. Some arrives from relatives, some from neighbours, and we send ours out. For a few days there are three different kinds in the fridge and nobody can remember whose is whose.

Once it has been offered, the family eats it. The offering simply becomes the afternoon snack. That continuity between ritual and ordinary eating turns up in a lot of food here.

Sweetness, and size

Some are close to a fist. The paste is sweet, the rice is substantial, and one gets you most of the way to full — closer to a light meal than to a sweet.

Home-made ones are larger still, since nobody at home is worrying about portion cost. "My grandmother's ohagi don't fit in one hand" is a complaint you hear often enough.

Rice, red beans, sugar. Three ingredients are enough. No flour, no eggs, no butter — and this is produced in quantity in kitchens every equinox, placed in front of the altar, and eaten by the family afterwards. That is roughly what a kitchen in the middle of September is doing.

Fact-check note
  • ✅ Ohagi and botamochi are essentially the same food — part-pounded cooked rice wrapped in sweet red bean paste — with a customary seasonal split in the naming (autumn / spring).
  • ✅ Visiting family graves during the autumn equinox week (roughly three days either side of the equinox) is a widespread custom, and ohagi is made and offered then.
  • ✅ Leaving the rice grains half intact is called hangoroshi in some regions.
  • ✅ The basic ingredients are rice, red beans and sugar — no wheat flour, eggs or dairy.
  • ⚠️ The bush-clover / peony origin is given as the account usually told, with competing accounts noted in the text.
  • ⚠️ "The red of the beans wards off misfortune" is likewise given as one account, not as fact.
  • ⚠️ "Hardly anybody polices the distinction" and "some shops sell it year-round" are the writer's observation, not statistics.
  • ⚠️ Reactions to size and sweetness vary between people; "one gets you most of the way to full" is the writer's own sense of it.
  • ⚠️ The equinox date shifts from year to year, so the text says only "late September" and names no date.
