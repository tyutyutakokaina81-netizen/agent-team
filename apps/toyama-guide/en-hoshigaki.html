A Fruit Too Bitter to Eat, Turned Into a Sweet by Wind Alone

Rows of orange fruit hang under the eaves of houses across Toyama every autumn. They are persimmons — peeled, strung on a cord, and left to dangle in the open air. What is worth knowing is that the fruit hanging there could not be eaten as it was.

Two kinds of persimmon

Japanese persimmons come in two kinds. There is the sweet sort you can pick off the tree and bite into. And there is the astringent sort, which makes the inside of your mouth go rough and your tongue feel numb — an effect usually attributed to tannins. The astringent kind is not a failure, though. It is often said to hold more sugar than the sweet kind, with the bitterness simply masking it. Which is why people worked out several ways to strip the astringency: steeping in spirits, warm-water treatment, and drying.

Why drying makes it sweet

Drying uses no sugar at all. You peel the fruit, hang it, and leave it to the air and the temperature. As the fruit loses water, the compounds behind the astringency are generally said to shift into a form the tongue no longer registers, while the sugar that was already there concentrates. The technical side is beyond me, but the result is not ambiguous: two or three weeks later, a fruit that numbed your mouth has become something dense and confectionery-sweet. A white bloom sometimes appears on the surface — said to be sugar drawn out from inside rather than mould. Those tend to taste the strongest.

Drying it in Hokuriku

None of this is guaranteed, because the method is at the mercy of the weather. Hokuriku autumns are humid and it rains a good deal. Hung somewhere without a draught, the fruit will mould — which is presumably why the eaves became the chosen spot: dry overhead, air still moving through. Some people say it goes better once the cold sets in. Enough attempts fail that it is less a reliable procedure than an arrangement with that particular year's weather.

Food with only time added

What makes this interesting is what is absent from it. No sugar, no heat, no equipment. Peel, hang, wait. The only additions are time and wind. Those curtains of orange under the eaves are not decoration. They are fruit that could not be eaten, partway through turning into something that can be. The half-dried ones have gone wrinkled and darker at the surface — food still in the middle of becoming itself, hung where the street can see it.

Fact-check note
  • ✅ Japanese persimmons include sweet and astringent types; the astringent type is harsh raw, mainly due to (soluble) tannins.
  • ✅ Known ways to remove astringency include drying, alcohol (shochu) treatment and warm-water treatment.
  • ✅ Hoshigaki is made by peeling and air-drying with no sugar added; the white surface bloom is sugar from the fruit itself.
  • ⚠️ "Astringent persimmons often contain more sugar" is an explanation commonly given, stated here as such rather than as fact.
  • ⚠️ The mechanism (soluble tannins becoming insoluble, so the tongue stops registering them) is given as the general account, not a technical claim.
  • ⚠️ "Humid Hokuriku causes mould / better once it turns cold" is local experience, not a guaranteed rule.
  • ⚠️ "Two or three weeks" is a rough figure depending on size and weather.
