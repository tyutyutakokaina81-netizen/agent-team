A Fruit Where You Eat the Inside and Leave the Rest

A pod, split lengthways, open at the seam, packed with something white.

This is akebi. It ripens on a vine in the hills in autumn and is picked once it has opened like that. The outside is usually purple, though some come out greenish — it depends on the type and how far it has ripened.

The white part

What you eat is the white flesh inside: jelly-like, sweet, though not emphatically so — a thin sweetness.

The difficulty is the seeds, since almost all of that flesh is attached to them. You put a piece in your mouth, work the sweetness off, and spit the seeds out. The procedure is close to eating a pomegranate. Little to show for the effort, if you want to put it that way.

What stays with me is less the flavour than the method — the seeds accumulating beside the plate.

Elsewhere they eat the skin

I have heard that in some places it is the skin that gets eaten, bitter enough to want stuffing and then frying or grilling. I have never tried it.

The same fruit, and which half you eat depends on where you are. I would not say either is the correct answer.

Never bought one

I have almost certainly never bought an akebi. You see them at a farm stand now and then in autumn; I cannot remember ever seeing them in a supermarket.

Poor keeping, poor travelling, not much of it edible — plenty of reasons come to mind, none of which I have checked.

Mostly they arrive by way of someone who has been up in the hills, which is how ours turns up: on the kitchen counter once or twice each autumn, provenance unknown.

In Toyama the hills start immediately behind the flat. There are mountains within sight of the town, and going up and coming back is not a day's undertaking — so things from the hills reach lowland kitchens by being handed person to person rather than by entering a supply chain. Mushrooms and chestnuts mostly travel that way too.

Where its sweetness sits

Fruit now is mostly sweet; breeding has made a sour one harder to find. Against that, akebi is decidedly mild.

As a child I found it thin. These days the thinness reads to me as a sign of something that grew where it grew — which may only mean I have got used to it.

Someone will almost certainly bring one round again this year.

Fact-check note
  • ✅ Akebi is the fruit of a climbing vine; the rind splits lengthways as it ripens in autumn.
  • ✅ Rind colour varies — purple is common, but greenish fruit occurs, depending on type and ripeness.
  • ✅ The part usually eaten is the white pulp inside, which contains many seeds.
  • ✅ In some regions the rind is cooked and eaten — stuffed and then fried or grilled are known preparations.
  • ✅ It is not a fruit with steady supermarket distribution; farm stands and personal foraging are the usual routes.
  • ⚠️ "Some places eat the skin" is given as something heard, and the text says the writer has never tried it. No region is named.
  • ⚠️ The suggested reasons it does not travel (keeping, transport, low edible yield) are stated in the text as guesses that have not been checked.
  • ⚠️ Sweetness is perceived differently by different people; "mild" is the writer's own sense, and the text raises the possibility that he has simply grown used to it.
  • ⚠️ Ripening shifts with the year and the place, so the text says only "autumn".
  • ⚠️ Foraging is subject to landowner permission and local rules. This is not a guide to picking it.
  • ⚠️ No district, mountain, farm stand or personal name is given (prefecture level only).
