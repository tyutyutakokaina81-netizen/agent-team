The Electricity Here Was Snow Six Months Ago

Most people rarely think about where the electricity in their house comes from. I don't either, most days. Living in Toyama, though, there are a few moments a year when you are reminded — and they happen while it is snowing.

Height and water, both

The prefecture has three-thousand-metre mountains along its eastern edge and a short run from there to the sea, which makes the rivers steep. Steep rivers suit hydroelectric generation, because the same water falls further.

Those mountains also take a great deal of snow in winter, and snow does not run off at once. It melts gradually from spring into summer, so the water arrives spread out over months rather than all together. Height, and water that keeps coming. Hydro has been generated here for a long time, and the share of the prefecture's generation that comes from it is said to be on the high side. I am not going to put a figure on it, because the figure moves year to year and with how it is counted. For the avoidance of doubt: not all the electricity here is hydro — there is large thermal generation in the prefecture too.

Snow as a resource

Snow in Hokuriku is normally discussed as a nuisance. Clearing it is hard labour, roads and trains stop, and there is infrastructure that exists specifically to deal with it.

But the same snow becomes electricity. What fell out of the sky in winter is held in the mountains, released slowly as water from spring onward, and ends up as the light in a room. Knowing that shifted something for me. I cannot honestly claim to think "this is next summer's electricity" while shovelling, but knowing it is not purely an obstacle does change the mood of the job.

Not an unmixed good

Building dams and stations means altering a river and putting what was there under water. Settlements have been relocated for such projects in this country, and there are quiet lake surfaces with a different landscape beneath them.

Snowfall is not constant either. Light winters and heavy ones, and generation varies with them, so what looks stable still depends on the weather.

The relationship is legible, though: what falls on this prefecture becomes some of this prefecture's electricity. Energy does not feel like something arriving from far away here.

Anyone visiting in winter will probably find the snow exhausting. I find it exhausting every year. And somewhere in the middle of shovelling it, I sometimes remember that the same snow will be lighting someone's room in a few months' time. It does not make the shovelling shorter. It did change what I think the snow is.
