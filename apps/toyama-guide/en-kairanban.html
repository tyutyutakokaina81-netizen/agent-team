It Has Stopped at My House

Three days it sat by the door.

A thin binder with the neighbourhood notices clipped inside: read them, stamp your seal, carry it to the next house. That is the whole task, and it keeps getting put off.

The next house is fixed

The order is set — after us it goes to a particular house.

So if I hold it, everything downstream holds too. Knowing that does bother me. But the moment I start calculating whether they will be home when I walk over, my feet stop: evenings are late, mornings are early.

I do read it

Recycling collection days, the schedule for cutting the grass along the road, a reminder about fire safety.

Little of it concerns me directly, but I read it, stamp it, and set it down. Setting it down is where it stops.

I know I am the one stopping it

The date it left the previous house is written on it, so I know exactly how many days I have had it.

I know, and I still leave it there. The excuse I give myself is that none of it is urgent — though not being urgent and being fine to delay are two different things.

In Toyama

Some neighbourhoods here have a long walk between houses; ours is a short one.

And it still took three days. Distance is not the issue. I will take it over this evening, probably.

Having thought that, sometimes another two days go by.

Fact-check note
  • ✅ Neighbourhood associations in many parts of Japan circulate notices house to house in a binder.
  • ✅ A seal or signature is commonly used to mark receipt.
  • ⚠️ Order, frequency and format vary widely by area; this is written as the writer's own household.
  • ⚠️ The listed notices are examples, not a standard set.
  • ⚠️ "Some neighbourhoods have a long walk between houses" is a general statement; no district is named.
  • ⚠️ Moves to digitise or abolish the practice are not discussed — regional variation is large and nothing was checked.
  • ⚠️ No district, household or individual is identified (prefecture level only).
