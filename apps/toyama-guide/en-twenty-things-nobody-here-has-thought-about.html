Twenty Things Nobody Here Has Thought About

People visiting from abroad have asked me things I could not answer. What is that music at five in the evening? Why does one house have two places to pray? Can you actually eat those? All of them are things I see every day — and seeing them every day is exactly why I had never worked out the reason. You only think about it when someone asks, and then it turns out you cannot say.

For about two years I have been writing those down one at a time. Here are twenty of them. I suspect they are about equally interesting whether or not you are planning to come.

Music plays from loudspeakers across the whole town in the early evening — not from one house, but from poles standing high over the streets. People hearing it for the first time sometimes brace for an emergency; it is only telling the time, and partly telling children to go home. Most towns have the system, and the hour and the tune differ from place to place.

One house has two places to pray: a Buddhist altar in the living room and a Shinto shelf above the kitchen. Saying people "follow two religions" is not quite right. Both feel less like belief than like fixed places inside a house. Every time I try to explain it, it comes out wrong.

The sound of insects is called a voice. From September the nights fill with it, and Japanese treats it as something you listen to rather than noise — it appears in classical poetry and functions as a season word. It is a signal that autumn has arrived.

People pick things up off the pavement and eat them. From late September there is a strong, unpleasant smell under the ginkgo trees. What surprises visitors is what happens next: the nuts inside are collected, in parks and under street trees, by people with bags. The outside smells; roasted, the kernel is something else entirely.

A fruit too astringent to eat is turned into a sweet by wind alone. Persimmons hang in orange rows under the eaves in autumn. One bite of the astringent kind stops your mouth. Peeled and hung, with no fire and no sugar, they shrink over weeks until sugar comes to the surface and whitens them.

Clothes change by date, not by temperature. The first of June and the first of October switch school uniforms over. Hot on the first of October, you still change into long sleeves; that was the original form, though most schools now allow a transition period. Every year I think it would make more sense to go by the weather, and every year I go by the date.

The same food changes its name between spring and autumn. Rice, lightly crushed and wrapped in sweet bean paste, is ohagi in autumn and botamochi in spring. The recipe is the same; the names come from seasonal flowers.

Water comes up out of the road surface to melt snow. In snow country there are small holes down the middle of the carriageway, and in winter groundwater rises through them, so snow melts before it settles and you do not wait for a plough. The inspections happen in autumn, long before any snow.

Fast water runs through the middle of town. Irrigation channels pass between houses, often without railings, with signs warning you not to fall in — which means people do, and accidents happen. The channels were there for the fields, and the town grew around them.

Chestnuts are not sweetened. Autumn rice with chestnuts is rice, chestnuts and a little salt. People who know candied chestnuts find the plainness surprising. Salt alone, the thinking goes, brings out what sweetness the chestnut has.

Some people forecast the winter by counting insects. Stink bugs come in with the laundry in autumn; they do not bite or spoil food, but handle one wrong and the room smells. A year with many of them is said to mean heavy snow. Whether that holds, I do not know.

The mountain goes white while you are still in short sleeves. September is hot, and yet the summit turns white on a particular day. There is a word for it, and a meteorological office decides it from a fixed observation point. Green in front, white behind.

A harvested field turns green again. After cutting, rows of brown stubble stand for a while, and then new shoots come up. Nobody harvests them; they stay through the winter. Something that finished appears to start over.

The same rice variety tastes different in autumn. New-crop rice holds more moisture, so it cooks up plump and glossy. Same variety, same fields — different bowl.

There is a night for looking at the moon and nothing else. It falls on the fifteenth day of the eighth lunar month, so the date moves from year to year, somewhere in September or October. Some households set out dumplings; some do nothing but look.

The smell arrives before you turn the corner. Osmanthus flowers are tiny, the size of a grain of rice, packed orange along the branch. You smell them first, and the flowering lasts only about a week.

Red dragonflies come down from the mountains. I assumed they hatched in autumn because the numbers jump; in fact they spend the hot months higher up and descend as it cools. They did not multiply — they came back.

People eat something deliberately flavourless. Tokoroten is seaweed boiled, set, and pushed into strands. It has almost no taste of its own and is eaten with vinegar-soy or with black syrup, depending on the region. A food praised for having no flavour is a hard thing to explain.

People try to get cooler by means of a sound. A glass wind chime hangs under the eaves and rings when air moves. The room is no cooler. It still feels cooler — and it is also a way of knowing that there is a breeze at all.

People throw water on the ground to get through summer. Water goes onto the entrance and the garden in the evening; morning or evening rather than the hottest part of the day, so that the heat leaves with the evaporation. Even houses with air conditioning do it, less for the cooling than because it is one of the shapes summer takes.

Two things struck me once they were lined up. The first is that the things I cannot explain are exactly the things I see every day — a sight has an explanation attached, a year it was built and a reason it is known, and daily things have none of that. The second is that not knowing has never been a problem: I do not know why the five o'clock music plays, but when it plays I think, ah, five o'clock, and that has done me for thirty years.

I live in Takaoka, in Toyama, and write two of these a day. Mostly seasonal, so autumn will become winter soon, and in a place with this much snow that means clearing it, being stopped by it, and waiting for it to melt. The snow-melting pipes in number eight have not run yet this year. When they do, that gets written too.
