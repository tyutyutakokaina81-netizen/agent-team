Two Places to Pray, in One House

There is a Buddhist altar in our living room. High up near the kitchen, in the same house, there is a Shinto shelf. Two religions, sharing one address.

I have yet to find a way of putting that which lands, because "practising two religions at once" does not feel like an accurate description of what is happening.

They do different jobs

Put roughly, the butsudan is where you face family members who have died, and the kamidana is where you wish for the household's safety and its daily business — that is how it is usually explained. The altar holds memorial tablets and receives water, flowers and food. The shelf, in most houses that have one, receives rice, salt and water.

Someone will put their hands together at the altar before leaving in the morning. The same person changes the water on the shelf. No contradiction is being felt, as far as I can tell.

Plenty of houses have neither, or only one. I know a home where a photograph stands in for the altar entirely. Practices and offerings differ by sect. So "Japanese houses are like this" is not a claim I can make — ours is like this, and that is as far as it goes.

They take turns across the year

A few times a year one of them gets more attention than the other. Around the autumn equinox it is the altar; at New Year it is the Shinto shelf.

What is normally a moment of hands-together in the morning turns into extra cleaning and changed offerings for that stretch. In Toyama you can see the season arrive in the shops: once the equinox week starts, altar flowers are stacked out front, so you notice it while doing the shopping.

I am not really talking about faith. It sits in the house as a habit, and that is closer to how it feels — less like praying than like greeting each in turn, which probably sounds too light.

Having two was the normal thing

Shinto and Buddhism were treated in an intermingled state here for a very long time, and there was a state effort to separate them in the Meiji period. Inside houses, though, some of the mixture stayed where it was.

Both objects being in the house now is, I assume, a residue of that. Less something anyone chose and arranged than something inherited already assembled.

The Shinto shelf is high up, so it is in view the moment you walk into the room. The altar's doors are often closed, so you would not know it was there unless someone said so. Both in one house, and one of them is visible while the other is not. The morning routine does not change either way: the water above gets replaced, the doors below get opened. That is the whole of what gets done each day.

Fact-check note
  • ✅ Homes in Japan containing both a Buddhist altar and a Shinto shelf are not unusual. The altar is generally described as the place for ancestors and the dead, the shelf as the place for Shinto deities.
  • ✅ Memorial tablets are kept in the butsudan, which receives water, flowers and food; rice, salt and water are the offerings generally described for the kamidana.
  • ✅ Some families tend the altar and visit graves at the autumn equinox, which falls in late September.
  • ✅ Shinto and Buddhism were long syncretised in Japan, and a state policy of separating them was carried out in the Meiji period.
  • ⚠️ No generalisation about "Japanese homes" is made. The text states that some homes have neither and some have only one.
  • ⚠️ Offerings and practices differ by sect and region, so the text keeps to "usually", "in most houses", "as it is generally explained".
  • ⚠️ "A residue of the syncretic period" is the writer's reading, not an account of any particular household's history.
  • ⚠️ The article does not enter into doctrine or the meaning of belief, and does not explain or evaluate any particular religion.
