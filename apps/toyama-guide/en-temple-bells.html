Where Japan's Temple Bells Are Cast: Takaoka, the Sound of Japan

On New Year's Eve in Japan, temple bells ring out slow, deep tones — a sound a lot of people carry in their memory as "the sound of Japan." What most visitors don't know is that a great many of those bells are said to be cast in Takaoka, the mid-sized city on the Toyama coast where I live. I grew up taking it for granted; it was only when I traveled elsewhere that I realized how unusual it is to come from the town that makes the country's bells.

A town that has cast metal for 400 years

Takaoka's metalworking goes back to the early Edo period, when the local domain invited casters to settle here. For about four hundred years the town has melted, poured and hammered copper and brass into tools and Buddhist wares, and the craft — Takaoka copperware — is recognized as a traditional Japanese handicraft. If you want the hands-on, where-to-see-it side of that, see the Takaoka copperware guide; this page is about one particular thing the town makes: sound.

Making a sound, not just a shape

What I find striking, as a resident, is that these artisans are shaping sound as much as metal. A temple bell (bonshō) isn't just a big lump of bronze — small changes in its thickness and form change how it rings and how long the note lingers, so bell-making is partly acoustic design. The same is true of the orin, the small clear-toned bell tapped at Buddhist home altars: that pure sound and long fade come from the alloy and the shape. Some workshops here now cast orin and wind chimes from tin, with a startlingly clean tone — even a wind chime ringing under a summer eave is, in a sense, an extension of this town's craft.

You've probably already heard Takaoka

Takaoka also has a large seated bronze Buddha — the Great Buddha — and it, the bells and the wind chimes are all siblings born of the same casting craft. So the bell you once heard at some far-off temple, or the little altar bell in a Japanese inn, may well trace back here. To be honest and clear: not every bell in Japan is from Takaoka, and there are other casting centers — but the share attributed to this town is large enough that, as someone from here, I feel a quiet pride in it.

How to experience it as a visitor

  • Kanaya-machi — the old casters' quarter, with stone-paved lanes and latticed houses. Walk it to feel the town's long relationship with metal. (See the copperware guide for workshops and shops.)
  • The Great Buddha — a bronze Buddha in the town center, an easy stop that shows the same casting craft at scale.
  • Try the sound yourself — some craft shops let you tap an orin or a tin wind chime and hear the tone; availability varies by shop and day.
  • Listen — early mornings you may catch a temple bell; near the workshops you can sometimes hear the dry ring of metal being worked.

Fact-check note
  • ✅ Certain: Takaoka has about 400 years of metal-casting history; "Takaoka copperware" is a nationally designated traditional craft, and the town produces temple bells, Buddhist altar fittings, bronze statues and orin. (Widely documented.)
  • ✅ Certain: Kanaya-machi is the historic casters' quarter, and Takaoka has a large bronze Great Buddha. (First-hand / widely known.)
  • ⚠️ Requires checking: The exact share of Japan's temple bells or altar fittings made in Takaoka — often described as "a large proportion," but figures vary by source, so I state it as a claim, not a precise number. Individual temples' bells have their own makers.
  • ⚠️ Requires checking: Which specific workshops offer hands-on bell/orin experiences, and their hours — confirm locally before visiting.
