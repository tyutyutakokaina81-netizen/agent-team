Toyama Dialect: What "Kitokito" Means & a Few Useful Local Words

If you come to Toyama, you'll start seeing one word again and again — on market signs, on fishmongers' banners, and even in the name of the airport: Toyama Kitokito Airport. Most visitors smile at the sound of it and wonder what it means. I grew up here, so I never thought about it much until friends from outside the region kept asking. Here's the short, honest version, plus a couple of other local words that make the place a little warmer once you know them.

"Kitokito" — fresh and full of life

Kitokito is a Toyama-dialect word that's usually explained as "fresh and lively." The classic image is a just-caught fish, still glistening and firm — that liveliness is the feeling the word captures. It doesn't translate cleanly into English; "fresh" is close but flatter. When people here see good fish, they'll say something like "this one's kitokito." That's exactly why the local airport carries the name — it's a friendly way of saying the whole area is fresh and full of energy.

There's a reason a fishing-and-farming region would keep a word like this. Fish pulled from Toyama Bay can be on the table the same day, so whether something is kitokito is genuinely the thing people care about most. The word mirrors how people live. (If you want the food side of that, see white shrimp and what to eat in Toyama.)

A few other words you might hear

  • Naan (なーん) — a soft way of saying "no," or "you're welcome / don't mention it." Reply to a thank-you with "naan, naan" and it carries an easy, unpretentious warmth.
  • Tsukaen (つかえん) — roughly "no problem, it's fine." "Can I have this?" "Tsukaen yo." It's more relaxed than the standard-Japanese equivalent.
  • Kitokito (きときと) — as above; you'll see it far more often than you'll need to say it, but recognizing it helps.

People in Toyama are often described as quiet rather than showy — a Hokuriku temperament, not given to loud self-promotion — but warm and helpful once you're actually dealing with them. That character comes through in the gentle sound of the dialect.

Why it's worth knowing as a visitor

You don't need to study any of this. But knowing just one word — kitokito — can make a small moment friendlier. Choosing fish at a market or a sushi counter, you can ask, "Is this kitokito?" and you'll almost certainly get a smile and a nod. Local language is the closest, cheapest doorway to the people of a place, and this one is easy to carry home. English is limited in smaller shops, so a translation app still helps for anything beyond a word or two.
