The Banner Goes Up Every Autumn; Whether I Can Taste It Is Another Matter

Honestly, I cannot tell new-crop soba from ordinary soba.

Every autumn the banners appear outside the soba shops — shin-soba — and I have gone in because of one. But I have never eaten the two side by side, so it stops at "if you say it's different, I suppose it is."

Harvested twice a year

Buckwheat comes in twice, in summer and in autumn. The autumn crop is usually the one that gets the name, and it is said to have the better aroma.

I write "said to" because hearing it said is all I have. How many people have actually compared the same shop's summer and autumn?

Aroma is also supposed to fall away with time since milling, since the dough was cut, since the noodles left the water. If so, how the shop worked that day may matter more than how new the grain is. An amateur's guess, that.

Talking about soba in Toyama

This is rice country. Sushi counters and ramen shops are more visible here than soba shops.

The banners still go up in autumn — rice country does not mean nobody eats soba. But you rarely hear the prefecture named as a buckwheat producer, and the growing regions printed on menus are usually elsewhere.

Jūwari and nihachi

Noodles made from buckwheat alone are jūwari; nihachi has twenty percent wheat flour worked in. The wheat is there to hold the dough together, not to cut corners.

Jūwari smells stronger and breaks more easily, and can come out crumbly. Nihachi is smoother in the mouth. Neither is above the other — it is preference, and the skill of the shop.

A twenty-percent difference with its own name. That degree of fineness must look odd from outside. If bread flour ratios each came with a name, I would not remember them either.

Still eating it, still not knowing

The banner makes me want to go in, and that is not because I know the difference. It is because I know it belongs to the season.

This year I intend to eat at the same shop in summer as well. There is no other way to find out.

Fact-check note
  • ✅ Buckwheat is grown in both summer and autumn cropping cycles, and the autumn harvest is commonly what "shin-soba" refers to.
  • ✅ Jūwari is pure buckwheat; nihachi contains twenty percent wheat flour, used as a binder.
  • ✅ Jūwari is described as more aromatic but more fragile; nihachi as smoother in texture.
  • ⚠️ The article does not claim shin-soba tastes better. The writer states up front that he cannot tell, and never claims to have worked it out.
  • ⚠️ "Said to have the better aroma" is reported speech, with no source claimed.
  • ⚠️ "Freshness of milling/cutting/boiling may matter more" is explicitly flagged in the text as an amateur's guess.
  • ⚠️ "Rice, sushi and ramen are more visible than soba here" is the writer's lived impression, not shop-count data.
  • ⚠️ The terms and ratios vary between shops and regions; they are not given as strict definitions.
  • ⚠️ Harvest timing shifts by region and year, so no dates are given.
  • ⚠️ No shop, district, producing region or personal name is given (prefecture level only).
