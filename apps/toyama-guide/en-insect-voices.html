There Is a Word for It, and It Isn't "Noise"

Something changes in Toyama nights once September arrives. On the evenings when you can finally sleep with a window open, a sound starts up out of the dark — insects. Japanese has a phrase for it: mushi no ne, or mushi no koe — literally the insects' voice. Not chirping, not noise: a voice. Visitors sometimes stop at that word, because in English the same sound tends to get filed under noise.

Treated as something to listen to

In Japan these autumn insects have long been treated as something to be listened to. They appear in classical poetry and work as seasonal markers, received as a signal of the turning year rather than as background racket. People also distinguish species by sound — the bell cricket rendered one way, the field cricket another — though the exact onomatopoeia varies from book to book and person to person, and is not fixed. You may also come across the claim that Japanese people process insect sounds like language; that is disputed and far from settled, so it is left aside here.

The nights get longer

Part of why the sound carries is simply that the nights are lengthening. The sun drops earlier, the heat eases, and there are stretches of days when you can leave the window open. The air conditioner goes off and only a screen door stands between the room and the field. In Toyama many houses sit near paddies and long grass, so the sound is close — going all night after the lights are out. It ought to be loud enough to keep you awake, and somehow it isn't. You mostly notice it when it stops, and then you know autumn is over.

A quiet that isn't silence

Japanese quiet does not always mean the absence of sound. A night full of insect voices can register as quiet — which sounds contradictory until you have heard it. A sound that is steady, regular and asks nothing of you becomes part of the silence rather than an interruption of it. If you ever stay here in autumn, open the window a little at night and just listen. Why it came to be called a voice is quicker to hear than to explain.
