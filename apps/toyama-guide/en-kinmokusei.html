The Smell Rounds the Corner First

The flowers themselves are startlingly small — each about the size of a grain of rice, orange, packed tightly along the branches. Standing under the tree and looking up, it does not feel like looking at large flowers at all.

You notice it in the wrong order

With kinmokusei, the smell arrives before the sight.

It reaches you before you have turned the corner, and when you look up there is the tree, a little way ahead. You do not find the flowers by looking for them; the smell tells you where they are.

It is described as a strong scent, and on a windy day you can pick it up from some tens of metres away — my own impression, not a measurement. Some people love it; some find it overpowering.

The air freshener problem

The scent was widely used in Japanese toilet air fresheners, so for some people the flower leads straight to a memory of a bathroom.

How strongly depends on your age. Current air fresheners mostly smell of other things, so it does not always land with younger people. I am just about old enough to get it.

The flower came first and the product copied it, and yet the order is reversed in memory. That happens with other things too.

About a week

The flowering is brief — late September into October, a week or ten days, varying by year.

Rain brings the whole lot down at once, and often what tells you it is over is the orange patch on the ground under the tree, the fallen flowers lying flat against it.

In Toyama it turns up as a garden tree, in hedges and in parks. It is not a flower you go to see; it is one that comes to you while you are walking.

Next year it will happen again, on the same corner, in the same way. The tree flowers reliably every year. It is the noticing that I forget.
