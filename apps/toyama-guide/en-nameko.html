Slippery Is Not Usually a Compliment

Autumn puts mushrooms into the miso soup in Toyama kitchens, and the days with nameko in it are a little different: the soup itself goes thick. That thickness comes from the slime coating the mushroom — and slippery food, I gather, is not much of a selling point in most countries, which makes this the dish I always stumble over when I try to explain it. The quality other people list as a drawback is the one being sold here.

The slime is the point

Nameko are small, brown-capped and wet-looking, already glossy inside the bag they are sold in. Dropped into miso soup they lend the whole bowl a viscous quality, closer to swallowing than to sipping. They are usually paired with tofu, whose own smoothness blurs into theirs until it is hard to tell in the mouth where one stops and the other starts.

What is worth holding onto is that the slime is not something to be washed off. It is the reason the mushroom is in the bowl, and most people say they give nameko a quick rinse at most; scrub it away and half the reason for adding them goes with it.

Japan has a whole category of this

There are a surprising number of foods here built on the same quality — natto, grated yam, okra, mekabu seaweed — all valued for precisely what would count against them elsewhere. Why that came about I genuinely don't know. Health claims get made, and I will leave those alone. What does seem to be there is an extra axis for judging texture: not only firm and soft, but slick and thick as a kind of good in themselves.

It is, honestly, awkward at first. The mushrooms slide off your chopsticks, and the soup runs hotter than it looks.

The shortest recipe of the season

Nameko soup takes almost no time: stock, mushrooms, tofu, dissolve the miso. Five minutes from deciding to eat. Set against the chestnut rice that costs an hour of peeling, or the persimmons left hanging under the eaves for weeks, that is nearly the opposite proposition — the same season, at the other end of the effort scale.

And on a cold autumn morning the thickened version seems to hold its heat longer than the thin one does. Japanese tables keep a category for things that are good precisely because they are slippery; nameko sits at the near end of it, and comes out fastest.

Fact-check note
  • ✅ Nameko are a mushroom coated in a viscous substance, commonly eaten in Japan.
  • ✅ They are a standard miso-soup ingredient, often with tofu, and thicken the broth.
  • ✅ Rinsing lightly rather than washing hard is the usual advice, to keep that coating.
  • ✅ Natto, grated yam, okra and mekabu are further examples of foods valued for viscosity.
  • ⚠️ Health benefits of the coating are commonly claimed; no such claim is made here.
  • ⚠️ The idea of "smoothness" as a distinct axis of Japanese taste is the writer's reading, not an established fact.
  • ⚠️ "Seems to hold its heat longer" is described from experience, not from measurement.
  • ⚠️ Cultivated nameko are sold year-round; "autumn" here describes the seasonal table, not availability.
  • ⚠️ "Five minutes" is a rough figure that depends on how the stock is made.
