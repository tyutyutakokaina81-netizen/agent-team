Fish, Still Shaped Like Fish

In a supermarket here, the fish are laid out whole. Head, eye, tail, lined up facing the same way on crushed ice. Having grown up with it, I did not know this was unusual; apparently, for anyone who has only ever seen fillets, standing in front of that counter is a surprise.

Because they are whole, there are things to look at before you buy. The eye, first: clear or clouded, clouded meaning it has been sitting a while. Some people check the gills, lifting them to see whether the colour is still red, though plenty of shops now ask you not to handle the fish, so most people only look. The old test of pressing the flesh to see whether it springs back is going the same way. In the end you judge by eye — but the point is that by eye you can. With a fillet, none of that is available to you.

Buy a whole fish and you will usually be asked, before you reach the till, whether you want it cut: into three, into two, or just gutted and beheaded. They take it away to the back of the counter and you wait a few minutes. In most shops this is free — most, because I have not checked them all. Seen from outside this seems to be the strange part: the shop that sold you the fish will also take it apart for you, so you can buy a whole fish without ever using a knife at home.

What is left after that is bones and head, and if you ask whether you can keep them, they will generally wrap them up for you. These are called ara. They go into miso soup, or get simmered; some people will tell you they are better than the fillet. A few shops put ara out on the counter from the start, very cheaply. That only exists because the cutting happens inside the shop — the counter sells the by-product of its own work.

The sashimi shelf changes through the day. Thin in the morning, filling out after midday, fullest in the early evening, timed for dinner. Later on the discount stickers go on, and there are people who wait for them, drifting slowly past the shelf. The same fish costs differently at two in the afternoon and at eight at night, so anyone not in a hurry goes at night.

A mixed sashimi plate holds several kinds at once, and what is on it turns over with the seasons: firefly squid in spring, white shrimp in their weeks — and white shrimp is not the same thing as sweet shrimp, so ordering the latter here will not get you the former. In winter the fattier fish arrive, visibly paler at the cut. You could work out roughly what month it is from the plate without looking at a calendar.

Small labels appear on some packs reading jimono, meaning caught nearby. Not everything is; plenty has travelled, which is why the label needs to exist at all. Local is sometimes dearer and sometimes cheaper, depending on the catch — a big catch makes it cheap, and on a poor day there are simply fewer labels. You can read the state of the sea off the counter, in the sense that the counter is the only part of it I actually see.

Stacks of polystyrene boxes sit at the edge of the floor or just behind it: the boxes the fish arrived in, still damp from the ice. People take them home for garden tools, or for pickling in winter, and some shops will hand one over if you ask. You go shopping and come back with a fish and a box, and the box is the larger item.

The counter has a smell, and I understand people who dislike it, though a counter with fresh fish on it does not smell strong — it is closer to water and ice. When it does get strong there is probably another reason, and since I am guessing, I will leave it there. It is also noticeably cold, because it is chilled, which is why in summer certain people linger.

The shelf that stops visitors, I think, is the one with sashimi already pressed between sheets of kelp. White-fleshed fish, mostly, sold that way in a tray: raw fish laid against kombu and left to rest, so that moisture leaves it, the flesh firms, and the kelp's taste moves in. It becomes a different food from sashimi. People make it at home, but you can also simply buy it made — not kelp to press your own with, but the finished thing. There is a reason households around here keep so much kelp in the cupboard, which I have written about elsewhere. The logic even extends to beef pressed with kelp, at which point, I am told, some people feel it has gone too far.

Beside the raw fish sit the salted and the dried. Some carry a label saying overnight-dried, which is what it means, though whether it is literally one night I could not say. Dried keeps longer and only needs grilling, so on a weekday it is usually what dinner ends up being; the salted fillets are what turns up at breakfast. The same species can be there in all three states at once — raw, salted, dried — and which one you buy is decided by what time you expect to get home.

Cut fish comes in more than one form, too. Blocks for sashimi, fillets for grilling, and separately, pieces cut for simmering: chunks with the bone left in, because bones make the broth better, so the shop does not remove them. Some trays are labelled for soup, or for hotpot. Rather than deciding on a dish and then shopping for it, people look at the counter and let it decide the evening.

Once a year the counter visibly changes. At the end of December, large fish appear whole, in sizes that are never stocked otherwise. The crowd changes too — people who never buy fish turn up, and a queue forms of customers waiting to have one cut. Everything for New Year arrives at once and the colours of the counter change with it. For those few days, the number of people buying a whole fish clearly goes up: even the fillet-buyers take one home entire.

Small children tend to stop in front of it. They look at the faces, and do not move when called. It is, after all, about the only place where food still has a face on it — the meat counter has none, nor do the vegetables. I am not going to turn that into a lesson about teaching children where food comes from. It is only that there is still somewhere in a shop worth stopping at. I stopped at the same spot myself.

Unfamiliar fish show up fairly often, because the bay here is deep and deep-water species come up with strange shapes you would not know how to approach. You ask. The person behind the counter will tell you — simmer it, grill it — and the answer is short. It is not a recipe, and it is enough.

Someone who works at one told me that fewer people buy whole fish now. Fillets sell; there is no work in them and the quantity is easy to judge. Whole fish are still there, presumably because some people still buy them, though the amount on display does feel smaller than it used to — feels, that is, I have not counted. Perhaps one day the fish shape will go from these counters. Perhaps not.

When visitors ask what to see, I list the obvious places and then suggest the supermarket fish counter, which usually gets me an odd look. But the most honest display of what a place actually eats is not the market and not a tourist facility — the market starts early and has a face it turns to visitors. The supermarket has what local people are eating that day, at the price they really pay. A fish lies there whole, and one of them becomes somebody's dinner. That is all it is, and I still think it is worth looking at.
