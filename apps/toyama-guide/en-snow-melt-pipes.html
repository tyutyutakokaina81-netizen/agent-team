Roads That Water Themselves So the Snow Can't Settle

Winter talk starts early in Toyama. There are still warm days in September, and yet people mention that the peaks have gone white, and from there the conversation turns to the roads — because September and October are when the roads get checked. Not the snowploughs. The road surface itself, which here has water plumbed into it.

The holes down the middle of the lane

Walk a street in Hokuriku and you will notice small holes set at regular intervals along the centre of the lane. Not manholes, not drains. In winter, water comes up out of them, wets the asphalt, and melts the snow as it lands. Some installations run off a snow sensor and some are switched on by hand; either way the aim is to wet the surface before anything can accumulate. These are shōsetsu pipes — snow-melting pipes — and they are utterly ordinary here. Some households have their own set laid into the driveway, and flicking a switch before taking the car out is simply part of a winter morning.

Where the water comes from

It is mostly groundwater, which holds a fairly steady temperature through the year and therefore arrives warmer than the winter air. That is not cost-free. Drawing large volumes raises questions about the ground itself, and some areas have rules or shared-well arrangements around how much may be pumped. I do not know the regulations in any detail, but "you cannot simply run it forever" is understood locally.

Nor is it a cure-all. The surface ends up wet, and on a hard freeze that water can turn to ice and make the road worse rather than better. What it does reliably is stop snow from piling up, and between a street that has these and a street that does not, winter is a different experience.

Not moving the snow — preventing it

Clearing snow normally means relocating it: push it, truck it, find somewhere to put it. This is the opposite approach. Rather than shifting what has landed, it stops the snow existing on the road at all — which means the pipework has to be decided on when the road is built, long before any particular winter.

So the autumn inspection is really a rehearsal, confirming that this year's system will run. While it is still short-sleeve weather, the road is getting ready for a winter that everyone here already knows is coming. A visitor will probably notice the holes only once water is coming out of them; the arrangement that puts it there was made seasons earlier.

Fact-check note
  • ✅ Shōsetsu pipes spray water from outlets in the road surface to melt falling snow, and are widely used in snowy regions including Hokuriku.
  • ✅ Groundwater is the main source; it is warmer than winter air and so can melt snow.
  • ✅ Some households install them on their own driveways.
  • ✅ Because the surface stays wet, a hard freeze can cause re-icing and make the road slippery.
  • ⚠️ Sensor-triggered vs manual operation is described as a general difference; it varies by installation.
  • ⚠️ Groundwater pumping rules and shared-well arrangements are noted as existing in some areas; no specific regulation is claimed.
  • ⚠️ Ground subsidence is given as a concern that gets raised, not as an asserted causal outcome.
