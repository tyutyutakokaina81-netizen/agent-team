Six in the Morning, for a Patch of Ground

We leave the house before six on sports day. Events start at nine.

The reason is the ground. Families spread a sheet around the edge of the schoolyard to claim somewhere to sit, and the earlier you arrive the better the position.

What the day actually is

Japanese primary and middle schools hold a day — or half a day — in which the whole school competes outdoors: sprints, relays, tug of war, a game where beanbags are thrown into a raised basket, plus dance or display pieces by year group.

Parents come to watch. Grandparents too. That assumption of an audience is what produces the queuing.

Not everywhere, mind. Some schools ban the practice outright and others run a ballot, and it already differs between schools near us. Nobody should picture the entire country lined up at dawn.

What has changed

Half-day events are more common now. When I was a child it ran from morning to evening; what I hear about these days is finishing before noon and going home.

A schoolyard in September is still hot. Shortening the day, or moving the whole thing to spring, is done to keep children out of the heat. Human-pyramid displays have been scaled back or dropped at some schools on safety grounds, which has been widely reported.

Some schools no longer have the family picnic lunch, since half a day does not span midday. People who loved that hour will find the change a loss; whether spending a whole day on a baking schoolyard was itself a good thing is another question.

A September schoolyard in Toyama

September here can still feel like an extension of summer. Strong sun, no shade on the yard, the tents reserved for officials, guests and any child who is unwell.

Rain is the other half of it. A reserve date sits in the calendar and the call comes the evening before, so there is a night spent waiting to hear whether it is going ahead. Autumn on this coast is not short of wet days, and that wait arrives every year.

The lines

The running track is marked out in lime, which means rain erases it.

A few days after the event you can see the lines going faint. The ground where all those people sat and shouted the week before is back to being a schoolyard.

The white lines go with the next rain.

Fact-check note
  • ✅ Japanese primary and middle schools widely hold a whole-school outdoor sports day; sprints, relays, tug of war and beanbag-throwing are common events.
  • ✅ Family spectating is customary, and securing a viewing spot is practised in some places.
  • ✅ Shortening to half a day, and moving the event to spring, have both become more common, with heat given as a reason.
  • ✅ Scaling back or discontinuing human-pyramid displays on safety grounds has been widely reported.
  • ✅ Ground markings are made with lime and are washed away by rain.
  • ⚠️ Spot-claiming is not a nationwide uniform custom. The text states that some schools ban it and others ballot, and that it differs school by school locally.
  • ⚠️ Half-day scheduling, spring scheduling and the lunch arrangement all vary by school. The text says "more common" and "what I hear", and gives no proportions.
  • ⚠️ "Autumn on this coast is not short of wet days" is generally said, but varies considerably year to year.
  • ⚠️ Timing, events and running order differ by school; one school's format is not presented as the Japanese standard.
  • ⚠️ No school, district or personal name is given (prefecture level only), and no individual child is written about.
