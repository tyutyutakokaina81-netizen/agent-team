The Same Word, a Different Food

Baked sweet potato tastes different now than it did twenty years ago. The name has not changed; the thing largely has.

The older sort was floury. Split it and steam came out, and it broke apart in the mouth in a way that made you want something to drink. What gets roasted in front of shops today is dense and wet-looking at the cut, holding together rather than crumbling, clinging to the tongue like syrup.

The varieties turned over

The reason is not mysterious: what gets grown for sale has changed, from starchy types toward sweeter, moister ones, and that shift is generally put at roughly the past two decades.

Why it happened I can only guess at, though it seems tied to how they are sold — a floury potato earns its keep in cooking, while one eaten on its own as a sweet thing does better dense. Not everyone is pleased. People who grew up on the floury kind find the current ones too sweet, and since it is the shop-shelf mainstream that turned over rather than the number of varieties, "fewer options" is a fair way to feel about it.

A sweet with nothing added

The part that stops me every time I try to explain this abroad is that nothing goes in. No sugar, no butter, no cream. A potato, skin on, given enough time over heat, arrives at dessert.

The usual account is that slow, low heat converts starch into sugar inside it. The mechanics are past me, but I can confirm that a careless hour in a home oven does not produce the same thing, so there is a knack in there somewhere. From autumn into winter, dedicated roasting ovens appear outside shops, and what you buy is handed over wrapped in newspaper — hot enough to warm your hands while you carry it.

Skin and all

Most people here eat the skin. You can peel it, but the faint bitterness of the scorched outside against the sweetness inside is the point of leaving it on.

One is generally too much for a single person, which is why half of it tends to end up in somebody else's hand. If you buy one in a Japanese autumn, the dense kind is what you will find most easily now; tracking down the floury sort of twenty years ago means reading variety names on the label. That is what the shopping has become.
