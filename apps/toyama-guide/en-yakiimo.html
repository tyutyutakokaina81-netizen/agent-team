The Same Word, a Different Food

Baked sweet potato tastes different now than it did twenty years ago. The name has not changed; the thing largely has.

Winters in Toyama are long and cold, so the roasting season runs long too. The ovens are out near us from October into March, and the heat of one handed over on a cold evening is tied up with what winter feels like here.

The older sort was floury. Split it and steam came out, and it broke apart in the mouth in a way that made you want something to drink. What gets roasted in front of shops today is dense and wet-looking at the cut, holding together rather than crumbling, clinging to the tongue like syrup.

The varieties turned over

The explanation given is that what gets grown for sale has turned over, from starchy types toward sweeter, moister ones, and that shift is usually put at roughly the past two decades.

Why it happened I can only guess at, though it seems tied to how they are sold — a floury potato earns its keep in cooking, while one eaten on its own as a sweet thing does better dense. Not everyone is pleased. People who grew up on the floury kind find the current ones too sweet, and since it is the shop-shelf mainstream that turned over rather than the number of varieties, "fewer options" is a fair way to feel about it.

Buying one is now a choice

The variety name is printed on the label these days, and printed means choosable. Dense types are eaten as they are, sometimes sold on the idea that they are good chilled. Floury ones go better with a drink, and hold their shape if they are heading into tempura or a stew.

So buying a baked sweet potato has become a small act of selection — not only price and size but the name. I cannot keep the names straight, so I tend to ask the person at the oven which one is sweeter.

The selling has changed too. From autumn into winter a fixed oven appears outside a shop, or a kei truck with the oven in the back parks at a corner; what you buy is handed over wrapped in newspaper, hot enough to warm your hands while you carry it. Slow, low heat turning starch into sugar is the account you usually hear. The mechanics are past me, and all I can say is that an oven at home does not produce the same thing, so there seems to be a knack in it somewhere.

Skin and all

Most people here eat the skin. You can peel it, but the faint bitterness of the scorched outside against the sweetness inside is the point of leaving it on.

One is generally too much for a single person. Half of it tends to end up in somebody else's hand.

Buy one in Japan today and the dense kind is what you will find most easily; tracking down the floury sort of twenty years ago means reading variety names. The same food, and the shopping around it changed too.
