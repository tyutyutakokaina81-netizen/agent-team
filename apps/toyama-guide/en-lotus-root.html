A Vegetable With Holes, Eaten for Luck

Slice a lotus root crossways and you get a ring of holes you can see straight through. That, in Japan, is the reason it turns up at New Year: you can see through it, so it stands for seeing ahead — a clear view of the year to come.

The logic is a leap, and it held anyway

Put plainly, there is nothing connecting the shape to the meaning except the sentence itself. No mechanism, no evidence. And yet it stuck.

Japanese food symbolism does this a lot. Kelp, konbu, gets eaten at celebrations because the word sounds like yorokobu, to rejoice. It is wordplay that hardened into custom.

I have never had the origin explained and come away persuaded. I would still notice if the New Year simmered dish had none in it.

Autumn into winter

Lotus root is harvested from autumn through winter. What you eat is a stem that grows in mud, not a root — something I have been told rather than watched being dug.

Shops sell it two ways: still muddy, or washed white. The muddy ones are said to keep longer. I have never actually tested that.

Three textures from one vegetable

Sliced thin and fried fast, it is crisp and snapping — the sound is most of the point.

Cut thick and simmered a long time, it goes soft and faintly floury, almost like a potato. It is hard to believe it is the same thing.

Grated and formed into dumplings it becomes a third thing again, chewy and dense. Getting three genuinely different textures out of one vegetable is unusual, as far as I can tell.

In Toyama

The muddy ones show up in shops at the end of the year, and the New Year simmered dish nearly always has some.

Right now, in autumn, it is just a vegetable with holes in it — fried with soy sauce and chilli, eaten on a weekday. The luck part only comes up in winter.

Fact-check note
  • ✅ Lotus root is sliced to reveal a ring of holes and is treated as an auspicious food for that reason.
  • ✅ It is a standard component of Japanese New Year dishes.
  • ✅ It is harvested from autumn into winter.
  • ✅ The edible part is a rhizome — a stem, not a root.
  • ⚠️ "Muddy ones keep longer" is commonly said; the writer has not tested it.
  • ⚠️ Texture differences are well known, but the degree varies with variety, part and cooking method.
  • ⚠️ "Three textures is unusual" is the writer's impression, not a comparative study.
  • ⚠️ No origin story is asserted — the text says plainly that the explanation has never convinced the writer. Accounts differ.
  • ⚠️ "Nearly always in the New Year dish" describes the writer's surroundings; households and regions differ.
