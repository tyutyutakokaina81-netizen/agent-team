The Smell Comes First, Then Somebody Picks It Up

From late September into October, walking a Japanese street can bring you up against a smell that is genuinely unpleasant. It is ginkgo — the fruit dropping off the trees, getting stepped on, bursting. What surprises visitors is what happens next: here, people gather it and eat it.

When a street tree becomes food

Ginkgo is a common street tree in Japan, including in Toyama, and the yellow avenues are a fixture of autumn. Some of those trees fruit — small, orange-ish, wrapped in soft flesh. The fallen ones smell strong enough that most people walk around them. A few arrive with a bag and gloves and crouch under the tree. What gets eaten is not the flesh but the seed inside: soak the fruit, wash the flesh away, and a hard white shell appears; crack that and there is a jade-green kernel. That is ginnan.

The smell and the taste are separate things

The preparation is a genuine nuisance. The smell transfers to your hands, and handling the raw flesh bare-skinned is said to irritate the skin, which is why gloves keep coming up whenever anyone describes doing this. People go through with it because roasted ginnan bears no resemblance to what you smelled: slightly bitter, faintly chewy, with the sweetness arriving late. Salted. Or set into a savoury egg custard, one or two pieces to a cup. Or served shell-on and toasted at an izakaya.

It is also a thing people say not to overdo, children especially. The reasoning is beyond me, but the caution is old and widely shared here, and the practical upshot is that ginnan is eaten a few at a time by design rather than by the handful.

Food you pick up before you buy it

What holds my attention is the order of those two verbs. Ginnan sits in shops, certainly. The same seed also lies on the pavement, under a tree nobody owns, in the middle of a city — and someone will crouch down and take it home.

Whether a given tree is one you may gather from varies by place, and some parks and roadsides ask people not to, so it is worth checking rather than assuming. Still, that early-autumn sight of people walking with their eyes down has become part of the season. Come across that smell here in late September and nothing has gone off. It is the sound of food hitting the ground, waiting for whoever comes for it.

Fact-check note
  • ✅ Ginkgo is widely planted as a street tree in Japan; some trees bear fruit in autumn.
  • ✅ The outer flesh is strongly malodorous; what is eaten is the seed inside, not the flesh.
  • ✅ Contact with the raw flesh is said to irritate skin, and gloves are commonly recommended.
  • ✅ Roasting, toasting, and use in chawanmushi (savoury egg custard) are common preparations.
  • ⚠️ "Don't eat too many, especially children" is given as long-standing common caution — no medical mechanism or quantity is claimed here.
  • ⚠️ Taste and texture descriptions are personal and vary with variety and cooking.
  • ⚠️ Whether gathering is permitted depends on the location and whoever manages it; the article says to check rather than recommending it.
  • ⚠️ Why the flesh smells as it does is left as unknown rather than filled in with folklore.
