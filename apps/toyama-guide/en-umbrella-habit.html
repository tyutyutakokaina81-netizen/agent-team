Forget Your Lunch, Not Your Umbrella

Right now I will not decide in the morning whether to hang the washing outside. Clear sky or not, the decision waits until midday, and even then it sometimes goes wrong.

The saying

The Hokuriku coast has a line for this: bentō wasurete mo kasa wasureru na — forget your lunch if you must, but do not forget your umbrella. It belongs to the region as a whole, not to Toyama alone; people say it in Ishikawa and Fukui too.

It is usually told about winter, when the weather here turns over quickly and a bright morning means nothing by afternoon. But the saying is not only a winter one. From the second half of September into October there is a run of wet days that behaves the same way.

People explain it with a term for the autumn rain front. I do not understand the mechanism, and I am not going to pretend otherwise. What I notice, at ground level, is that the morning sky stops being reliable for a few weeks.

Why the umbrellas multiply

Several stand in the hall at home. One lives at my workplace. One lived at school, for years.

The logic is simple enough: being caught out is worse than owning too many, so you scatter them across the places you might be. The consequence is that you lose count, decide you have none with you, and buy another. That is most likely the whole reason the cheap transparent ones accumulate — they are sold everywhere, for very little, at exactly the moment you have concluded you have no umbrella.

In the hall they have stopped belonging to anyone in particular. Which one is whose has gone vague, and the one nearest the door is the one that leaves with you.

Carrying one you don't open

Plenty of days I take an umbrella out and never open it. Possibly most days. I have not kept a record, which is the honest position.

The extra thing in your hand is a nuisance, and every time there is a small argument for leaving it. I take it anyway. A day spent soaked stays with you much longer than a day spent carrying something you did not need.

That, I think, is what the proverb actually encodes — not the weather, but the lopsidedness of the memory. Skip lunch and you have missed a meal by evening. Forget the umbrella once and you are still thinking about it years later.
