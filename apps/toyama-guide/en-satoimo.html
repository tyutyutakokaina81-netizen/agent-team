Dig One Up and the Whole Family Comes With It

Peeling taro makes my hands itch. I forget this every year and remember it every year.

The itch is usually explained by fine crystals in the corm. How badly it hits you seems to depend on the person, and some people are entirely unbothered. Vinegar is said to help; at our house we just put up with it.

It arrives still covered in soil

In autumn taro turns up unwashed. Washed ones exist, but unwashed is said to keep better, so that is mostly what is on sale.

In Toyama it arrives by the boxful — dug by a relative, over-bought at a farm stand — and ends up on newspaper in the corner of the kitchen for a while.

Parent and offspring

Taro grows as a cluster underground. A large parent corm sits in the middle, with smaller ones around it, and sometimes a third generation outside those. Dig, and the whole bunch comes up together.

I have been told this is why it counts as an auspicious food — descendants multiplying. It does not seem to be a settled matter, and I could not say how many people eating it at New Year have that in mind.

Shops sometimes sell the parent and the offspring separately. The small ones hold their shape better in a simmer; the big one gets cut up.

Simmered

Mostly it goes into dashi and soy sauce, simmered until the liquid reduces to a gloss, at which point it slides off your chopsticks.

It slides because the surface is slippery. In a soup that slipperiness is kept; for a simmered dish it is often parboiled off first. The same ingredient, treated in opposite directions depending on where it is going.

Some households here put it in itokoni. What goes into that varies by house, so again, no generalising.

Cooked, it is stickier than potato and less sweet than sweet potato, and floury it is not. Every word I reach for is the name of some other food. Late September, the soil-covered box turns up, and I think: ah, this again.

Fact-check note
  • ✅ Taro grows as a cluster with a parent corm and offspring corms (and sometimes a third tier), lifted together when dug.
  • ✅ It commonly reaches shops with soil still on it, which is said to keep it better.
  • ✅ Simmering in dashi and soy sauce is the standard treatment; the surface is slippery, kept in soups and often parboiled off for simmered dishes.
  • ✅ Peeling taro can cause itching, generally attributed to fine needle-shaped crystals in the corm.
  • ⚠️ How much the itch affects you varies between people — stated in the text. "Vinegar helps" is given as something heard, not as an established remedy.
  • ⚠️ The "auspicious food / descendants" reading is one account, and the text says it is not settled.
  • ⚠️ Whether taro goes into itokoni depends on the household; no claim is made about all homes in Toyama.
  • ⚠️ The texture comparisons are the writer's own sense of it and shift with variety and cooking method.
  • ⚠️ Timing moves with the year and the place, so the text says only "autumn" and "late September".
