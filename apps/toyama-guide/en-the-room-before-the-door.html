The Room Before the Door

I live in Takaoka, in Toyama prefecture, where it snows. To get into the house, you open two doors.

The outer one lets you into a small room. One or two steps across it there is a second door, and only then are you inside. The room has a proper name — fūjoshitsu, "wind-blocking room" — and because it was there when I was a child, I assumed every house had one. It took me a long time to learn that they do not.

When I try to explain it to people from elsewhere, the first thing they say is usually: so, a storage room? Things are stored in it. But nobody builds a storage room directly in front of their own front door.

What the two doors do is simple enough. With a single door, the moment you open it the outside and the inside become one space. On a winter morning that matters: the warm air leaves, the cold comes in, and the house cools a little every time somebody goes out. With two doors, the inner one is shut while the outer one is open and the outer one is shut before the inner one opens. The two are almost never open at once, so there is never a clear path for the air. Put like that it sounds obvious — but building a whole extra room in order to get something obvious is the local way of doing it.

The name points at wind, and I am sure wind is part of it. What I actually notice, living here, is the snow. You come home with snow on your shoulders, your hat, your bag; walk straight in and the entrance floor is soaked. In the outer room you can shut the door behind you, beat your coat, stamp your feet, and only then open the inner door. Which is why, all winter, that floor is almost always wet.

What is kept there tells you most of what you need to know about a household's winter. Boots, usually several pairs — adults', children's, and some whose owner is no longer clear. Umbrellas, also several, slowly accumulating until nobody can say whose is whose. Snow tools: a shovel, and the big push-type one, leaning untouched through the summer. A red plastic drum of kerosene, whose weight you can judge from the angle it is standing at. Then polystyrene boxes, flattened cardboard, a bicycle, an unused plant pot, a stack of newspapers.

In summer the room has no job at all. Worse than that: the walls are usually glass, so in direct sun it gets genuinely hot, and opening both doors does not help much because the air has nowhere to travel. Nobody takes it down for the summer, though. It is simply what the house is. When autumn comes and the boots come back out, you remember what it was for.

One use has been added in recent years. Deliveries get left there. Most households do not lock the outer door, so the driver opens it, sets the parcel down, closes it and goes. Nothing gets rained or snowed on; nothing is visible from the road; and it is not quite inside the house either. It was not built for that, and it turned out to suit it exactly.

Most households do not lock the outer door. The inner one is locked, which means the lock sits between the house proper and this room, leaving the room itself outside it. That can sound careless until you consider what is in there: boots, a shovel, a drum of kerosene. A neighbour dropping by comes this far in and no further, and the circulating noticeboard that goes from house to house is sometimes simply left here. Not inside, not outside — so nobody has to be invited in, and nobody minds being found standing there. Where the lock is turns out to be a measure of how close people are.

On a winter morning the difference in temperature is obvious, which it should be, since nothing heats the room; but you have just come out of a warm one, so it lands hard enough to make you say something. Frost forms on the inside of the glass, and rubbing it with a finger opens a small window onto the street. I was told off for writing on it as a child. Water left out there freezes: the saucer under a plant pot will have a thin sheet of ice by morning. Things that never happen indoors happen one door away.

It also gets dirty, and that is rather the point. Grit comes in on shoes and stays underfoot. Leaves get in through autumn somehow, with the doors shut. In winter the floor is permanently wet with meltwater and mud off boots, and since wiping it changes nothing for long, most households — certainly mine — give up on cleaning it properly until spring. The house stays clean because this room agrees not to be.

Plants that spent the summer outdoors come in when it turns cold, and there is never enough space indoors, so some end up here: warmer than outside, cooler than the house, which apparently suits certain plants. Apparently, because the person in my house who does this is not me, and I have never asked which plants those are. In spring they go back out. In households with a cat, I am told, the cat claims the room.

For a child it was a useful place. You peeled off wet gloves there after making a snowman and let your hands sit a while before going in, because going straight into a warm room makes your fingers hurt. The winter equipment lived there too — sledge, shovel, bucket — until spring, when it was cleared away and something else took its place. Looking back, having a buffer between inside and outside is also why coming home soaked never got anyone shouted at.

Not every house here has one. Some were added later, an aluminium-and-glass box fitted outside the original entrance — which is why, from the street, that part of the house often looks newer than the rest. Flats generally do not have them. New houses vary, and I suspect that has to do with changes in heating and insulation, though I have not asked anyone who builds them.

The written name turns up in municipal paperwork and property adverts. In speech, we do not really call it anything. It is neither outside nor inside, so it ends up being "the bit outside the front door" — a room with a name that nobody uses the name for. I have heard there are other words for it in other regions. If the word differs from place to place, perhaps the thing was invented separately in each of them; that is a guess, and I have not looked it up.

Once you know what it is, you see it from the street everywhere: walking through a residential part of Takaoka, a great many houses bulge slightly at the entrance, and that bulge is this room. Roof shapes, the direction snow is made to slide, where the door is put — all of it presumably connects. I cannot explain most of it, and I walk past it every day regardless.

A visitor from a part of the country that gets no snow once came in, stopped in the middle of the room, and looked confused; they had not expected a second door. When they asked what the room was for I said it was for knocking the snow off, which was not much of an answer, because it was not snowing that day. They will have to come back in winter.

Writing this down, I realised how little of it I know. When these rooms started, who first built one, how far across the country they reach — none of that. I pass through one several times a day all the same, taking off shoes, standing umbrellas up, collecting parcels, beating snow off a coat. People rarely know the reason for the things they do every day. Writing about this place, it is nearly always like that.
