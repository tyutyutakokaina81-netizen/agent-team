The Noisiest Day the Fields Have

The gap between the fields turning yellow and the fields being cut is shorter than you would expect. A few days of colour changing, then the machines arrive. There is not much of a waiting period.

The day of noise

On the day itself the paddies are the loudest they get all year.

A combine goes in — one machine that cuts, threshes and holds the grain. The low continuous noise runs from morning until it is evening.

It carries. With the windows shut you still know someone is cutting somewhere today. Voices carry too, because people working near a running machine end up shouting.

Birds circle overhead while it happens, drawn by the insects the cutting flushes out. A white heron will sometimes walk along directly behind the machine.

The smell changes too

Afterwards the field smells different: cut green plant and damp soil together.

If there is a drying facility nearby that adds another smell, which is hard to describe. Not quite toasty — closer to the dustiness of dry grain.

You rarely see hasagake now

There is an older method, hasagake, where the cut stalks are hung on frames to dry in the sun. It appears in photographs constantly.

I rarely see it around here now; almost everything goes through machine drying, which given the labour and the weather risk seems reasonable enough. When you do come across one it catches the eye — and the fact that it catches the eye as "the old way" is itself the point.

A cut field looks suddenly wide

What is left is rows of stubble. After a while shoots come back up out of the stumps, trying again; they do not get as far as grain.

The Toyama plain changes colour all at once at this time of year and is cut all at once too, so within about a month an expanse of yellow has become an expanse of brown stubble. It starts once September is underway and then, without your noticing, it is over.

Birds come down onto the cut fields. It looks like part of the tidying up.

Fact-check note
  • ✅ A combine harvester cuts, threshes and collects the grain in one pass and is standard for rice harvesting.
  • ✅ Birds — herons among them — gather around harvesting machinery to take insects disturbed by the cutting.
  • ✅ Hasagake, sun-drying cut stalks on frames, is a traditional method; machine drying is now the mainstream.
  • ✅ Shoots can regrow from the cut stumps; they do not normally reach harvest.
  • ✅ Toyama Prefecture is a rice-growing area, with harvesting across its plain.
  • ⚠️ Harvest timing shifts with variety, area and the year's weather, so the text says only "once September is underway" and names no date.
  • ⚠️ "You rarely see hasagake now" is what the writer can see locally, not a proportion. It is still practised in some areas.
  • ⚠️ The smell descriptions are the writer's own; drying facilities and methods vary.
  • ⚠️ "The loudest day of the year" is a subjective impression, not a measurement.
  • ⚠️ No district, facility or personal name is given (prefecture level only).
