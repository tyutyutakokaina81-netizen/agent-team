The Curry Outgrew the Noodle Shop

From today I am going to write about food I order in from around Japan. The first is a curry from Niigata.

Inside the bus centre in Niigata there is a standing noodle shop. You buy a ticket from a machine, eat on your feet, and get on your bus. There must be a shop like it in every town. The curry it sells became better known than the noodles.

When a place has a famous dish, it usually comes from an old restaurant. This one came from the plainest spot there is: somewhere for people waiting for a bus.

In photographs the first thing you notice is the colour. Most curry in Japan now is brown. This one is yellow — the colour of curry powder, of school lunches and canteens. It has stayed that colour. A shop that did not repaint itself to match the fashion.

For some years now it has been sold as a retort pouch, so you can eat it without going to Niigata. That is why I chose it to start with. Famous food comes in two kinds: the kind that only works where it is made, and the kind that survives being put in a box. Sashimi and fresh tempura are the first kind. Curry is probably the second.

I should be honest: I have not eaten it yet. I am ordering it. So today there is no description of the taste. People say it is hot, but passing on what I have heard as though I knew it is the one thing I try not to do here. When it arrives and I have eaten it, I will write about the taste then.

Food from further away mostly reaches me by post. So these will be accounts of eating something without going there — a different thing from what someone writes on the spot.

Before going further, the line I draw — this is not something I looked up, it is something I worked out.

What travels is food whose shape does not change with time. Simmered dishes travel: curry, oden, anything you can seal in its own liquid. Meat in a marinade travels. Noodles travel if they are not yet cooked. What does not travel is food where the gap between making and eating is the point. Tempura starts falling off the moment it leaves the oil. Sashimi changes from the cut surface inwards. Freshly boiled noodles go soft. For those, "just made" is part of the dish, and the box takes that away. Curry is the first kind — people even argue it is better the next day — which is why I started here.

The retort pouch is what opened the way for regional food to travel. It sits on a shelf at room temperature and comes back with hot water. No refrigeration, so postage is cheap and it keeps anywhere. What you give up is variation: in a shop the day's batch and the person cooking move the taste a little, and a pouch does not move at all. Whether that is good or bad I cannot say simply. No variation is why it can go a long way; no variation is also why it is not the same as eating it there.

Freezing keeps food closer to its original state. Fried things and dumplings usually come frozen, I assume because batter and wrappers fail if they sit in liquid. But freezing moves the work to you: microwave, hot water, or leave it out — the result depends on which you choose. A pouch only needs heating, which is easier.

The biggest difference between eating something where it is made and eating it from a box is that the place does not come with it. The bus centre curry is eaten standing up by people waiting for a bus: a ticket machine, a counter, a few minutes. That speed is probably part of the taste. What arrives in a box gets eaten sitting down in my own kitchen, with nobody beside me and no departure time to watch. Same food, different thing. You have to accept that.

I still think it is worth doing, because more people cannot go than can. You read about something famous, you think you would like to try it, and the trip turns out to be years away or never. The box still arrives. And if you do go later, having eaten it first probably changes what you look at — you notice the shape of the shop and the people queuing rather than the food.

Three rules for this series. I do not describe the taste of something I have not eaten; today is such a day, and the taste comes later. I do not print numbers I have not checked — when it started, what it costs, who makes it. And I write each thing as belonging to where it is from, without measuring it against where I live.

Japan has a lot of places where you eat standing up, and the station noodle shop is the commonest. No chairs — a counter, a ticket machine, and you eat what comes out where you stand. Ten minutes at most, then you put the bowl back and leave. It is not as uncomfortable as it sounds, because you know you will not be kept waiting: the time is predictable. When you have fifteen minutes before a train or a bus, this is the only kind of place you can go into. The format shapes the food as well. It has to be something you can put out immediately — boiled, or heated — and curry fits.

I wrote that most curry in Japan now is brown. But few people here have never seen the yellow kind. School lunches were that colour. Canteens were that colour. It is what you get from curry powder and flour cooked together, the old way. Home curry went brown, I think, once boxed roux became common — though that is my memory talking, not something I have checked. So a yellow curry makes a certain number of people feel they have seen it before. Part of why this one is known may be the colour carrying that time with it, not only the taste.

There is a moment when something eaten locally for years starts being called famous. Usually an outsider finds it. To the people here it is just the shop they use, so it never occurs to them to recommend it. Then someone writes about it, people start coming, and one day there is a queue. I imagine that is what happened here: a place for people waiting for a bus turned into a place people travel to. What I like is that it did not change where it sells from — still standing room, still the ticket machine, still the same spot. They did not rebuild it for visitors.

Before it arrives, I am deciding what to look at. The colour: whether the yellow in the photographs is the yellow on the plate. The heat: people say it is hot, and I want to know what kind — spice heat and chilli heat are different things. And the thickness: something served in a standing shop has to be eaten quickly, so it is probably thickened for that, and I want to know whether the box version matches. If I do not decide in advance, all I will be able to write afterwards is that it was good, and that is not an article.
