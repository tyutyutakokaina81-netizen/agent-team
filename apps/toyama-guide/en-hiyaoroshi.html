A Step in Which Nothing Is Done

One stage in Japanese sake production consists of letting the summer pass.

Sake brewed in winter is not shipped. It sits in the brewery through the hot months, and what comes out in autumn is called hiyaoroshi.

Holding still counts as work

Since it is only held at a controlled temperature, the step looks like nothing is being done. The taste is nevertheless said to change — the edges come off, people say; it rounds out.

I would not back myself to identify that difference. I have never tasted the two side by side. I buy a bottle labelled that way in autumn, find it good, and stop there.

The names, and how loose they are

Because the final pasteurisation before shipping is skipped, it is also called namazume. The name hiyaoroshi is explained as shipping it out cold.

The definitions vary between breweries, and the related term akiagari is not used consistently either. I have not managed to straighten this out in my own head, and I would not trust a tidy explanation of it.

When timing becomes the product

What interests me is that the timing, not the contents, is what gets named.

The same brewery, the same rice — and the label changes with the month it leaves the building. Buyers pick it up because it is that season. It sits on the shelf less as a product than as part of the calendar.

New-crop rice and new-season buckwheat work the same way. In Japanese food a great deal of the value is carried by the information that this is what is available right now.

In Toyama

A corner of the drinks aisle fills with these bottles each autumn, local breweries among them.

People here talk a lot about the rice and the water. With this, honestly, what moves me is not the origin but the season. It is on the autumn shelf, so I pick it up — and there is not much more reason than that.

Fact-check note
  • ✅ Sake brewed in winter, stored over the summer and shipped in autumn is called hiyaoroshi.
  • ✅ Skipping the pre-shipping pasteurisation gives the related name namazume.
  • ⚠️ Definitions vary by brewery, and akiagari is not used consistently. The text says so rather than offering a tidy rule; the origin of the name is given as something the writer has read.
  • ⚠️ "The edges come off" is a commonly used description. The text states plainly that the writer has not compared them side by side.
  • ⚠️ No brewery or brand is named, deliberately.
  • ⚠️ "Timing carries the value in Japanese food" is the writer's view, not research.
  • ⚠️ This page describes a product category. It says nothing about how to drink, and nothing about health. Drinking age in Japan is 20.
