They Summer in the Mountains and Come Down in Autumn

The red dragonflies around the house right now were not here over the summer.

Numbers jump in autumn. It was only as an adult that I learned they had not multiplied — they had come back.

The hot months are spent somewhere cooler

Some of the dragonflies called akatombo emerge in early summer from lowland rice paddies, move up to cooler high ground for the hottest part of the year, and descend again once the temperature drops. Down here, they lay eggs around the paddies.

In Toyama that high ground is right next door. A wall of near-three-thousand-metre mountains runs along the eastern edge of the prefecture, in view from the flat every day. Something that spent the summer up there is what comes down in autumn.

Akatombo is not one species, though. It works as a collective name for dragonflies that turn red, their habits differ between them, and not all of them make this trip.

Nor does every individual of a given species go red. Males colour more deeply; females often stop at yellow or orange. A pale one in the swarm is not necessarily a different species.

Low flying in the evening

On autumn evenings they fly low, and on a good day the light picks them out as red points hanging in the air.

They land on the ends of washing-line poles and on clothes pegs — something about a thin projecting tip suits them — and will let you get fairly close before leaving. As a child I would edge a finger towards one hoping it would settle, and mostly failed.

Dragonflies have long been treated here as a marker of the season. From where I stand it is less a symbol than an insect that came down because it got cooler.

"There are fewer now"

People say there are fewer than there used to be, and I half feel that myself.

My memory is not evidence, though. Children watch insects more closely and spend longer outdoors. Pesticides and changes in paddy management get mentioned as causes; I have no basis for judging between them.

What I can say is that they are still flying in autumn. I have never counted.

A creature about four centimetres long, commuting between the mountains and the flat by season, returning to the paddy it hatched in after a summer away. Think about that distance, and the one resting on the end of a pole looks different.

Fact-check note
  • ✅ Some dragonflies known as akatombo (such as Sympetrum frequens) emerge from lowland paddies in early summer, move to cooler high elevations for the summer, and return to the lowlands in autumn to breed.
  • ✅ "Akatombo" is a collective name for reddening dragonflies rather than a single species.
  • ✅ In Sympetrum frequens, males develop a deeper red while females commonly remain yellow to orange.
  • ✅ Mountains approaching three thousand metres run along the eastern side of Toyama Prefecture.
  • ✅ Dragonflies have long featured in Japan as a seasonal marker.
  • ⚠️ Not every "akatombo" makes this seasonal migration — stated in the text, since life histories differ by species.
  • ⚠️ "There are fewer now" is given as something people say plus the writer's impression, with the possible bias of childhood memory noted in the text. Causes are listed as things that get mentioned, not concluded.
  • ⚠️ "About four centimetres" is approximate and varies by species and individual.
  • ⚠️ Elevation, distance and timing of the movement shift with the year, the place and the temperature, so no figures or dates are given.
  • ⚠️ Getting one to settle on a finger is a childhood recollection, not a reliable outcome.
  • ⚠️ No district, mountain, facility or personal name is given (prefecture level; "the eastern side" at most).
  • ⚠️ The header image shows a female, which reads orange rather than red — consistent with the text above, but noted here for anyone expecting a bright red insect.
