A local's summer in Toyama

Most guides show you Toyama's headline sights — the Alps rising from the sea, the snow-corridor Alpine Route. This is the other half: what a summer here actually tastes and feels like, from someone who grew up in Takaoka. It's quieter and more everyday than a festival brochure, and — if you time a visit for July or August — it might be the more memorable side. Wedged between one of Japan's richest bays and a wall of 3,000-metre mountains, this is a corner of Japan most visitors pass over on the way to Kanazawa.

The bay, at its most generous

Summer here is really a story about water. Toyama Bay is deep and cold, and in the warm months the fish counters fill up with things that would cost a fortune in Tokyo. Locals wait for white shrimp (shiroebi) — tiny, sweet, almost translucent — and, if you know where to look, summer rock oysters (iwagaki), which are said to be at their best in the heat, plump and briny, eaten raw with a squeeze of citrus. (Raw shellfish carries a food-safety risk; buy from a trusted source labelled for raw eating, or have them grilled.) Even a modest place will serve sashimi so fresh it barely needs soy sauce. If you eat one thing here, make it the sea — see the Toyama Bay sushi guide.

Beating the heat, the old way

Toyama summers are humid, and the ways people cool down are half the charm. In the late afternoon you'll still see someone flick water across the pavement outside their door — uchimizu, a splash of water that cools the ground as it evaporates. After sundown comes yūsuzumi: stepping outside to sit in whatever breeze the evening offers, when the tar of the day finally lets go. Reed screens (sudare) hang over windows, a wind chime ticks in the doorway, and a paper fan does the work no button can. None of it is efficient. That's the point — it's a slower relationship with the heat than air-conditioning allows.

Obon: the quiet heart of the season

If there's one thing that surprises visitors, it's how gentle mid-August feels. Around Obon, families come home — the roads clog, grandparents' houses fill up, graves are visited and cleaned. In the evenings, neighbourhoods gather for bon-odori, a slow circle dance around a drum tower that anyone can join (badly, in my case). On the water, small paper lanterns are floated to see the spirits off — tōrō-nagashi — a hush that's hard to forget. It isn't a spectacle staged for tourists; it's a living custom, and details vary by area. If you can, this is the week to come.

Small pleasures of a Toyama summer

  • Flowing somen (nagashi-somen) — cold noodles sent sliding down a split-bamboo chute; you catch them with chopsticks. More a game than a meal, and pure summer.
  • Shaved ice (kakigori) — a mountain of ice and syrup that turns your tongue blue and buys ten minutes of relief.
  • Fireworks (hanabi) — the walk home afterward, tired feet in wooden geta and the last boom at your back, is somehow the part you remember.
  • The sea as a playground — with the Alps on the horizon, the coast around Himi and the bay is where summer actually happens.

When to come, and how

Peak summer is July and August — hot and humid, but this is when the bay, Obon and the small rituals above are all in season. See the best time to visit Toyama, and note that you can reach it in about 15 minutes by shinkansen from Kanazawa: a day trip from Kanazawa is easy, though two or three days lets you pair a sea day with a mountain day.
