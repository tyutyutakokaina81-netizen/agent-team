The Mountain Turns White While the Town Is Still in Short Sleeves

September in Toyama is still hot — short sleeves in the daytime, and evenings that do not quite cool off. Yet this is the month when people start glancing east more often, because the tops of the mountains are about to go white.

A word for the first snow

Japan has a term for it: hatsu-kansetsu, the first day of the year on which snow is confirmed lying on a summit. It is not measured by climbing. A meteorological office observes the peak from a fixed point below and announces what it sees — which means the judgement is made from the town, looking up. In Toyama the peaks in question are the Tateyama range, a wall of three-thousand-metre mountains along the eastern edge of the prefecture, visible straight from the flatland on a clear day. People often notice it on the commute before it reaches the news. The date moves around considerably from year to year and cannot be pinned down in advance, but once September starts you begin to hear "it's about time."

From zero metres to three thousand

The unusual part is how compressed the view is. Toyama Bay is deep, so standing on the shoreline puts you at essentially zero elevation; look east and the three-thousand-metre peaks begin not far inland, with little high ground in between. Sea and snow can occupy the same field of view. Living here it is genuinely hard to judge how rare that is — though watching visitors stop on the beach and turn their cameras inland suggests it may not be ordinary. Autumn sharpens it further. The range that hazed out all summer seems to move closer from late September, even though only the air has changed.

Taking the season from the mountains

When people here talk about the weather, the mountains tend to come into it: whether cloud is sitting on them, whether the top has gone white. The range reports the season before the calendar does. Once the first snow shows, the lowland stays warm for a while and then moves fast — roughly a month to cold mornings, and after that the conversation turns to preparing for winter. So it reads less as winter is here than as autumn starts moving now.

What I would pass on to a visitor is that the season here is legible as colour rather than temperature. If you come between September and October, stand at the shore and face east on a clear day. On some days the colour at the top has changed while everything at your feet is still summer.

Fact-check note
  • ✅ Hatsu-kansetsu is the meteorological term for the first confirmed snow cover on a summit in a given year, determined by visual observation from a designated point.
  • ✅ The Tateyama range includes peaks around 3,000 m and is visible from Toyama's lowland on clear days.
  • ✅ Toyama Bay is a deep bay, and the horizontal distance from shoreline to high mountains is short.
  • ⚠️ The date varies year to year; no specific date is claimed here.
  • ⚠️ "About a month later the mornings turn cold" is a felt rule of thumb, not an established meteorological relationship.
  • ⚠️ "Autumn air makes the mountains look closer" is given as a commonly noted tendency.
  • ⚠️ Whether seeing sea and snow together is genuinely rare is left open rather than asserted.
