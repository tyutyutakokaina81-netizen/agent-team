One Proverb, Two Opposite Explanations

There is a Japanese saying almost everyone here has heard: aki-nasu wa yome ni kuwasu na — don't feed autumn aubergines to the bride. I grew up hearing it. I have no memory of anyone explaining what it meant.

The unkind reading

In the first reading, autumn aubergines are simply too good to share. The mother-in-law keeps them from her son's wife because they are delicious, and the proverb becomes a small portrait of domestic spite.

This is the version most people reach for. It fits a whole genre of stories about the relationship between a wife and her husband's mother, which Japanese comedy and drama have worked over for a very long time.

The kind reading

The second reading points the other way. Aubergines are said to cool the body, so a considerate household spares her the chill. A related version says autumn fruit has fewer seeds, and treats that as a bad omen for having children — so the dish is withheld out of care rather than meanness.

The same sentence, then, works as cruelty and as consideration. I cannot tell you which came first. There is even a reading in which yome is not a bride at all, but yo-me, an old word said to refer to rats — keep the aubergines away from the vermin.

My guess is that meanings get added afterwards. Whoever first said it, and whatever they meant, is long out of reach. What survives is a sentence flexible enough to carry whichever explanation a household prefers.

What actually changes in the vegetable

Set the proverb aside, and the aubergine itself really is different in autumn.

The skin goes firmer. The flesh tightens and holds less water. Put it over heat and there is a sweetness that the summer ones do not have — summer aubergines are watery and light, and the autumn ones have more density to them once cooked.

Whether the seeds are genuinely fewer, I have never checked. Every year I think I will count, and every year the season finishes before I do.

In Toyama

Once the mornings and evenings turn cold, the ones coming out of the fields are smaller. What appears at the roadside produce stands is a size down from the height of summer.

People who keep a vegetable patch start saying it is nearly finished while still handing away more than they can use. I end up with aubergines a few times each autumn this way, and each time they get grilled or simmered.

So the proverb stays unresolved and the vegetable does not. One of them ends properly every year.

Fact-check note
  • ✅ Aki-nasu wa yome ni kuwasu na is a widely known Japanese proverb.
  • ✅ Multiple interpretations coexist, including the spite reading and the considerate reading.
  • ⚠️ There is no settled answer as to which is original. This page does not pick one.
  • ⚠️ The reading of yome as an old word for rats is one of the circulating interpretations, not an established fact.
  • ⚠️ "Aubergines cool the body" belongs to traditional and folk thinking about food. Nothing here is medical advice.
  • ⚠️ "Autumn aubergines have fewer seeds" is commonly said; the writer has not counted.
  • ✅ Autumn aubergines are generally described as firmer-skinned and denser than summer ones, which matches the writer's experience. Variety and growing conditions differ.
  • ⚠️ The observation about smaller fruit as the mornings cool is personal, not statistical.
