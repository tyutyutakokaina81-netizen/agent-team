Buying the Whole Roe Sac in Autumn and Curing It at Home

It takes about an hour to separate the eggs, one at a time.

Come autumn, fishmongers and supermarkets here put out nama-sujiko — an intact salmon roe sac, unprocessed. The job at home is to break it down and cure it in soy sauce.

Off the membrane

The eggs are held together by a thin membrane, and getting them off it is the work.

Warm water loosens it. Some people use salt water, some rub with their fingers, some use a whisk. I use warm water, which is not the same as saying that is the correct method — it is the kind of task where you settle into whatever your hands remember after a few attempts.

The water clouds white and the eggs themselves go briefly pale before coming back. First-timers apparently assume at that point that they have ruined it.

The cure

Once separated, they go into soy sauce, sake and mirin, in proportions that also vary by household. After a few hours they are edible.

Fresh from the cure the skins are soft and the eggs burst readily. Left a day, the flavour deepens and they firm up. Which is better is a matter of preference.

What it costs

It is not cheap. The price swings a lot year to year, and in expensive years you hesitate. You hear about poor salmon catches, though I do not really understand what sets the price.

Still, I buy it once each autumn. The tedium of the separating work is probably what makes the final quantity feel justified — and it is likely cheaper than buying the same amount already cured.

In Toyama

This is more of a yellowtail place than a salmon one. Come winter the conversation is all yellowtail.

But the roe sacs appear at the fishmonger every autumn without fail. It does not feel like salmon country, and yet there they are, the same weeks each year.

It is a job I do exactly once a year, every year — forgetting a little of the method each time and remembering it again as I go.
