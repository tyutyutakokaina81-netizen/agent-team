## 本文
```
平日の午前十時に、庭にしゃがんで草をむしっている。会社にいたころなら、ちょうど打ち合わせが始まっていた時間だ。急かす人はいない。指の先が土で黒くなっていくのを、ぼんやり見ている。

自由なはずなのに、ときどき、妙に落ち着かなくなる。草を引く手が止まって、こう思う。——で、これから何をして食べていくのか。

■ 書くことは、続けてきた

会社を辞める前から、毎日のように文章を書いてきた。この土地の食べ物のこと、町のこと、季節がうつっていくこと。海の向こうの人にも読んでもらえたらと、英語も添えるようにした。

正直に書いておくと、ほとんど読まれていない。数字というのは冷たいくらい正直で、こちらの気持ちを少しも忖度してくれない。読まれない文章は、書かなかったのとそう変わらない——そう言われたら、返す言葉がない。

それでも書くのは楽しかった、と言えば聞こえはいいのだけれど、白状すると、書いているあいだは「これからどうするのか」を考えずに済んだ、というのが大きい。手を動かしていると、悩んでいないような気になれる。ずるい話だ。

[写真①]

■ 自分の写真を、見返してみた

どこで間違えたのだろうと思って、この何年かに自分が撮った写真を、順に見返してみた。

まだ暗いうちに港を出た日の、夜明けの海。船べり。上がってきたばかりの銀色の魚。その日のうちに切った刺身。七輪におこした炭の火。あぶられて照りの出た団子。スパイスから煮出した黒い飲みもの。夏の畑。昆布の上に並べた肉。

見ているうちに、ひとつ気がついた。どの写真にも、自分の手が写っている。

文章は、いまや誰にでも書ける。機械にだって書ける。けれども、あの朝、眠い目をこすって海に出ていたのは私だし、しゃがんで炭に火をつけたのも、この手だ。そこだけは、代わりがいない。

■ 「今、何してるの」と聞かれる

会社を辞めてから、顔を合わせた人にいちばんよく聞かれるのが、これだ。「今、何してるの」。

そのたびに、少し詰まる。「まあ、ちょっと書きものを」と濁して、あいまいに笑ってごまかす。相手も深くは聞かないでいてくれるので、話はそこで終わる。終わるのだけれど、家に帰ってからも、その一言がしばらく耳の奥に残っている。

肩書きが無くなるというのは、こういうことなのだと思う。以前なら、勤め先の名前を言えば、それで説明が済んでいた。相手も分かった顔をしてくれた。今は、自分を説明する言葉を、自分で用意しなければならない。

そして気づくのは、答えられなくて気まずいというより、その答えを、私自身がまだ持っていないということのほうだ。

■ 人が来る側に、立ってみる

前に市内の公園で、外国から来たらしい人たちが、像の前に列を作って順番を待っているのを見たことがある。順番が来ると、みんな似たようなポーズで写真を撮って、うれしそうに戻っていく。それを何組も、飽きずに眺めていた。

あの人たちが海を渡ってまで欲しかったのは、たぶん、情報ではない。その場所に自分が立っている時間のほうだ。調べれば分かることを知りに来たのではなくて、そこにいる、という体験を買いに来ている。

だとすれば、私にできるのは、案内をすること、火をおこすこと、この土地の人間として、しばらく隣にいること。それくらいかもしれない。派手ではないし、大きく儲かる話でもなさそうだ。ただ、そこには私の手が要る。

■ まだ、何も決まっていない

もっとも、これは今のところ、ただの思いつきでしかない。うまくいく保証はどこにもないし、いざ始めるとなれば、必要な届け出や決まりを一つずつ確かめるところからだろう。始める前から分かった顔をするのは、いちばんかっこ悪い。

やってみて、だめならやめて、また考える。会社を辞めていちばん贅沢だと感じるのは、その「やめて、また考える」ができることだ。以前の私には、それを試す時間がなかった。

決まらないまま、また草をむしる。土の匂いがする。答えはまだ出ていないけれど、どのあたりを探せばいいのかの見当だけは、少しついてきた気がしている。

（※本文は「私の場合」の個人的な考えと途中経過です。働き方・早期退職・独立をすすめるものではありません。実際に何らかの仕事を始める場合、業種によって必要な許可・届け出・資格が異なるとされるため、各自で最新の決まりをご確認ください。会社名・職種・年齢・資産額・金額などの特定の情報は書いていません。）

――――――――――
【English】At ten in the morning on a weekday, I'm crouched in the garden pulling weeds. Back when I was still at the company, a meeting would have been starting right about now. Nobody is rushing me. I watch my fingertips turn black with soil. I'm supposed to be free, and yet now and then a strange restlessness comes over me. My hand stops, and the thought arrives: so — what am I going to do for a living from here?

I kept writing, even before I left. Every day, more or less: the food of this place, the town, the seasons turning. I began adding English, hoping someone across the sea might read it. Honestly, almost no one does. Numbers are coldly honest; they show no consideration at all for how you feel. If someone told me that writing nobody reads is not so different from not writing at all, I'd have nothing to say back. It would sound better to say I kept writing because it was fun — but the truth is that while I was writing, I didn't have to think about what comes next. Moving your hands lets you feel as though you aren't worrying. A convenient trick.

Wondering where I'd gone wrong, I went back through the photographs I'd taken over the past few years. The sea at dawn on a day I left port while it was still dark. The gunwale. A silver fish just hauled up. Sashimi cut the same day. Charcoal lit in a small clay grill. Dumplings glazed and glistening over it. A dark drink simmered from whole spices. A summer field. Meat laid out on kelp. Looking at them, I noticed one thing: in every single photograph, my own hands appear. Anyone can write now — a machine can write. But it was me who rubbed the sleep from my eyes and went out on the water that morning, and it was these hands that crouched down and lit the charcoal. There, at least, no one can stand in for me.

Since leaving, the question people ask me most when we meet is this: "So what are you doing now?" Each time, I falter a little. "Oh, a bit of writing," I mumble, laughing vaguely to move things along. They kindly don't press, and the conversation ends there — except that the question stays in my ears for a while after I get home. This, I think, is what it means to lose a job title. Before, saying the name of my employer settled the matter, and the other person would nod as though they understood. Now I have to prepare the words that explain me, myself. And what I notice is not so much the awkwardness of having no answer, as the fact that I don't yet hold that answer at all.

Once, in a park in town, I watched people who seemed to have come from abroad lining up, waiting their turn in front of a statue. When their turn came they took photographs in much the same pose, then walked back looking delighted; I watched group after group without tiring of it. What they crossed an ocean for wasn't information. It was the time of standing in that place themselves — not to learn something they could have looked up, but to buy the experience of being there. If that's so, then what I can offer may be small: to show people around, to light a fire, to sit beside them for a while as someone who belongs to this place. It isn't glamorous, and it doesn't sound like it would make much money. But it does require my hands.

For now, though, this is only a thought. There's no guarantee it will work, and if I actually begin, it will start with checking the permits and rules one by one. Acting as if I already understand, before I've begun, would be the least becoming thing of all. Try it, stop if it fails, think again. The greatest luxury of having left the company is being able to stop and think again — before, I had no time to test anything. Still undecided, I go back to the weeds. The soil smells of soil. I don't have an answer yet, but I think I've begun to see roughly where I ought to be looking.
```

## 英語要約
```
Weeding the garden at ten on a weekday — the hour a meeting used to start — I feel the unexpected restlessness underneath the freedom, and the question surfaces: what will I actually do for a living now? I have written nearly every day, before and after leaving: this region's food, its town, its turning seasons, with English added in the hope of readers across the sea. Almost nobody reads it. Numbers are coldly honest, and if you told me that unread writing differs little from writing nothing, I could not argue. Saying I wrote because it was fun would sound better than the truth: while writing, I didn't have to think about what came next. Looking for where I went wrong, I went back through my own photographs — the sea at dawn, a silver fish just landed, sashimi cut the same day, charcoal in a small grill, glazed dumplings, a drink simmered from whole spices, a summer field, meat laid on kelp — and noticed that my hands are in every one of them. Anyone can write now; a machine can write. But it was me on the water that morning, and these hands that lit the fire. The question I now get asked most is "So what are you doing these days?" — and each time I falter, mumble something about writing, and let it pass; losing a job title means having to supply the words that explain you, and what unsettles me is less the awkwardness than realising I don't hold that answer yet. Once I watched visitors from abroad queue happily before a statue in a park here, and understood they had not crossed an ocean for information but for the time of standing there themselves. So perhaps what I can offer is plain: to guide, to light a fire, to sit beside someone as a person of this place. It is only a thought so far, with no guarantee, and beginning would mean checking permits and rules one at a time. Try, stop if it fails, think again — that is the real luxury of having left. Undecided, I return to the weeds; the soil smells of soil. No answer yet, only a sense of where to look.
```
