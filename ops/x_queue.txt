=== tap-water ===
In much of Japan the tap water is drinkable — in Toyama it tastes good. Snow on the 3,000m Tateyama range melts, filters underground for years, and reaches your glass soft and clean. Same water shapes the rice, sake, tofu. Bring a bottle and refill. #Japan #Toyama

=== new-rice ===
Every September Japan gets "shinmai" — new-crop rice. Fresher grains, more moisture: plump, glossy, faintly sweet. Eat it plain first, then a pinch of salt. In Japan, even rice has a season — and the first bowl is the finale. #Japan #JapanFood #Toyama

=== nihyakutoka ===
Around Sept 1, Japan's old calendar marks nihyaku-tōka — the "210th day." Ripe rice + peak typhoon season = a year's work at risk in one night. Before forecasts, a named day was the weather alert. Weather-lore folded into a date. #Japan #Toyama

=== cosmos ===
Toyama is famous for spring tulips. Its quiet autumn cosmos (akizakura) may be the better view — no crowds, self-seeded along the paddies, rippling in the wind with the Tateyama range behind. The unknown autumn of a place known for spring. #Japan #Toyama

=== croquette-thread ===
1/ I found a Japanese city obsessed with croquettes. Not fancy croquettes. Not artisanal. Just golden, simple, deep-fried happiness. And it's only in Takaoka, Toyama. Here's why. 🧵
2/ After WWII, Takaoka's factories boomed. Thousands of workers needed cheap lunch. Enter: the croquette. Easy to fry in bulk, filling, ¥500-800. The culture stuck. 70 years later, small unmarked food halls still serve croquette *sets* as the main course.
3/ It's so hyperlocal that no guidebook mentions it. You have to find these 食堂 (shokudo) near the station or factory districts. Cash only. Lunch only (11am–2pm). Each place hand-makes the filling different.
4/ I ate at 3. One used beef, one used pork, one used shrimp. Each was better than the last. Locals *go for* the croquette, not "with" it. This has never happened to me in Japan.
5/ This is what hyperlocal food culture looks like: no branding, no marketing, no Instagram. Just accumulated craft passed down by daily repetition.
Takaoka proves you don't need fancy ingredients to make something memorable. You just need 70 years of practice. #JapanFood #Takaoka #ToyamaTravel

=== himi-thread ===
1/ This fishing port doesn't plan your meals. The ocean does. Welcome to Himi, Toyama—where your breakfast depends on what the boats caught 6 hours ago. 🧵
2/ At 5 AM, fishing boats return. Auctioneers call. Buyers grab the catch. By 6:30 AM, locals line up at market food halls. By 7 AM, you're eating yesterday's night catch, fresh and raw.
3/ But here's the catch (pun intended): **The menu changes every single day.** Weather cancels fishing. Water temperature shifts seasons. You don't know what you'll eat until you arrive.
4/ April–May: Firefly squid (translucent, sweet) June–August: White shrimp (melts on your tongue) Sept–Nov: Yellowtail (fatty, rich) Dec–March: Red snapper (usually expensive, here just a lunch)
5/ Most people visit "tourist" markets. Wrong. Go to actual fish shops where locals buy. That's where the realness is. A full sashimi breakfast: ¥1,200–¥1,800 (~$9-14).
6/ Himi doesn't romanticize itself. No Instagram cafes. No "artisanal" branding. Just a port town living off what the sea gives it. If you're tired of curated travel, this is it. #HimiToyama #JapanTravel #Seafood #OffTheBeatenPath

=== takaoka-thread ===
1/ Takaoka is a city where you can walk from the 1600s to 1970s in 30 minutes. And it doesn't try to be cute about it. It just... exists in all its eras at once. 🧵
2/ Most cities choose: either demolish old buildings and go modern, or restore them for Instagram. Takaoka does neither. Medieval streets? Lived in. Edo-era merchant houses? Still have residents and laundry hanging outside.
3/ Copper craftspeople still use 400-year-old hand techniques. A 16-meter Buddha statue (cast from Takaoka copper) doesn't sit in a shrine—it stands on a regular street where people walk by daily.
4/ 1970s food halls operate unchanged. An old trolley follows the original 1600s trade route to Kyoto. This is a time capsule that never stopped moving.
5/ No "cultural preservation board" stopped things. No "heritage tourism" budget. It's just a working city that accumulated history as it lived.
6/ Bonus: Takaoka is also famous for its hyperlocal croquette culture and postwar factory heritage. This place gets weirder the longer you look.
If you've found cities in Japan that don't try to be "cute"—just stubbornly authentic—share them. #Takaoka #JapanArchitecture #CastleTown #OffTheBeatenPath #ToyamaTravel

=== rice-fields-thread ===
1/ Right now in Toyama, the rice fields are turning from green to gold. It looks like the quietest month of the year. It's actually the most important one. 🌾🧵
2/ What looks idle is the plant's hardest work: flowering, pollinating, filling each grain with starch. This single month decides most of the year's yield and flavor.
3/ Farmers watch two things obsessively: • Water — flood to swell the grain, then drain to firm the ground for machines • Typhoons — late Aug–Sept storms flatten heavy, ripening rice. Cut early or wait it out?
4/ Walk the edge of a paddy now: a drier, faintly sweet smell, the rustle of hardening ears, dragonflies skimming low. Behind it, the 3,000m Tateyama range.
5/ It's not a tourist sight. But this green-to-gold plain is the backbone of the region — and knowing what's happening in the field changes how a bowl of rice looks. #Toyama #Japan #rice #ruraljapan

=== two-day-thread ===
1/ The perfect 2-day Toyama itinerary: a fishing port and a castle town, sea to 3,000m mountains. One route, the whole prefecture. 🧵🇯🇵
2/ DAY 1 — HIMI (the sea). Dawn fish auction. Breakfast from the morning's catch. Watch locals buy at the REAL market. Conveyor-belt sushi = whatever the sea gave today. Then drive 45 min south.
3/ DAY 2 — TAKAOKA (history). Edo-era streets still lived in. Copper craftspeople using 400-year-old techniques. A 16m Buddha standing on a street. And lunch: a croquette SET — the croquette is the MAIN dish here.
4/ Cost: ~¥54–88k/person ($370–600) incl. flight, car, 2 nights, meals. The only rule: surrender to the weather. Boats don't sail → you eat frozen. Rain → you read in a cafe. That's Toyama. #JapanTravel #Toyama #itinerary

=== august-thread ===
1/ August in Toyama runs differently from the rest of Japan. Two homecoming waves, festivals all month, and rice farmers who never stop. 🧵🎆
2/ Most of Japan: one fixed Obon (mid-Aug), everyone returns home. Toyama: TWO waves — mid-Aug Obon, then late-Aug when local festivals pull people back again.
3/ Meanwhile rice farmers don't rest. Peak-heat paddies need constant water management — dawn and dusk, every day, so the rice doesn't cook. A few days off for Obon, then back.
4/ Festivals spread across the WHOLE month — neighborhood Bon-Odori, fireworks in different towns on different weekends. Not one big day. A whole season. That's Toyama in August. #Japan #Toyama #Obon #matsuri

=== higanbana ===
Every mid-September, red spider lilies (higanbana) suddenly line Japan's rice-paddy edges — a red line along the gold fields. Its oddity: flower and leaves never appear together. Folklore: the toxic bulb was planted to keep moles from the fields. #Japan #Toyama #higanbana

=== tsukimi ===
1/ Japan has a festival that's only about looking at the moon: tsukimi, the mid-autumn harvest moon. No fireworks, no stalls. You set out pampas grass, offer round dumplings, and just watch. 🌕🎑
2/ The date follows the old lunar calendar (Sept–Oct), timed to when the air is clearest and the moon most beautiful. Its roots: gratitude for the rice harvest.
3/ Susuki grass stands in for rice ears (a wish for a good crop). Round dumplings echo the moon. Offer taro too and it's the "potato moon." (Meanings vary — none definitive.)
4/ In rice-country Toyama, a great moon rises over paddies just cut to brown stubble. In a life of notifications, a night meant only for watching the moon is its own luxury. #Japan #Toyama #tsukimi

=== rail-compact-thread ===
1/ Rural Japan keeps losing its local trains: fewer riders → service cuts → more people leave → lines abandoned. Toyama chose the opposite. 🚋
2/ In Takaoka, a street tram runs past people's front doors. A coastal line reaches a seaside town on Toyama Bay. Everyday transport, not a tourist ride.
3/ In Toyama City, a fading JR line was reborn as a modern low-floor LRT — north & south of the station joined so you can loop the centre. They bet on making rail EASIER, not smaller.
4/ It's the "compact city" idea: cluster homes & services along the rails so people who can't drive still get around. Not a simple success — but a town that held onto its trains. #Japan #Toyama #compactcity

=== pears-thread ===
1/ Japanese pears are nothing like European ones. Round like an apple, pale inside, and SO full of water. You eat them firm and chilled — they snap with a crunch, juice bursting out. 🍐
2/ That clean crisp bite comes from tiny grains in the flesh ("stone cells"). Not gritty — just crisp. No waiting for it to soften like a European pear.
3/ Toyama isn't the most famous pear region, but it has its own pear country. Roadside stands sell the day's fruit by the box — bigger, cheaper, juicier than a city shop.
4/ How to eat one: chill well, peel, cut into wedges, eat as is. Rarely cooked. On a warm September day, that cold dripping bite is the taste of summer ending in Toyama. #Japan #Toyama #nashi

=== dual-income-thread ===
1/ In Toyama, it's totally normal for both parents to work AND have kids. City visitors ask "how do you manage it?" Toyama has one of Japan's highest shares of dual-income households. 🏡
2/ The reason isn't willpower — it's the shape of "home." High home ownership, big houses, and 3 generations under one roof: a share that's high by national standards.
3/ While both parents work, grandparents are home — school runs, sudden fevers, the after-daycare gap. The "who watches the kids?" problem is solved inside the family.
4/ Less a policy than a long-inherited habit: a big house, three generations, grandparents nearby. Changing slowly, but "raising kids as a whole family" still runs deep. #Japan #Toyama

=== lion-dance-thread ===
1/ Between Sept and Oct, flutes and drums start up across Toyama. Autumn festival season = shishimai, the lion dance. 🦁🎶
2/ What surprises visitors isn't the dance — it's the number. One city holds many. The next hamlet has its own, and so does the one after that. Toyama has one of Japan's highest concentrations of troupes.
3/ It was never made for tourists, so neighbouring troupes dance differently. Some lions have long cloth bodies with several people inside, rippling like one creature. A tengu or a child squares off against it.
4/ And the lion comes to your door — down the lanes, dancing at each house. Performers are fewer each year, some troupes paused. Hear flutes on an autumn afternoon? Walk toward the sound. #Japan #Toyama #matsuri

=== kurigohan-thread ===
1/ Outside Japan, a chestnut becomes dessert: marrons glacés, Mont Blanc, roasted and sugared. In Japan the most common autumn treatment is different — the chestnuts go into the rice. 🌰🍚
2/ Kurigohan = rice + chestnuts + a little salt. That's basically the whole recipe. Some add glutinous rice or a splash of sake. No sugar goes in.
3/ Why no sugar? The explanation you hear: rice and chestnut are two different kinds of sweetness, and sugar would flatten the difference between them. Hard to prove — but one bite makes it plausible.
4/ The catch is the peeling: a hard outer shell AND a bitter inner skin, both by hand. An hour of peeling for ten minutes of eating. People still do it, probably because the hour is the season. #Japan #japanesefood

=== insect-voices-thread ===
1/ Something changes in Toyama nights once September arrives. Open a window and a sound starts up out of the dark — insects. Japanese has a phrase for it: mushi no koe. Literally, the insects' VOICE. 🦗
2/ Not chirping. Not noise. A voice. These autumn insects have long been treated as something to listen to — they appear in classical poetry and work as markers of the turning year.
3/ (You may hear that Japanese people process insect sound "like language". That's disputed and far from settled, so I'll leave it aside.)
4/ Japanese quiet doesn't always mean no sound. A night full of insect voices registers as quiet — a sound that's steady and asks nothing of you becomes part of the silence. #Japan #Toyama

=== hoshigaki-thread ===
1/ Walk through a Japanese autumn and you'll see rows of orange fruit hanging under the eaves. Dried persimmons. Here's the part people miss: that fruit couldn't be eaten when it was picked. 🍊
2/ Japanese persimmons come in two kinds — the sweet sort you bite straight off the tree, and the astringent sort that leaves your mouth rough and your tongue numb (tannins). It's the astringent kind that gets hung up.
3/ No sugar is used. You peel it, hang it, and wait. As it dries, the compounds behind the astringency are said to shift into a form the tongue stops registering, while the sugar concentrates. 2-3 weeks later: dense, confectionery-sweet.
4/ It fails, too. Hokuriku autumns are humid; hung without a draught it moulds — hence the eaves, dry overhead with air moving through. Nothing added but time and wind. #Japan #Toyama #japanesefood

=== first-snow-thread ===
1/ September in Toyama is still hot — short sleeves, warm evenings. And yet people keep glancing east, because the mountaintops are about to go white. 🏔️
2/ Japan has a word for it: hatsu-kansetsu, the first day snow is confirmed lying on a summit. Not measured by climbing — a meteorological office looks up at the peak from a fixed point below and announces what it sees.
3/ The bay drops off steeply near shore; the shoreline itself is essentially zero elevation. Look east and peaks approaching 3,000m start a short way inland, with little in between. Sea and snow can sit in the same view.
4/ It reads less as "winter is here" than "autumn starts moving now" — about a month to cold mornings after that. Come in Sept-Oct, stand at the shore, face east. Summer at your feet, winter at the top. #Japan #Toyama

=== ginnan-thread ===
1/ Late September in Japan, a street can hit you with a smell that is, to put it mildly, not a good one. It's ginkgo fruit — dropped, stepped on, burst. The surprising part is what happens next: people gather it and eat it. 🌳
2/ Not the flesh. The seed inside. Soak the fruit, wash the flesh off, and you get a hard white shell; crack it and a thin-skinned kernel comes out, which turns jade-green once cooked. That's ginnan.
3/ Never eaten raw — always roasted or toasted. The prep is a nuisance: the smell sticks to your hands, and handling the raw flesh bare-skinned is said to irritate the skin, hence the gloves. Roasted, it's nothing like the smell: faintly bitter, chewy, late sweetness.
4/ People also say don't overdo it, children especially — old, widely shared caution, so it's eaten a few at a time by design. What gets me: it sits in shops AND lies on the pavement. Food you pick up before you buy it. #Japan #Toyama

=== snow-melt-thread ===
1/ Winter talk starts early in Toyama. Snow is still some way off, and yet the roads are already being checked during the autumn. Not the snowploughs — the road surface itself. ❄️
2/ Walk a street in Hokuriku and you'll see small holes at regular intervals down the middle of the lane. Not manholes. In winter, water comes UP out of them, wets the asphalt, and melts snow as it lands.
3/ Mostly groundwater, I'm told. It sits near the same temperature all year, so in winter it arrives warmer than the air — warm enough to melt what lands. Not free, though: heavy pumping raises questions about the ground, and some areas have rules or shared wells.
4/ Clearing snow usually means moving it — push, truck, find somewhere to put it. This stops it existing on the road at all, which means laying pipework: into new roads, or into old ones by cutting the surface open. Autumn's inspection is the rehearsal. #Japan #Toyama

=== nameko-thread ===
1/ Autumn puts mushrooms in the miso soup here, and the days with nameko are different: the soup itself goes thick. That thickness is slime off the mushroom — and yes, that's the point of it. 🍄
2/ Slippery isn't much of a selling point in most countries, I gather — which is why this is the dish I stumble over explaining. Here it's the reason the mushroom is in the bowl, and most people give nameko a quick rinse at most; scrub it off and half the point goes.
3/ Japan has a whole category like this: natto, grated yam, okra, mekabu — all valued for exactly what would count against them elsewhere. Why? I genuinely don't know. But there seems to be an extra axis for texture: not just firm/soft, but slick and thick as their own good.
4/ It's awkward at first — they slide off your chopsticks, and thickened miso soup runs hotter than it looks. Upside: stock, mushrooms, tofu, miso. Five minutes. The chestnut rice takes an hour of peeling; this is the same season at the other end. #Japan #Toyama

=== evening-chime-thread ===
1/ Every late afternoon in Toyama, music arrives — not from a house, but from loudspeakers high above the town, aimed at all of it at once. First-timers brace for an alarm. It's only announcing the time. 🔊
2/ The hardware belongs to the municipality and exists for emergencies. The explanation you hear for the daily chime: it doubles as a test that the system still works when nothing is wrong.
3/ The tune varies by place, often a children's song. So does the hour — some towns shift it with the season, later in summer, earlier as autumn comes, tracking when it gets dark. In most places it reads as "time to head home", and you can watch a park empty out as it plays.
4/ What gets me is that it names nobody. Not "so-and-so, come home" — just music belonging to no one, so each person takes what applies. Not everyone's charmed; some find it noisy. But for that moment the whole town is on one clock. #Japan #Toyama

=== yakiimo-thread ===
1/ Baked sweet potato tastes different in Japan now than it did 20 years ago. Same name, largely a different food. 🍠
2/ The old sort was floury — split it, steam came out, it crumbled in your mouth and made you want a drink. What's roasted in front of shops today is dense and wet at the cut, clinging to the tongue like syrup.
3/ The explanation given: varieties grown for sale turned over, starchy → sweeter and moister, roughly over two decades. Not everyone's pleased — people raised on the floury kind find these too sweet, and it's the shop mainstream that changed, so "fewer options" is fair.
4/ Buying one is a small act of choosing now: the variety name is on the label, and you pick dense or floury. I can't keep the names straight, so I ask whoever's at the oven which is sweeter. Handed over in newspaper, hot enough to warm your hands. #Japan #Toyama

=== early-foliage-thread ===
1/ The Japanese foliage photos that travel are from Kyoto in November. On the flat that's about right. But in Toyama there are places where autumn colour starts in September — the high ones. 🍁
2/ Above 2,000m on the Tateyama range it's cold enough to run a different schedule. Rowan of some kind reddens first, then the grassland goes yellow to red-brown, with creeping pine holding green in between. One slope, all three.
3/ Peak timing moves around — late September some years, October in others, weather depending. No date guarantees it. And early colour is really early cold: strong September wind can call for winter kit, and when the weather turns the view just goes.
4/ Living here you get autumn twice: once on the mountain, once where you actually live, roughly six weeks apart. The calendar calls both autumn. The altitude disagrees. #Japan #Toyama
